Q.I5. What karmic consequence does a person who dies by suicide face?

This is one of this group's heaviest questions — and usually asked in very painful circumstances: a family just bereaved, relatives despairing. First it must be stated clearly: Buddhism offers no simple "suicide = surely hell" formula. But neither does that mean suicide is a lightly regarded act. In most ordinary cases, suicide joins suffering, despair, hatred, fear, confusion — unwholesome mind-states of very important karmic significance. Meanwhile, the Pāli Canon has three special cases — Channa, Vakkali, and Godhika — in which self-inflicted bodily ending is described by the Buddha in ways unfitting the "surely fallen" conclusion — for the discourses place them in liberation-level contexts at death. Hence the right question is not only "does suicide bring retribution?", but: which mind led to that act, what practice-level was that person at, and how do the discourses describe each case?

Call local emergency services, a psychological support hotline, or find a trustworthy person to stay beside at once. When immediate danger exists, keeping safe takes priority over any doctrinal question.

1. The short answer

Six firm points can be kept:

  • Suicide is no ordinary liberation-method — death does not automatically end craving and confusion.
  • Intention and mind-state matter greatly to karma — "Intention, I declare, is karma" (article G.C4).
  • No canonical "suicide = hell" rule for all cases.
  • Channa, Vakkali, and Godhika are special exceptions — joined with very high realization-levels, not a general rule.
  • Do not use exceptions to justify despairing suicide, nor judge the dead with a single verdict.
  • Those left behind can still do good deeds and dedicate for the departed (article Q.H3), and such dedicatory offerings still make wholesome karma for the living themselves (article Q.H4).

2. Is suicide killing?

In Buddhist moral analysis, killing (Pali: pāṇātipāta - killing living beings) is deliberately causing a living being's death — and suicide victims themselves are living beings. Hence deliberately taking one's own life is traditionally classed within very grave killing. In the Vinaya (Pali: Vinaya - monastic code), the first rule on human killing prohibits bhikkhus from personally causing death, finding proxies to kill, praising death's benefits, and inciting one toward death — violations belonging to pārājika level. [Source: Pāli Vinaya, Pārājika 3 (Pātimokkha) - Theravāda Buddhism]

This shows: Buddhism does not regard deliberately causing death — including one's own — as morally negligible. But two issues must be distinguished: the Vinaya's Saṅgha-rules are one matter; a suicide victim's karmic fruits another — and the second needs analyzing per karmic factors: intention, mind-state, action, and the whole causal stream.

One in despair usually carries minds: "I cannot bear anymore. I want to vanish." — i.e. hatred toward oneself, fear, confusion about direction, and craving (Pali: taṇhā - craving) toward ending present feelings. Conversely, one of very deep wisdom no longer clinging to body — those two mind-states cannot be lumped into one karma-kind (article Q.A3 already saw the principle: same outer result, different intentions mean different karma).

3. Three special cases in the Canon

3.1. Channa — the gravely ill bhikkhu "blameless"

In the Pāli Canon, venerable Channa was gravely ill, violently pained. Before ending himself, two elders helped him contemplate the six senses — eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, mind — not viewing them as "mine, me, my self"; Channa answered he no longer clung so. After he used a knife to end himself, elders asked the Buddha where Channa was reborn; the Buddha answered Channa died "blamelessly," and the discourses say blameworthy is one abandoning this body to seek another body — while Channa was not so. [Source: MN 144 (Cūḷachunda Sutta); parallel at SN 35.87 - Theravāda Buddhism]

Many commentarial traditions explain Channa attained arahantship just before or at death — why the story is not read as "the Buddha permitting suicide." Some modern research also debates these discourses' textual structure. But most certain from the texts themselves: the Buddha did not assess Channa's death as an ordinary rebirth-fruit case.

3.2. Vakkali — "his death will not be a bad death"

Venerable Vakkali was also gravely ill. The Buddha visited him; afterward Vakkali expressed deep understanding of the five aggregates (Pali: pañca-khandhā - five aggregates) — form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness — all impermanent, all unworthy of clinging as "mine, me, my self." After the Buddha left, Vakkali ended himself. Two devas said Vakkali was heading to liberation, and the Buddha confirmed: "Do not fear, Vakkali; his death will not be a bad death." [Source: SN 22.87 (Vakkali Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

Many traditions explain Vakkali attained liberation at the final moment. The point to remember: this is a gravely ill bhikkhu with special mind-state and realization-level — not a person despairing about life.

3.3. Godhika — attaining with-residue nirvana

Venerable Godhika repeatedly attained temporary liberative states then lost them. After six such times, he ended himself. Māra (Pali: Māra - Māra, the tempter) sought Godhika's rebirth-consciousness but could not find it — for a liberated mind has no rebirth-place to find. The Buddha confirmed Godhika attained with-residue nirvana (Pali: parinibbāna - final Nibbāna). [Source: SN 4.23 (Godhika Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

4. What those cases teach — and do not teach

The Channa, Vakkali, Godhika stories do not teach "Buddhism permits suicide." They teach something subtler: karma cannot be assessed by outer action alone; actors' mind-states and liberation-levels play decisive roles.

  • Not every self-caused death is textually assessed alike.
  • One who has destroyed craving differs from one dying of despair.
  • Rebirth-fruits cannot be inferred from death-manner alone.

But reading backward — taking exceptions as rules — is also wrong: "ordinary despairing suicide will also be like Channa" is a false inference. Those three are not described as despairing about life, not described as carrying hateful or fearful minds, and are placed in very high realization-level contexts. Another deep paradox must be seen: thinking "dying ends all suffering" rests on assuming death ends the causal stream — yet per the rebirth teaching, if ignorance (Pali: avijjā - ignorance) and craving remain, the karmic stream continues conditionally. Suffering's true ending is nirvana (Pali: nibbāna - liberation) — ending greed — hatred — delusion, not merely making the body die (articles Q.C1 and Q.C4 distinguish death from nirvana; article G.C7 on the liberation goal).

5. So what about ordinary suicide?

Ordinary suicide is usually not from "having destroyed craving." Motives usually approach: despair, unbearable suffering, wanting to escape present feelings, self-hatred, shame, future-hopelessness, no-exit feelings. If action is strongly driven by those unwholesome minds, unwholesome karma may be very considerable — why tradition views suicide very gravely though offering no "suicide = hell" formula.

One cannot say surely "suicide makes how many times heavier karma than natural death" — the discourses give no such coefficient. But deliberately taking life is a volitional act; accompanied by hatred, despair, ill will, or confusion, it may make considerable unwholesome karma. And overly simple moral judgment must be avoided in cases: severe mental illness, extreme crisis, considerably lost judgment-capacity — in such cases, volition-degree and mind-states differ greatly from sober decisions, and the discourses pronounce no single verdict for all cases.

On rumors spreading after such deaths — "suicide becomes wandering spirits," "suicide becomes hungry ghosts" — the Canon has no such formulas (the "wandering" and hungry-ghost concepts are separately discussed in articles Q.B7, Q.B8 and Q.F1). One after death is reborn per overall karma and conditions — one death-manner alone cannot surely declare where they are.

6. Where does "death ends pain" err?

In the Buddhist view, death does not guarantee ending suffering. If suffering's deep causes remain — greed, hatred, delusion, clinging — then death is only one body-and-mind life's ending, not necessarily the whole suffering-round's ending. Hence: suicide ≠ liberation. One may end the body yet not end craving. Conversely, one who has ended greed — hatred — delusion, their final life's death is final nirvana — not liberated through suicide.

Besides "after-death" fruits, right-present fruits must also be seen: mind-streams "I am useless," "I have no exit," "I want others pained like me" already create present suffering — also part of karma, for karma is not only "post-mortem punishment" but which direction mind is training itself. And not all self-harming behaviors should be lumped: suicide with dying-intent differs from non-dying-intent self-injury, differs from reckless behavior, differs from treatment-refusal — intention is decisive.

7. Compassion for suicide victims and those left behind

Buddhism views suicide victims first as beings who suffered — not persons deserving curses. The fitting response is compassion + conditional understanding + preventing further suffering, not contempt or exclusion. Telling bereaved families "they will go to hell" is both scripturally groundless for all cases and further pains mourners — a more balanced phrasing: "One's karma depends on intention and the whole causal stream; suicide usually joins painful mind-states, but death-manner alone cannot surely declare their rebirth."

Those left behind can still do good deeds and dedicate for the departed — no reason to say "because they died by suicide they deserve no offerings." The Canon shows the living's giving and dedication may benefit the dead under suitable conditions — when they are in realms "able to receive merit" from dedicated giving. [Source: Tirokuḍḍa Sutta, Kp 7; and the dedicated-giving discourse, AN 10.177] If they are in unsuitable places, dedicated offerings do not reach them by the stated mechanism — yet the living still make wholesome karma for themselves, and that is never futile. This mechanism's details are presented in articles Q.H3 and Q.H4 (article group Q.H1 → Q.H5).

In modern life, for the suffering, the Buddhist way does not say "endure indefinitely" but: finding supporters, finding treatment when needed, practicing mindfulness, developing loving-kindness, and seeing present states are impermanent (all mind-states impermanent — article G.C5). Seeking medical and psychological support does not contradict Buddhism — on the contrary, it may be a form of compassion (Pali: karuṇā - compassion) toward oneself (article M.D2.2 on compassion-cultivation).

8. Summary and next steps

  • No "suicide = surely hell" rule — nor may the three canonical exceptions turn suicide into a spiritual path.
  • Suicide falls within very grave killing in the Vinaya; each case's karma assessed per intention, mind-state, and whole causal stream.
  • Channa, Vakkali, and Godhika are three special liberation-level cases: the discourses describe their deaths as "blameless," "not a bad death," and "attaining nirvana" — contexts untransferable into general rules.
  • "Death ends pain" errs in that: with ignorance and craving remaining, the karmic stream continues; only nirvana truly ends suffering.
  • Those left behind act with compassion: not cursing the departed, doing good and dedicating, supporting the suffering — including seeking medical, psychological support when needed.

Glossary:

  • Killing living beings (Pali: pāṇātipāta): deliberately causing a living being's death; in this article — including personally taking one's own life.
  • Vinaya Piṭaka (Pali: Vinaya Piṭaka): the canonical section recording Saṅgha-life rules.
  • Pārājika: bhikkhus' gravest violation-level, entailing loss of monastic status; the third rule prohibits deliberately taking human life.
  • Intention (Pali: cetanā): karma's core — "Intention, I declare, is karma" (AN 6.63).
  • Karma (Pali: kamma): deliberate bodily - verbal - mental action with its natural results; no verdict pronounced by anyone.
  • Craving (Pali: taṇhā): the thirst making mind want to push away or end what exists; in this article — craving to end present painful feelings.
  • Ignorance (Pali: avijjā): the rebirth-stream's root; while remaining, death does not end the suffering-round.
  • Five aggregates (Pali: pañca-khandhā): in the Channa and Vakkali stories, what they contemplated to no longer cling as "mine, me, my self."
  • Māra (Pali: Māra): in the Godhika story — seeking rebirth-consciousness yet finding none, for liberated mind has no rebirth-place.
  • Nirvana (Pali: nibbāna): complete ending of suffering; ending greed — hatred — delusion and craving-born rebirth; unlike bodily death.
  • Final Nibbāna (Pali: parinibbāna): the phrase the discourses use for Godhika's case.
  • Compassion (Pali: karuṇā): the fitting response to the suffering and those left behind — not contempt or curses.
  • Bhikkhu (Pali: bhikkhu): fully ordained monks under the Pāli Vinaya; subjects of the Pārājika rules mentioned in this article.
