Q.J4. If everything is impermanent, what meaning does love have?

This is the closing question of group J, and also a question many carry hearing of impermanence (Pali: anicca - impermanence): if everything changes, the loved will one day depart, so why love? This question usually hides a confusion between "not lasting forever" and "having no value." Buddhism's answer may surprise: yes. Indeed, looking deeply into impermanence may make love more genuine — for what Buddhism questions is not love, but craving (Pali: taṇhā - craving) and clinging (Pali: upādāna - clinging) hiding in how we love.

1. The short answer

The question should be reframed: instead of "since everything will change, why love?" — ask "knowing my loved ones are impermanent, how will I love them so love does not turn into possessiveness, fear, and suffering?"

  • Buddhism does not teach that because everything is impermanent one must be cold or love no one. The Canons devote many teachings to family life, marriage, mutual care, and loving-kindness (section 5).
  • What causes suffering is not things changing, but our wanting what changes to not change — where craving and clinging operate.
  • Impermanence does not destroy love; it destroys the illusion that love can be forever possessed. As that illusion lessens, love may shift from "you must stay here for me to be happy" to "knowing you are impermanent, while together, I want to love you wakefully and kindly."

2. "Impermanent" does not mean "meaningless"

A common misunderstanding: "if in the end all is lost, then nothing is meaningful." But something's not lasting forever does not prove it valueless while existing. One meal ends. One sunset ends. Youth ends. One conversation ends. One life ends. Yet those things may still be meaningful.

Doctrinally, impermanence describes conditioned phenomena's (Pali: saṅkhata - conditioned things) character: they arise, change, and perish. The Canon states very plainly: all we love, changing over time, will alter — if one clings to that, suffering will be born precisely from loved things' change. [Source: Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 22.1 (Nakulapitā Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism] Note: the discourses point out where suffering is born — in clinging — not concluding "love is bad."

3. Precisely because impermanent, love is precious

Knowing surely your loved ones will never change, bodies never age, no one falls ill, no one dies, relations never lost — then "cherishing" feelings might be very different. One hug is meaningful precisely because it is a hug happening now. A father watching children grow is meaningful because today's child will no longer be that child ten years later. An old couple sitting together in old age is meaningful because both dwell in finite lives.

Ask: "What meaning do flowers have if they will fade?" — that question sounds absurd; usually we ask reversely: "because they will fade, do I see them today?" Likewise: "because this person will one day leave my life, am I truly present beside them today?". In that sense, impermanence is not love's enemy — it is part of the context making love urgent and cherishable. That is a reply very close to mindfulness of death (Pali: maraṇassati - mindfulness of death) spirit in the Meditation tradition: remembering finitude not for pessimism, but for living more rightly (the recollection-group — including mindfulness of death — is presented in article M.D3.7 of the Meditation section).

4. Yet the discourses also warn: feelings easily accompany suffering

Honesty with the issue's other face is needed. The Dhammapada has a series plainly linking: fondness accompanies worry, attachment accompanies suffering; from fondness fear is born, from attachment fear is born — whoever escapes fondness and attachment no longer fears and grieves. [Source: Dhammapada, verses 212–216 - Theravāda Buddhism] The point to read rightly: the discourses do not say "love is bad" — they point to clinging to what one loves: losing loved objects births fear and suffering.

Placed side by side:

  • Love: "The best I wish for you is your peace, though life does not always follow my wishes." / Clinging: "You must stay beside me."
  • Love: "I cherish you very much." / Clinging: "Because I cherish you, you must respond exactly as I want."
  • Love: "I want to care for you." / Clinging: "Having sacrificed for you, you must belong to me."

Outwardly the two may look alike, yet differ psychologically: one opens mind, one binds mind to "I" and "mine" (the craving - loving-kindness distinction was presented in article Q.J3).

5. Buddhism does not teach "do not love" — teachings for householders

Were Buddhism only wanting people not to love, many family-life teachings would be hard to understand. Two typical discourses:

  • The co-dwelling discourse: an old couple telling the Buddha they wish to dwell together not only this life but later lives. The Buddha did not deny that feeling; he directed both to shared grounds: faith, virtue, giving, and wisdom in common — keeping those together, later lives they will meet again and dwell together. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 4.55 (Samajīvī Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]
  • The discourse teaching Sigāla's son: in the husband-wife-role part, the Buddha describes the two's relation with respect, non-contempt, fidelity, and mutual support. [Source: Dīgha Nikāya, DN 31 (Sigālovāda Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

This shows: Buddhism neither equates householder life with the liberation path, nor regards householder life as worthless. The Canon exists in two contexts — the renunciant path (sensuality-relinquishing more fitting the swift-liberation goal) and householder life (marriage, family guided morally). Hence householders may live in love while practicing: no lying, no betrayal, no injuring, no turning the other into property, developing loving-kindness and wisdom. Saying "Buddhist practice absolutely forbids love" applies one practice-context to all life — a wrong simplification.

6. Separating "love's" components

In everyday language, "love" packs many components: fondness, cherishing, care, intimacy, desire, missing, empathy, protection — plus possessiveness, fear of loss. Buddhism tends to separate these components instead of lumping into one block:

  • Goodwill, loving-kindness, compassion, care — components encouraged for nourishing.
  • Craving and clinging — suffering-prone components, objects to clearly see and transform.

So "Buddhism says destroy love" is too crude a phrasing. More precisely: Buddhism seeks transforming craving-and-clinging-based loving into less-harmful, wider, less-possessive loving. It must also be added: love is not the stronger the better — "intense" yet jealous, controlling, manipulative feelings may pain both; a less dramatic yet truthful, kind, boundary-knowing love better fits the suffering-reducing goal. And because everything is impermanent, love needs all the more caring: an impermanent garden is no reason not to water plants; an impermanent relation is no reason to stop caring.

7. "Non-clinging" does not mean "not caring"

These two sentences entirely differ:

  • Not caring: "Whether they live or die, whatever."
  • Letting go of clinging: "They are very important to me, but I know I cannot control their whole lives."

One may care very deeply yet understand: the other has changing-rights; the other is no property; relations may end; bodies will age; illness may occur. That is not coldness — that is love with added wisdom of life's conditions.

A subtle transformation can be summed: from keeping people to caring for people — "you must stay beside me" into "I wish you happiness"; "children must live exactly as father wants" into "father wants the best for children while respecting them as separate persons"; "parents must live forever" into "knowing parents do not live forever, I want to presently kindly be with them today." We still keep care — but not ownership-rights.

8. Impermanence helps us love "people as they are"

Often we do not love present persons themselves — we love a mind-version: "he must be as in the first year," "she must always stay as gentle," "my child must forever remain obedient," "my parents must always stay healthy." But people do not stand still: today's loved ones may differ from last year, maturing, changing tastes, failing, wounded, weakened, aging.

If love depends on "always stay exactly as my image of you," then impermanence becomes the opponent. Understanding "I love a changing person," impermanence becomes a truth to accompany. This is also where the Canon speaks of the five aggregates (Pali: khandha - aggregates): form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness are all impermanent, and should not be grasped as "this is mine, this is me, this is my self." [Source: SN 22.59 (Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism] Applied to relations: loved ones — comprising changing body-and-mind — are no wholly ownable objects; and the possessing-demanding "self" itself is unfixed.

9. So losing loved ones still hurts?

Yes — and that does not prove you "do not understand Dhamma." Intellectually knowing impermanence and truly living with impermanence are two different matters: you may know "everyone will die," yet when mother passes, you still weep. Impermanence-knowledge is not synonymous with clinging-freedom — and pain losing relatives is a natural part of loving real people.

What Buddhism aims at is not turning hearts to stone, but: letting hearts ache without becoming pain's slaves. There may be love → loss → pain, without necessarily pain → hatred → blame → obsession → destroying oneself or others. Each training step needs time; mindfulness with strong feelings is presented in article M.C2.2, and observing feelings in life in article M.G1.3.

One more practical face must be stated clearly: changing mind does not mean enduring all circumstances. With truly harmful conditions (violence, abuse), changing outer conditions — protecting oneself, seeking help, leaving — is also part of wisdom. Impermanence-teaching should not advise the harmed to "merely let go."

10. Love becoming a practice path — and three meaning-levels

For householders, relations themselves very clearly expose what happens in mind — love becomes a mirror:

  • When lovers do not reply to messages — observing: "am I pained by this event, or by stories I create around the event?"
  • When loved ones change — "do I love this person, or their image?"
  • When relatives age — "am I resisting reality, or learning to presently be with reality?"
  • When parting — "do I wish them happiness, or only want them back to end my pain?"

Thanks to that mirror, love can be summed on three meaning-levels:

  • Everyday level: love makes intimacy, family, joy, support, memories — valuable things though impermanent.
  • Moral level: love offers opportunities practicing fidelity, kindness, patience, non-harming, knowing giving, knowing forgiveness, respecting others' freedom — a true moral practice-field (bodily - verbal - mental behavior-results in relations nourished which way differ — the karma (Pali: kamma - intentional action) ground already presented in article G.C4).
  • Spiritual level: love shows us craving, clinging, fear of loss, self, controlling-desires — and their transformability; on this level, love itself teaches loving deeply without needing possession.

A very simple practice beside loved ones, instead of only thinking "may we forever be so," three contemplations can be added: "this is changing" (the person before, oneself, the relation — all changing); "this does not wholly belong to me" (the other has their own life, will, and path); "hence, I will be present" (needing no turning moments into eternity — only truly being in this moment).

11. Three mindsets before the same impermanence-truth

The same "everything changes" truth, three different mindsets may form:

  • Avoiding: "I know my wife will age, but I do not want to think of it." — impermanence pushed out of sight, becoming shock when arriving.
  • Fearing loss and controlling: "My wife will age so I must keep her beside me at all costs." — impermanence turned into clinging.
  • Cherishing: "My wife will age, so I want to cherish the time we are still healthy together." — impermanence used to nourish gratitude.

The final paradox to remember: we usually think "loving deeply requires clinging tightly" — but Buddhism suggests reversely: the tighter clinging, the more fearing loss; the more understanding impermanence, the more cherishing without suffocating. If summing with one image: impermanence is like a river — love is not building walls forcing the river to stand still; maturer love learns to walk with the river. You still hold hands, care, build families, stay faithful, age together — yet in mind-depth, still knowing "nothing guarantees this will last forever" — and precisely hence, not wanting to waste it.

12. Summary and next steps

  • Impermanent ≠ meaningless. Not lasting forever does not mean valueless while existing.
  • Impermanence makes love more precious — for what is present is unrepeated.
  • What causes suffering is craving and clinging — clinging to what one loves, not natural love (Dhp 212–216).
  • Buddhism does not teach "do not love" — the Canon guides married life with faith, virtue, giving, wisdom (AN 4.55) and respect, fidelity (DN 31).
  • "Non-clinging" ≠ not caring — transforming from keeping people to caring for people.
  • Losing people still hurts — and pain is no sign of "not understanding the teaching"; the goal is aching without becoming pain's slave.
  • Three meaning-levels: everyday, moral, spiritual — love may become a true practice-field.
  • Paradox: the tighter clinging the more fearing loss; the more understanding impermanence the more cherishing without suffocating.

Glossary:

  • Impermanence (Pali: anicca): all things' changing character; one of the three characteristics.
  • Craving (Pali: taṇhā): suffering's root per the origin of suffering; the component easily mixed into possessive-style love.
  • Clinging (Pali: upādāna): mind's grasping, holding-tight behavior after craving.
  • Loving-kindness (Pali: mettā): wishing beings well — goodwill needing no return, no possession.
  • Compassion (Pali: karuṇā): wishing beings free from suffering.
  • Conditioned (Pali: saṅkhata): body, mind, relations — all arising then ceasing.
  • Five aggregates (Pali: khandha): analyzing "persons" into changing components (article G.C5).
  • Karma (Pali: kamma): deliberate bodily - verbal - mental action with its natural results.
  • Five precepts (Pali: pañcasīla): no killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, lying, intoxicants — householders' moral ground.
  • Mindfulness of death (Pali: maraṇassati): one of the Meditation tradition's recollections; remembering finitude not for pessimism, but for more wakeful living.
  • Theravāda Buddhism: the Pāli-Canon-based tradition, popular in Sri Lanka, Thailand, Myanmar, Laos, Cambodia.
  • Loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, equanimity (Pali: brahmavihāra): four vast mind-qualities encouraged for cultivation; detailed in article G.C6 and the Meditation section's group M.D2.
