Q.I1. Is doing good deeds in order to become wealthy a form of greed?

The Ethics-and-Doing-Good group opens with a question at the intersection of morality and material life: "Is doing good deeds in order to become wealthy a form of greed?" — for the answer will govern how we view many things: giving, offering, helping people, even daily business. Seeming to need only yes or no. But the Buddhist teaching gives a subtler answer: wishing for possessions is not automatically greed — the Buddha acknowledged lay followers (Pali: upāsaka - lay follower) may own property and have happiness from rightful property; yet doing good with a "bargaining" mind does not automatically cleanse craving either. The issue lies not in the word "rich," but in mind's relation to wealth. This article works step by step through that.

1. The short answer

The quickest answer: "doing good to become rich" cannot be called = greed; yet neither can "all good deeds are entirely greed-free." Place three common people side by side:

  • The first person thinks: "I want enough money to support family, debt-free, and help parents." — This wish is not necessarily greed; it may be responsibility and love.
  • The second person thinks: "I want successful business, means, then building schools, helping the poor." — Genuine goodwill exists; alongside still some self-seeking. Ordinary minds are mixed so: kindness, growth-wishes, responsibility, and self-benefit-desire co-present.
  • The third person thinks: "I must be richer than all. I must win at all costs." — Here greed and conceit (Pali: māna - conceit) are already clear.

Hence the right question is not "greedy or not?" ticking one box, but three smaller questions:

  • Rich for what?
  • Earning money how, harming anyone?
  • How much does mind cling to money — especially when losing?

Placing these three side by side, one conclusion gradually appears: wishing for possessions is not automatically greed; yet doing good with a bargaining mind to exchange for possessions still craves. Following sections open each aspect.

2. Buddhism does not teach lay followers to be poor

First remove a reverse misunderstanding: thinking good practice requires hating money, poverty for purity. The discourses say otherwise. In the Anāthapiṇḍikovāda Sutta, the Buddha speaking with the householder Anāthapiṇḍika — the famous wealthy lay follower of the Buddha's time — stated four happinesses householders may have:

  • ownership happiness (Pali: atthisukha);
  • enjoyment happiness (Pali: bhogasukha);
  • debtlessness happiness (Pali: ananasukha);
  • blamelessness happiness (Pali: anavajjasukha) — body, speech, mind pure. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 4.62 (Anāthapiṇḍikovāda Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

Notably, the discourse describes the first happiness's property as wealth righteously earned, through one's own effort and labor. Those four happinesses are spoken for Buddhist householders (article G.A3).

Two conclusions from the discourse:

  • Owning property is in itself not prohibited. The Buddha did not teach lay followers to abandon occupations, accumulation, to live miserably; the discourses acknowledge both ownership-joy and enjoyment-joy.
  • Yet the discourses rank the fourth happiness above money-related happinesses: the wise see blameless-life happiness as far more valuable. In other words: the Buddha did not deny possessions' joy, but placed it beneath a pure life's value.

The Aṅguttara further calls faith, virtue, moral shame, fear of wrongdoing, learning, generosity, and wisdom the seven riches — saying those possessing them are truly rich, their lives not barren. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 7.6 (Vitthatadhana Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism] The meaning for this article's question: a person's worth is not measured by material assets — neither for self-contempt when poor nor self-pride when rich.

3. When possessions become good-doing means

The above discourse does not stop at "having money is good." It distinguishes ownership-happiness from enjoyment happiness: householders enjoying rightful wealth's fruits and doing meritorious deeds with it. This is a very important direction: lawful possessions may become good-doing means — supporting family, paying fair wages, helping the needy, offering, giving (Pali: dāna - generosity).

In the discourses, wholesome deeds like giving are called creating merit (Pali: puñña - merit); worthy venerable persons deserving respect and support are likened to fields of merit (Pali: puññakkhetta - field of merit) — like good soil, merit sown therein bears wholesome fruit. Tradition even has a separate recollection practice on generosity — recollection of generosity (article M.D3.7).

Through this a natural conclusion appears: wealth is not necessarily opposed to morality. The discourses even teach householders to manage assets — balancing income-expenditure, avoiding waste, laziness, treating those around responsibly (section 6 will cite details). Money's moral issue therefore lies not in coins themselves: money has no intrinsic morality; how people make and use it matters.

4. Fruit-seeking-tinged wholesome karma — when deeds are wholesome yet mind still "wants"

This is the answer's core. Two situations need separating.

4.1. Doing good truly to help people — while also hoping to do better

One gives, helps the ill, builds schools, thinking: "I do so my life also improves." One cannot merely for that wish conclude the deed unwholesome. Outer action remains wholesome karma (Pali: kusala kamma - wholesome karma) — wholesome (Pali: kusala - wholesome) speaks of action-quality. Yet inside, mind still holds "I want to have." Hence distinguish: something may be wholesome, yet its performing mind has not wholly ended thirst — "wholesome" is not synonymous with "greed-ended." Karma lies in intention, not outer results (article G.C4): the same giving act, done with mind wishing the needy relieved is one karma; done with mind "giving to demand back manyfold" is another karma.

4.2. Doing good as an exchange — "bargaining with karma"

Some sayings show mind wholly shifted to transaction: "I donate this money so the Buddha must let me win the lottery." "The more beings I release, the richer I must become." This treats karma as a vending machine: inserting merit-amount, receiving exactly money-amount. The discourses do not present karma so — one deed's fruit depends on many conditions, far more complex than money-exchange (article Q.I4). Offering still creates true merit; yet that bargaining mind nourishes further thirst — precisely what the Buddhist path seeks to gradually reduce. Article Q.H2 already discussed temple money-donating separately.

4.3. Three layers examining one wholesome deed

  • Layer 1 — action: you truly help people → the deed may be wholesome.
  • Layer 2 — motive: you also want to receive wholesome fruits → wish-tinged wholesome karma; the wishing part does not make the wholesome deed bad.
  • Layer 3 — clinging: you think "I must receive exactly my wanted result; otherwise I will anger" → craving (Pali: taṇhā - craving) and clinging (Pali: upādāna - clinging) strongly operating.

In short: ask not only "greedy or not?", but "to what extent is greed ruling me?".

5. Three signs recognizing greed

Greed is not measured by money owned — it is measured by how much mind clings to possessions. The rich may be greed-free; the poor may still greed strongly (envying the rich, obsessing over money all day, always scheming to seize). Three signs below help self-examine:

  • "Never enough." Two people earning 1 billion alike: the first feels enough — repaying debts, supporting family, dedicating part to helping; the second with 1 billion wants 10, with 10 wants 100, having them wants more than others. The issue is no longer numbers, but mind without stopping-point. That unstopping thirst is precisely what the Buddha pointed to as suffering's root (article G.C1).
  • Fearing loss, and reactions when losing. This is the strongest test. Losing money with still relatively peaceful mind → little clinging. Losing money with despair, flaring, ready to do anything to regain → strong clinging. Hence "rich yet greed-free" is fully possible: seeing property as a conditionally converged tool, using rightly, sad when lost yet not collapsing — utterly unlike thinking "losing money I am no one." Letting-go practice when losing corresponds to equanimity (article M.D2.4).
  • Ready to harm to have. Deceiving, exploiting, bribing, lying to enrich — then no longer only "wanting wealth": unwholesome behavior has appeared, and a "good" goal cannot turn "bad" means "wholesome" (section 6).

The opposite of greed in canonical analysis is non-greed (Pali: alobha - non-greed). Briefly: wealth is not the issue; being "owned" by wealth is the issue.

6. Livelihood must be right — right livelihood

"Doing good to become rich" often covers a more important question: earning livelihood how. In the Noble Eightfold Path (Pali: ariya-aṭṭhaṅgika-magga - Noble Eightfold Path) — presented fully in article G.C2 — there is right livelihood (Pali: sammā-ājīva - right livelihood): not earning through harmful occupations; the discourses list such as weapons-trading, being-trading, meat-trading, intoxicant-trading, poison-trading. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 117 (Mahācattārīsaka Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

This principle removes a popular thinking-way: good intentions do not legitimize all means. "I want wealth for charity" sounds very good; yet earning through deception, harmful trading, exploitation makes good goals unable to turn bad means wholesome. For householders, the no-killing, no-stealing, no-lying precepts — three of the five precepts (Pali: pañcasīla - five precepts) — are precisely the frame applied to daily occupations.

Theravāda Buddhist discourses give householders concrete money teachings: balancing income – expenditure, no waste, no excessive stinginess, reasonably dividing assets (one part consuming, part for business, part reserved), diligence in occupations, and responsible relations with life's six "directions" — parents, teachers, spouses, friends, workers, and practitioners. [Source: Dīgha Nikāya, DN 31 (Sigālovāda Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism] Managing assets well is thus not contrary to the moral path — on the contrary, a responsible householder with assets is encouraged.

7. Five self-questions and the direction

7.1. Five self-questions before a financial goal

  • "Without this money-amount, do I still see myself as a valuable person?" — if no, self-worth is tied to results; a clinging-form.
  • "Am I ready to harm anyone to attain it?" — the sign greed has crossed the moral boundary.
  • "If richer, will I despise people more?" — greed usually accompanies conceit.
  • "Will these assets serve me, or will I become assets' servant?"
  • "Am I wanting more from real need, or from 'never enough' feelings?"

Self-questioning is not for self-condemning "I am greedy," but observing: which part of mind is leading? Also remember: "non-greed" does not mean "wanting nothing." Buddhism does not require householders to abandon plans, occupations, saving, and future-care. The issue is whether goals become clinging: "I want to build a good business" differs from "I must beat everyone; otherwise I am a failure" — the second already ties self-worth to results.

7.2. Direction: from fruit-seeking to contentment and using possessions to help people

A transformation-sequence many traverse can be pictured:

  • "I do good for merit." → a starting step, fully wholesome.
  • "I want rightful possessions." → wishing life-stability.
  • "I use possessions to help people." → possessions become means.
  • "I see possessions only as conditions — coming then going." → clinging subsides.
  • "I need no possessions to prove my worth."
  • "Having, use; losing, let go — letting rich-poor decide mind-quality never."

The Canon praises contentment (Pali: santuṭṭhi - contentment) as practitioners' quality; in the Karaṇīyametta Sutta, loving-kindness practitioners are summed in a "content in livelihood" life (article M.D2.1). While condition (Pali: paccaya - condition) reminds that rich-poor come from many converging conditions: effort one condition, not the sole condition. The final goal is thus neither "poor to end greed" nor "rich for merit," but: having or not, mind never ruled by greed — hatred — delusion.

8. Summary and next steps

  • Wishing for possessions is not automatically greed. The Buddha acknowledged ownership, enjoyment, debtlessness happinesses — with righteously earned property (AN 4.62) — ranking blameless-life happiness above all.
  • "Doing good to become rich" may be wish-tinged wholesome karma: deeds wholesome, yet "exchange-wanting" mind nourishes further thirst. Three examining layers: action — motive — clinging.
  • Greed measured by mind's clinging-degree to possessions, not sums: signs are "never enough," fearing loss to peace-loss, ready to harm to have.
  • Livelihood must be right (right livelihood); good goals do not legitimize means.
  • The pivotal question: not "how much money do you have?", but "how is your mind treating money?".

Glossary:

  • Lay follower (Pali: upāsaka): a home-dwelling Buddhist taking refuge and training in precepts, not gone forth.
  • Greed (Pali: lobha): the mind-material clinging, wanting to possess objects; its opposite is non-greed (Pali: alobha) — unclinging mind.
  • Craving (Pali: taṇhā): the thirst driving mind to want ever more, without stopping-point; pointed by the Canon as suffering's root (the origin of suffering, the second Noble Truth).
  • Conceit (Pali: māna): wanting superiority, admiration; in this article — rich to "beat" others.
  • Clinging (Pali: upādāna): grasping possessions, achievements, self-images as "mine"; saying "owned by possessions" denotes operating clinging.
  • Karma (Pali: kamma): deliberate bodily - verbal - mental action with its natural results; lying in intention, not anyone's judgment.
  • Wholesome karma (Pali: kusala kamma): giving, helping people, living truthfully...; "wholesome" speaks of action-quality, not yet mind wholly thirst-free.
  • Giving (Pali: dāna): the first wholesome deed usually mentioned by the Canon for householders.
  • Merit (Pali: puñña): "fruits" of meritorious deeds — giving, precept-keeping, practice.
  • Field of merit (Pali: puññakkhetta): the canonical image — highly virtuous persons are "good fields," merit sown therein bears wholesome fruit.
  • Recollection of generosity (Pali: cāgānussati): one of the recollections — recalling one's giving to brighten and strengthen mind.
  • Noble Eightfold Path (Pali: ariya-aṭṭhaṅgika-magga): right view, right thought, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration.
  • Right livelihood (Pali: sammā-ājīva): the Noble Eightfold Path's fifth branch — not earning through occupations harming self and others.
  • Five precepts (Pali: pañcasīla): no killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, lying, intoxicants — householders' moral ground.
  • Contentment (Pali: santuṭṭhi): not no striving, but mind demanding no infinity — knowing the stopping-point.
  • Condition (Pali: paccaya): rich-poor, success-failure come from many converging conditions; effort one condition, not the sole condition.
  • Anāthapiṇḍika: the famous wealthy householder of the Buddha's time, mentioned many times in the Canon.
