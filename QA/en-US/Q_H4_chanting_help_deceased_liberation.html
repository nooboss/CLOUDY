Q.H4. Does chanting sutras help the deceased find liberation?

After votive paper, donating, and offerings, the next question is very natural: in most Vietnamese funerals, chanting is indispensable — so does chanting truly help the dead "find liberation"? The answer needs passing through two layers: what chanting itself is, and what "liberation" means. Both show the same principle: the focus is not "reciting enough times," but truly wholesome karma.

1. The short answer

Per early Buddhist discourses, chanting by itself is not presented as a "spell" moving the dead from one realm to another. The discourses speak more clearly of the living making merit, giving, offering, and dedicating it to the departed; under suitable rebirth circumstances, the departed may benefit (article Q.H3). So: chanting ≠ automatically erasing karma ≠ guaranteeing liberation. But chanting may be part of creating wholesome conditions if joined with reverent mind, Dhamma-understanding, precept-keeping, giving, and dedication — and concrete effects on the dead still depend on rebirth-conditions, per AN 10.177's "suitable realm" limit.

2. What is "chanting" in substance? Three effect-layers

"Chanting" may comprise many different activities: reciting the Buddha's words, hearing discourses, reciting the Buddha's name, chanting protection verses, reciting for memorial, dedicating after chanting sessions. It should not be seen as one single act with one mechanism. Chanting with still-hateful mind, merely wanting to "work magic" for the dead — differs from chanting to understand the teaching, keep precepts, develop loving-kindness, then do good and dedicate: the two cases' practice-values differ greatly.

For effects, at least three layers can be seen:

  • Directly on the living: steadied mind, recalled teaching, less fear, developed loving-kindness, shifting from blind grief to mindfulness. This is already a real benefit.
  • Creating wholesome karma: chanting with reverence, mindfulness, wholesome resolve, learning-understanding, and practice — that activity becomes part of wholesome karma.
  • Grounding dedication: after chanting, vowing "may this wholesome deed benefit departed relatives" — under suitable conditions, the departed may benefit per the Tirokuḍḍa Sutta and AN 10.177 model.

3. What the discourses say directly — and do not say

The Tirokuḍḍa Sutta says departed relatives in the hungry-ghost realm may benefit from what living relatives do: giving, offering, rightly placing gifts, recollecting them; and states very plainly tears, lamentation, and grief do not benefit the dead, while rightly placed given gifts may long benefit. [Source: Tirokuḍḍa Sutta (Khuddakapāṭha 7; Petavatthu 1.5) - Theravāda Buddhism] This shows the discourses' focus is not "reciting very many sentences" but doing truly wholesome deeds.

Conversely, there is no canonical formula like "chanting once → the dead better; 7 times → heaven-born; 49 days → surely liberated." Specific numbers and rites may appear in later Buddhist traditions, but they should not be presented as a universal causal law the Buddha confirmed in the Pāli Canon. And AN 10.177 sets a very clear limit: some cases wholesome deeds reach the dead, some not. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 10.177 (Jāṇussoṇi Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism] Conclusion: "chanting enough times surely liberates" cannot be guaranteed.

4. "Liberation" in which sense?

In Vietnamese life, "liberation" may carry many different meanings: no longer a ghost; reborn into a good place; heaven-born; ended suffering; exited rebirth; attained nirvana. These are not identical: reborn as a better human is not synonymous with liberated from rebirth; heaven-born also not synonymous with nirvana (article Q.C1 analyzes what nirvana is). Hence saying "chanting helps the dead find liberation," one must first ask: liberation in which sense? Strictly understood: nirvana needs transformation of beings' own mind-streams — others cannot "attain awakening for them"; the living may do good for memorial purposes, but cannot live the dead's liberation path for them.

And the most asked question — "my mother passed; I chant daily for her; is she liberated?" — the most honest answer is: no one can guarantee merely because you chant that mother has surely "found liberation." But you can: chant to train mind, understand teaching, keep precepts, do good deeds, offer, dedicate — and per the discourses, if mother is in a suitable realm, those wholesome deeds may benefit her.

5. On the 49-day rites and "merit-transfer" phrases

49-day rites and many deliverance rites popular today have development-histories in later Buddhist traditions, especially East Asian Buddhism combined with local rites. They may be very meaningful for bereavement psychology, community, memorial, merit-making, and dedication — but one should not say "the Pāli Canon teaches that exactly on day 49 the dead must be reborn" or turn a sectarian/folk rite into a universal karmic law (the canonical — sectarian — folk layering spirit of article Q.H1).

On "merit-transfer" phrases: do not picture like an account-transaction (I have 100 units, giving 50 to the dead). The discourses are close to: the living create wholesome deeds; the departed may benefit from those wholesome deeds under suitable conditions — the Tirokuḍḍa Sutta's "water from high ground flowing to low" image is an image, not necessarily a physical "merit-substance" moved. The mind-direction difference: chanting then dedicating makes the wholesome mind's object clearer — in practice tradition, dedication usually accompanies offering, giving, precept-keeping, and that is nearer the discourses' model than merely reading sounds.

6. Chanting yet not living wholesomely — and the Pāli-language question

A paradox worth reflecting: one chanting a great deal, loudly, bowing much — yet still lying, hating, greedy. Should one say "chanting much surely lightens karma"? No. Buddhism does not place liberation in sound itself; what still matters is the mind → volition → action → karma chain. Picture two families chanting the same discourse for their just-passed father: family A afterward fights over property, sues; family B gives to the poor, cares for aged mother, lives kinder. The same chant — post-chant lives entirely different, and case B is what the discourses stress: real wholesome deeds, not mere rites.

On language: no basis to say "Pāli sounds themselves possess dead-saving power" — if Pāli chanting helps chanters concentrate, recall teaching, arouse faith and loving-kindness, that is practice-value; but language should not become "magic code." Conversely, chanting in Vietnamese is fully fine and has a huge advantage: chanters know what they read. One understanding "all conditioned things are impermanent" then truly contemplating may receive deeper practice-benefit than one reading perfect pronunciation without meaning. Sound and Dhamma-understanding are two different layers. Likewise, there is no "single liberating-guaranteed discourse" in the Canon — content and how mind is transformed matter more than chasing the "strongest mantra."

7. The greatest value: when chanting changes chanters

The living often ask "how much must I chant to save the dead?" — the discourses instead turn the question to: "how will I live after they pass?" If death makes us cherish parents more, hate siblings less, crave property less, do more good deeds, contemplate impermanence — then loss is becoming a practice-condition. This may be a deeper "deliverance" than rites. Relatives need no giant rite at all costs; more meaningful is the living becoming better after remembering them.

Yet rites are not denied: a rite may help focus mind, unite family, memorialize, arouse gratitude, urge doing good — on that layer it has real value. The issue appears only believing "merely reading right sounds, enough times, whatever my life, the dead are surely liberated" — a step far beyond what the discourses directly say.

8. Summary and next steps

  • Chanting is not an automatic spell bringing the dead to nirvana or switching rebirth-realms.
  • Three real effect-layers: settling the living — the living's wholesome karma — grounding dedication.
  • The Tirokuḍḍa Sutta prizes rightly placed giving and offering over sounds; weeping benefits less than doing good.
  • AN 10.177 sets the "suitable realm" limit: benefit depends on the departed's rebirth-realm.
  • "49 days" is a later tradition, with its own value — but no universal causal law of the Pāli Canon.
  • "Merit-transfer" should be understood as dedication, not money-transfer models.
  • Chanting in one's understood language is very valuable; there is no "strongest mantra."
  • The right chain: chant → understand → act → create wholesome karma → dedicate — not "chant → await miracle."

Glossary:

  • Deliverance: escaping suffering, exiting rebirth; an everyday word carrying many different meanings — good rebirth, heaven-birth, ending rebirth, nirvana; these meanings are not identical.
  • Deliverance rites: rites praying the dead escape suffering; popular in East Asian Buddhism, combining chanting, Buddha-recitation, giving, dedication.
  • Dedication of merit: vowing wholesome deeds toward others' benefit — unlike the "merit-transfer" money-like image.
  • Suitable realm: the rebirth-realm condition deciding whether the departed receive benefit (AN 10.177).
  • Paritta (Pali: paritta): protection discourses chanted for safeguarding, present in many Theravāda funerals.
  • 49-day rites: the 49-day funeral tradition in East Asian Buddhism; developed in later traditions, combined with local beliefs; possessing its own cultural — psychological value.
  • Mindfulness (Pali: sati): chanting's direct benefit-layer on the living.
