Q.B6. Can the deceased hear what the living say to them?

The previous article - Q.B5 presented: some beings after death can perceive the living (like hungry ghosts in the Tirokuḍḍa Sutta), but not every death means seeing the family. The next question follows very naturally: can the departed hear what the living say? — a child's words before the altar, an apology never spoken while they were alive, reminders that "grandparents are listening." The most careful answer: possible in some cases, but not affirmable for every dead person, and not to be imagined as hearing with living human ears.

1. The short answer

Separate the question into three levels, since the discourses permit speaking to a point at each:

  • "They know the living are doing a good deed for them" → the Pāli Canon gives a basis for saying this for some realms (Tirokuḍḍa Sutta; AN 10.177).
  • "They know the living are remembering them, calling their name" → the discourses point toward perception between relatives and the departed, but do not describe the mechanism in detail.
  • "They hear every sentence, every word, every conversation" → insufficient basis in the discourses to affirm, and it should not be stated as a teaching.

In short: "the dead can perceive the living" is not the same as "every dead person hears everything the living say" — and that perception depends on their realm of rebirth, as the previous article - Q.B5 presented.

2. What is the question assuming?

The question "can the departed hear?" usually hides a picture: the dead still have a pair of ears, standing somewhere, hearing sounds like the living. The discourses do not describe death that way. After death, beings (all sentient beings with mind - Pali: satta - sentient being) are described as reborn according to karma (Pali: kamma - intentional action) into different realms (article Q.B1, section 6). So the question must be exchanged for: "in that rebirth realm, to what extent can beings perceive sounds or affairs of the human world?" — and the answer differs case by case.

Another note: "hearing" and "knowing" are not one. A being may know something happening without hearing in the human way — this distinction will become clear in section 4.

3. What the discourses say about the departed's perception

A famous short discourse of Theravāda Buddhism describes hungry ghosts (Pali: peta - hungry ghost) gathering where their relatives are, waiting for giving and dedication. When the living offer and voice the wish "may this merit reach our relatives," those ghosts are described as rejoicing and benefiting. [Source: Tirokuḍḍa Sutta (Khuddakapāṭha 7; Petavatthu 1.5) - Theravāda Buddhism] But the discourse does not describe them sitting beside relatives and hearing entire conversations — a very important limit.

The AN 10.177 discourse clarifies the condition further: giving for the departed benefits them when they are reborn in the realm "dependent on others" (hungry ghosts); if reborn human, heavenly, animal, or in hell, they live by their own meritorious karma — offerings and dedicatory words are not described as reaching them by that mechanism. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 10.177 (Jāṇussoṇi Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

In another direction, Vajrayāna Buddhism describes in the intermediate state (Sanskrit: antarābhava - intermediate state) that the newly dead still hear relatives' weeping, see them, try to call but go unheard — a description familiar to the feelings of those left behind. [Source: Bardo Thödol - Vajrayāna Buddhism] Remember this is the presentation of one specific tradition, differing from the Theravāda model (rebirth immediately after death) — the differences among traditions were presented in article Q.B1 (section 7).

4. "Hearing" in the Buddhist cosmology

The Canon does not limit perceptive capacity to the five physical senses in the ordinary sense. The discourses describe the divine ear (Pali: dibbasota - divine ear): a practitioner with concentrated, purified, steady mind can hear sounds of humans and devas, near and far — beyond ordinary hearing range. [Source: Dīgha Nikāya, DN 7 (Jāliya Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

But two things must be distinguished very clearly:

  • The divine ear is a special attainment of realized practitioners, not default equipment of every dead person. Its existence does not prove "every dead person hears the living."
  • The Canon separates distinct capacities: divine eye (Pali: dibbacakkhu - divine eye), divine ear (hearing), knowledge of others' minds (Pali: cetopariyañāṇa - knowledge of others' minds), recollection of past lives (Pali: pubbenivāsānussati - recollection of past lives). Seeing ≠ hearing ≠ knowing minds. So when a discourse says a being "knows" relatives' offerings, it need not mean "they heard every sentence the living spoke."

It should also be added: perceiving is not two-way communication. Even in the canonical model, one being's perceiving what the living do does not mean the living can hear them answer or verify where they are — avoid turning belief into certainty.

5. Talking to the departed — is it futile?

Not futile — but name its value correctly. Standing before the altar saying "mother, I miss you," "I thank you, father," "I am sorry" can have three layers of meaning:

  • Psychological layer: words help release grief, express feelings unspoken while they lived, strengthen gratitude — a real value for the living.
  • Moral layer: words can become vows to live — "I will live kindly as mother taught" — and when truly followed, words turn into good intentions → actions → wholesome karma.
  • Doctrinal layer: if the departed are in a realm matching the discourses' description (like hungry ghosts), there is a basis to say good deeds done for them — even joined with dedicatory words — can benefit them (section 3).

What should not be done is to affirm with certainty "mother surely heard every word." Buddhism has no principle forbidding talking to the departed; what must be kept is not turning a memorial rite into a certain claim about reality that the discourses do not confirm (offering, chanting, and deliverance themes are discussed in article group Q.H, of which article Q.H4 specifically discusses whether chanting helps the dead find liberation).

6. Where folk ends — and two cautions

Two folk notions often mixed into this theme need separating:

  • Resentful ghost: folk pictures a soul "trapped" at its old place; Buddhism speaks of hungry ghosts as a realm of rebirth — the two notions share cultural resemblance but do not coincide. Saying "the dead have not transcended, so they stand by the house listening to us" is a folk interpretation, not a canonical formula.
  • Calling souls: the discourses have no rule that "calling the dead makes them at once return" — rebirth depends on karma and realm, not on the living's calling (the sudden-death and "wandering" theme is discussed in the next article - Q.B7).

A note on teaching small children: telling children "grandparents watch you 24 hours a day" can frighten them and build morality on fear of being watched. The karma teaching needs no such foundation. A healthier way: "we remember grandfather, and we live well to continue the good things he taught" — remembrance turns into wholesome action.

7. What is more certain: words act on the speaker's mind

Even when we cannot know whether the departed hear each word, one thing is far more certain: words are acting on the speaker's own mind. Saying "I thank you, mother" — gratitude is being nourished. Saying "I am sorry" — what is unhealed is being opened. Saying "I will live better" and truly doing it — words become wholesome karma. This is the part directly verifiable in life.

And in the true spirit of the discourses in section 3, if you wish to do what has the clearest scriptural foundation for the departed: turn remembrance into wholesome deeds — giving (Pali: dāna - generosity), precept-keeping, practice, and dedicating (Pali: pattidāna - transferring of merit) those good deeds (the theoretical foundation of merit and dedication is in article G.C4). Beautiful words are the starting point; real good deeds travel further.

8. Summary and next steps

  • Possible in some cases: the Canon describes hungry ghosts knowing and benefiting from the living's good deeds for them (Tirokuḍḍa Sutta; AN 10.177) — depending on realm of rebirth.
  • No basis to affirm that every dead person hears every word, all the time — and "hearing" in the human manner is not the model the discourses use.
  • Divine ear is a special attainment of realized practitioners; seeing ≠ hearing ≠ knowing minds — capacities are kept distinct.
  • Talking to the departed has real value for the living — but do not turn it into a certain metaphysical claim.
  • What is most certain: words act on the speaker's mind; and dedicated good deeds are what the discourses clearly indicate can reach the departed — under suitable conditions.

Glossary:

  • Sentient being (Pali: satta).
  • Karma (Pali: kamma): in this article — the factor deciding the realm of rebirth, thereby deciding the departed's capacity to perceive.
  • Hungry ghost (Pali: peta): a realm of rebirth; the case the discourses state directly on perceiving and benefiting from the living's giving and dedication.
  • Transferring of merit (Pali: pattidāna): wishing that the merit of one's good deeds be shared with others; the theory in article G.C4.
  • Giving (Pali: dāna).
  • Divine ear (Pali: dibbasota): a special capacity described in those of deep concentration; not default equipment of every dead person.
  • Divine eye (Pali: dibbacakkhu): presented in article Q.B5.
  • Knowledge of others' minds (Pali: cetopariyañāṇa) and Recollection of past lives (Pali: pubbenivāsānussati): two of the special capacities the Canon separates from seeing and hearing.
  • Intermediate state (Sanskrit: antarābhava): a Vajrayāna description; Theravāda has no such interval.
  • Resentful ghost: a folk concept; not matching the discourses' model of rebirth realms.
  • Devas (Pali: deva).
  • Rebirth: in Buddhism the continuation of a causal process when conditions suffice, not a soul stepping into a new body.
