Q.E3. If Buddhism teaches "non-self," then who is it that gets reborn?

This is regarded as Buddhism's greatest paradox, and the two previous articles have pushed it here: non-self (Pali: anattā - non-self) says there is no unchanging "I" (article Q.B2); rebirth says karma still continues and a new life arises (article Q.B1). So in the end, who is it that gets reborn? Is the good-doer in this life precisely the one receiving results in the next? If "precisely that person" — must there not be a continuously existing self? If "not that person" — why must this life's person bear responsibility for what they do? The discourses' answer does not lie wholly in the word "who" — but in changing how the question is put.

1. The short answer

  • No fixed "who" travels from one life to another — the "soul moving house" model is not Buddhist teaching.
  • Yet there is a continuing causal stream — old conditions condition new conditions; the continuation of a causal process when conditions suffice. These two propositions must be held together: neither turning "non-self" into "no causality," nor turning "there is rebirth" into "there is an unchanging soul."
  • The most distilled canonical answer lies in a famous dialogue text: the reborn person is "neither the same nor different" — like one flame lighting another (section 5).
  • And responsibility still stands: the continuing causal stream is precisely what karma affects — needing no unchanging "self" as owner and fruit-receiver (article Q.E2 on "there is suffering but no sufferer").

2. The assumption hidden in the word "who"

Picturing rebirth, we usually draw: person A dies → A's soul leaves the body → enters body B → person A reborn as person B. This model is very easy to understand — and rests on one assumption: an entity A exists independently, able to leave this body and enter another. That is precisely the permanent-self idea. Hence asking "who is reborn?" may already pack into the question an assumption — "there must be some unchanging who to be reborn" — and what Buddhism wants re-examined is precisely that assumption. The question is not wrong from curiosity; it is wrong in demanding something experience never shows to exist.

The two extremes on either side also need naming: eternalism (Pali: sassatavāda - eternalism) — "I am this soul; dying here, my soul goes to another life"; and annihilationism (Pali: ucchedavāda - annihilationism) — "death is the end; do whatever, since nothing remains." The middle way: there is continuity of conditions, but no unchanging self.

3. Continuity is not permanence

Is today's you entirely the same as you ten years ago? No — different body, thoughts, tastes, psychology, many cells changed. Yet you still say "that is me." Why? Because there is continuity. And here is precisely the key: continuity is not permanence. Today's river still bears the old name, yet water differs — saying "this is entirely the old river" is inaccurate; saying "this is entirely not the old river" is also inaccurate. There is continuity; no unchanging entity. If continuity needed a fixed object running through, even "ten-year-old you → thirty-year-old you" could not be explained — so we need no such assumption to explain "this-life person → next-life person" (article Q.B2, section 3).

4. The discourses answer: the Sāti story

This is precisely the question the Pāli Canon records a real case of. A bhikkhu (Pali: bhikkhu - Buddhist monk) named Sāti held the view: "it is this very consciousness that runs and wanders from one life to another, not another consciousness" — i.e. picturing consciousness as a fixed lump migrating between bodies. The Buddha reproved plainly: consciousness is no migrant; consciousness arises only conditionally — dependent on eye and forms there is eye-consciousness, dependent on ear and sounds ear-consciousness; consciousness is born, changes, and ceases per its conditions. The discourse ends with the image: as fire burns dependent on fuel, when fire goes out asking "did that fire go east, west, south, or north?" is an inapplicable question. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 38 (Mahātaṇhāsaṅkhaya Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

The meaning: what continues between two lives is not an intact "personal-consciousness lump" carried over — precisely the assumption the non-self teaching never establishes. Consciousness is a conditioned process, like all other phenomena in the aggregates. (Concepts presenting continuity in traditions — Theravāda rebirth-linking mind, Yogācāra storehouse consciousness — were analyzed in article Q.B4, section 5, with the note "do not turn them into a soul.")

5. "Neither the same nor different"

A famous dialogue text of the Pāli tradition records the conversation between King Milinda and elder Nāgasena. The king asked: is the reborn person precisely the other person? The elder answered: neither the same nor different — offering images: one lamp lighting another (the latter burns thanks to the former, yet is not the former itself); milk turning into curd, curd into butter (each stage forming dependent on the previous, nothing carried over "intact" into the later stage). [Source: Milindapañhā - Theravāda Buddhism] The flame-lighting-flame image was presented in full in article Q.B1 (section 4): rebirth is not "a soul moving house," but a conditional chain conditioning the next conditional chain to arise.

The "neither the same nor different" formula resolves both halves of the paradox at once: since "not the same," there is no unchanging entity to cling to; since "also not different," responsibility and causality are not severed.

6. So whose hands does karma fall into?

The question at once appears: without an unchanging self, who bears karma? The answer: the continuing causal stream itself is affected by karma. An example in this life: you stay up late continuously — the next day tired; keep staying up — body and mind grow accustomed; a month later, you find it very hard to sleep early. Is today's person entirely the same as one month ago? No. Is today's insomnia entirely unrelated to prior behavior? No either. That is causal relation needing no unchanging self.

Similarly with moral karma: habitually lying — mind grows accustomed to distorting truth — next lying grows easier — gradually a tendency — tendency affecting further action. A causal round forms, needing no soul inside scoring points. Karma operates through conditions and fruits — needing no "karma manager" (this operation was presented in articles Q.D1, Q.B4; and per AN 6.63, karma's core is intention — always present in the mental stream).

7. Rebirth is not "moving from A to B"

The word "rebirth" easily evokes moving. But in the dependent-origination view, what matters is not "a person traveling from A to B," but old conditions conditioning new conditions. The seed image: a seed in soil, with water, warmth, light — a sprout appears. The sprout is not the intact seed; yet the tree cannot be said entirely unrelated to the seed (this image was used in article Q.B4). Applied to rebirth: karma is the field, consciousness the seed, craving the moisture — a new life's sprout springs up when conditions suffice (AN 3.76; already presented in article Q.B4, section 5). In the Theravāda model, rebirth-linking consciousness (Pali: paṭisandhi-citta - rebirth-linking consciousness) arises immediately after the death moment — a link in the conditionally-arisen stream, arising per karma, itself arising-and-ceasing (article Q.B1, section 7).

The paradox dissolves on seeing: the question is not "who travels across?", but "which causal chain continues?" — and the answer is: karma, tendencies, craving, clinging, conditions of mind — what we conventionally call "Mr. A's successor." The conventional level remains needed and reasonable: saying "Mr. A is reborn" like saying "the river remains the river" — convention has its utility; the analytical level sees: no object transferred, only succeeding process (two language-levels were presented in article Q.E2, section 4).

8. Summary and next steps

  • The assumption "there must be some unchanging who to be reborn" is what needs re-examining — not the non-self teaching "lacking" a soul, but the question carrying one extra assumption.
  • Continuity ≠ permanence: "ten-year-old you → thirty-year-old you" already proves continuity needs no fixed entity.
  • The discourses answer directly: consciousness is no migrant (MN 38 — the Sāti story); the reborn person is "neither the same nor different" (Milindapañhā).
  • Karma falls into the continuing causal stream's hands — staying up late → tired → habit; lying → tendency — needing no "scorekeeper."
  • Rebirth is no moving — old conditions conditioning new conditions; two language-levels (conventional - analytical) both usable.
  • A sentence to remember: no "person" travels across two lives — yet there is a flow whose every moment bears prior moments' imprint.

Glossary:

  • Non-self (Pali: anattā): not meaning "does not exist"; meaning no independent, unchanging entity mastering all called "I."
  • Rebirth: in Buddhism the continuation of a causal process when conditions suffice, not a soul stepping into a new body.
  • Eternalism (Pali: sassatavāda) and Annihilationism (Pali: ucchedavāda): the two extremes surrounding the middle answer.
  • Consciousness (Pali: viññāṇa): easiest to mis-picture as a "migrating soul"; per MN 38, consciousness arises only conditionally.
  • Rebirth-linking consciousness (Pali: paṭisandhi-citta): in Theravāda Abhidhamma, the new life's first mind — a link in the conditionally-arisen stream, not a soul.
  • Karma (Pali: kamma): with intention as core (AN 6.63); affected on the continuing causal stream, needing no "scorekeeper."
  • Craving (Pali: taṇhā) — Clinging (Pali: upādāna): conditions nourishing continued renewed existence.
  • Dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda): the frame for the answer "old conditions condition new conditions."
  • Bhikkhu (Pali: bhikkhu): a fully ordained monk under the Pāli Vinaya.
  • Milindapañhā: the dialogue between King Milinda and elder Nāgasena; a post-canonical text (in some editions placed in the Minor Collection).
