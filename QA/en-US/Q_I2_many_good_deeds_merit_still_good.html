Q.I2. If someone does a great many good deeds in order to gain merit, is it still "good"?

This question directly continues the previous article - Q.I1: if "doing good to become rich" is not necessarily greed, is doing very many good deeds to pray for blessings still good? Seeming to need only yes or no. But the Buddhist teaching distinguishes many layers: outer deeds, inner motives, and mind's long-term direction. Quickly answered: blessing-seeking does not turn good deeds into evil — one giving, keeping precepts, helping people wishing blessings still creates wholesome karma. However, the discourses also show giving motives have many degrees, and those degrees decide whether good deeds stop at "merit-accumulating" or proceed into mind-transforming path.

1. The short answer

Place four people doing one thing — giving money helping the needy — side by side:

  • The first person gives fearing stinginess-blame. Giving still has value — recipients still helped; yet motives mix fear of losing face, wishing recognition.
  • The second person gives to make merit, hoping a better next life. This is clear wholesome karma — and clear fruit-seeking motive.
  • The third person gives to lessen their own stinginess, practicing letting go. This is a deeper inner level.
  • The fourth person gives unattached to "giver, gift, recipient" — giving only compassion's natural act.

One cannot say the first or second "are not wholesome." But one can say: wholesome karma's quality and mind's direction are not identical. This is precisely the difference between accumulating merit (Pali: puñña - merit) and using good deeds themselves to train mind. Following sections open each aspect.

2. Blessing-seeking is not synonymous with doing evil

This must be established at once. One thinking "I want merit so I will not kill, will give, will keep precepts" — then truly not harming, helping the hungry, supporting the ill, living truthfully: no reason exists to say "because they seek blessings their deeds become evil."

The Aṅguttara calls giving (Pali: dāna - generosity), virtue (Pali: sīla - moral conduct), and mental cultivation (Pali: bhāvanā - mental cultivation) the three merit-making-activity grounds; the discourses further describe different practice-degrees with corresponding wholesome fruits. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 8.36 (Puññakiriyāvatthu Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

This shows blessing-seeking motives may play behavior-educating and transforming roles: wanting merit, people abandon killing, learn giving, learn precept-keeping — those behaviors truly reduce others' and one's own suffering. One need not always start with perfect motives to step onto the wholesome path.

3. The Canon views giving motives

The discourses do not stop at "giving is good." The Aṅguttara has a discourse analyzing many different giving-motives: giving from tradition, giving hoping good reputation, giving hoping wholesome fruits, giving hoping good rebirth, giving to lessen stinginess and clinging — those motives leading different directions. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 7.49 (Dāna Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

Another discourse states six factors of complete giving. On givers' side: before giving rejoicing, while giving bright and clear-minded, after giving peacefully glad-minded. On recipients' side: those with greed — hatred — delusion-destroying ground or practicing to destroy. When those factors converge, giving's merit is described as very great, not easily measured. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 6.37 (Dāna Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

Notably: the discourses ask not only "how much do you give?" but view "how is your mind before, during, and after giving?". That is a very clear sign that in Buddhism, mind-quality matters more than outer form — the Canon itself distinguished "doing good deeds" from "good deeds' motives."

4. Two faces of "blessing-seeking"

4.1. The good face: a starting ladder-rung

One may pass rungs:

  • "I do not want to make bad karma" → avoidance-motivation, making one harmless.
  • "I want merit" → wholesome motivation, making one give, keep precepts.
  • "I want my mind less greedy, less stingy" → inner-practice motivation.
  • "I want to see clearly why I still cling" → wisdom-directed.
  • "Even the 'I am a good-doer' idea is contemplated" → deep letting-go-directed.

The "blessing-seeking" rung lies mid-way: no goal, yet a door bringing many from selfishness into giving. Many after giving realize: "The more I give the less stingy," "I begin rejoicing when others benefit" — initial motives have become mind-transforming means.

4.2. The still-clinging face: the "I give → I receive" structure

Yet "blessing-seeking" may also contain craving (Pali: taṇhā - craving): giving for "richer next life, more enjoyment" still nourishes possessing-wishes, enjoyment-wishes, future-controlling wishes. They still do good — yet that good still lies in exchange-structure.

Distinguish aspiration from clinging (Pali: upādāna - clinging). "May this merit benefit all" is a beautiful mind-direction. "I donated so I must become rich" is more grasping. A very simple test: if fruits do not come as wished, does mind collapse, blame, flare? If so, we cling to fruits more than imagined. While "donating so the Buddha must grant lottery-wins" is already bargaining with karma — article Q.H2 separately discusses temple money-donating, and article Q.I4 shows karmic fruits more complex than money-exchange.

In short: blessings may be a bridge, but not the entire far shore.

5. When good deeds nourish the "self"

There are cases where outer behavior is very good, yet mind quietly creates something else:

  • "I do more charity than you." → conceit (Pali: māna - conceit).
  • "I helped you so much; you must be grateful." → demanding, controlling.
  • "I give money so I may decide everything." → goodness becoming power-tool.
  • Anger when unthanked → good deeds tied to glorification-thirst.

This does not necessarily make the whole giving act unwholesome — recipients still helped, giving still valuable. But it shows something important: wholesome karma and defilements can coexist in one person. Hence Buddhism asks not only "what do you do?", but "while doing, what is your mind clinging to?".

More fearful than blessing-seeking is merit-clinging: when the "I am a good person" grows with good deeds, goodness becomes a controlling tool. The Buddhist path wants not only people doing many good things — but the self behind good deeds gradually lightening.

6. Four self-check questions

After doing good deeds, one may ask:

  • "Unknown to anyone, would I still do it?" — if yes, motives grow less fame-dependent.
  • "Unreceiving what I wished, do I regret?" — if greatly regretting, one may cling to fruits.
  • "If others are praised instead of me, am I uncomfortable?" — if yes, conceit operating.
  • "Do good deeds reduce my greed, hatred, delusion — am I glad?" — if yes, good deeds are becoming mind-practice, not only point-accumulating.

Self-checking is not for self-condemning, but observing: which part of mind is leading? All "blessing-seeking" should not be lumped into one mind-kind — "I want to live kindly," "I want merit to help more people," and "I give to surely become king next life" are three very different levels.

7. Seen from Mahāyāna Buddhism: threefold purity

Mahāyāna Buddhism, especially Prajñā thought, takes this analysis further: giving's perfection lies not in giving much quantity, but in not fixedly clinging to three spheres — giver, gift, recipient. This is called threefold purity of giving, presented in Prajñāpāramitā sūtras. [Source: Prajñāpāramitā - Mahāyāna Buddhism]

This view does not deny merit. On the contrary, it turns giving itself into perfection (Sanskrit: pāramitā - perfection) — a clinging-breaking means, not only an accumulation. Article G.E2 presents Bodhisattvas and six perfections; article M.D2.1 presents loving-kindness cultivation — giving's ground.

8. Summary and next steps

  • Blessing-seeking does not turn good deeds evil: giving, virtue, mental cultivation are the discourses' merit-making grounds (AN 8.36); giving with wholesome-fruit wishes remains wholesome karma.
  • Yet motives have many degrees: the discourses analyze different giving-motives (AN 7.49) and view givers' minds before — during — after giving (AN 6.37).
  • Blessing-seeking may be a ladder-rung bringing people from selfishness into giving — gradually turning into mind-practice.
  • Clinging's test: when fruits mismatch wishes, does mind collapse? Demanding gratitude, controlling, praise-envy — signs the self is growing.
  • Mahāyāna elevates giving into perfection through threefold purity — denying not merit, but turning giving into clinging-breaking means.

Glossary:

  • Seeking merit: doing good deeds wishing blessed fruits; in the Canon, one of many giving-motives — no evil, yet not the deepest level.
  • Merit (Pali: puñña): "fruits" of giving, precept-keeping, practice; not points granted by anyone, but good deeds' natural causal direction.
  • Three bases of meritorious action (Pali: puññakiriyāvatthu): giving, virtue, mental cultivation — stated by the Aṅguttara (AN 8.36).
  • Giving (Pali: dāna): the first wholesome deed usually mentioned by the Canon for householders.
  • Virtue (Pali: sīla): living without killing, stealing, lying...; the moral ground of both householders and monastics.
  • Mental cultivation (Pali: bhāvanā): the third merit-making ground — comprising concentration and wisdom; the Meditation section presents in detail.
  • Wholesome karma (Pali: kusala kamma): "wholesome" speaks of action-quality; not yet mind wholly thirst-free.
  • Craving (Pali: taṇhā): the thirst wanting ever more, without stopping-point; pointed by the Canon as suffering's root (the origin of suffering, the second Noble Truth).
  • Conceit (Pali: māna): wanting superiority, admiration — may grow with good deeds if goodness is used to compare.
  • Clinging (Pali: upādāna): grasping fruits, merits, "good-doer" images.
  • Taints (Pali: āsava): a very good deed may still hold wishes and clinging — i.e. still within taint-scope, not automatically leading to destruction.
  • Threefold purity of giving: not fixedly clinging to giver, gift, recipient — the giving-ideal in Mahāyāna Prajñā sūtras.
  • Perfection (Sanskrit: pāramitā): giving, virtue, patience, diligence, meditation, wisdom...; perfection-of-giving is giving practiced with threefold purity.
  • Aspiration: unlike clinging — aspiration expands, clinging grasps for self alone.
