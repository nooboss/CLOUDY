Q.H2. Does donating money to a temple really generate merit?

After the votive-paper question (previous article - Q.H1), this question comes very naturally: if burning paper money lacks scriptural basis, what about real money given to temples — does it truly generate merit? The answer is yes — giving is a merit-making ground the discourses state directly. But subtler questions follow: does donating more money surely mean more merit? Does donating to pray for wealth still count as merit? Does donating "erase karma" correctly? This article answers each.

1. The short answer

Yes — giving is a merit-making ground (Pali: puñña - merit) in the discourses. One discourse clearly states three merit-making grounds: giving (Pali: dāna - generosity), virtue (Pali: sīla - virtue), and mental cultivation (Pali: bhāvanā - mental cultivation). [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 8.36 - Theravāda Buddhism] But that does not mean handing money to a temple automatically yields a fixed "merit-amount" per sum: the discourses also examine intention (Pali: cetanā - intention), the giver's mind-state, the recipient's qualities, and how the gift is used. So: good donating is not handing much money to exchange for luck, but using giving to benefit others and make one's own mind less stingy, less attached.

2. Donating money to temples is giving — a merit-making ground

Giving means giving away, generosity. If one donates money to temples, supports the Saṅgha (Pali: Saṅgha - monastic community) or helps maintain practice-life with a wholesome mind, that is merit-making wholesome karma — not mere folk custom, but directly grounded in the discourses. Donating also joins the field of merit (Pali: puññakkhetta - field of merit) image: recipients' qualities affect giving's fruit, like seeds sown on different soils yielding different results; those with virtue and completed spiritual life are described as an especially fertile merit-field. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 3.57 (Vacchagotta Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism] But note: the discourses stress genuine qualities, not mere form — do not infer "wearing robes automatically makes a merit-field."

3. A donation's value does not lie only in the amount

If "donating 100 million = very great merit" sufficed, karma would resemble a cash-register system. The discourses do not present it so. One discourse analyzes giving with six factors — on the giver's side: before giving mind rejoices, while giving mind bright and clear, after giving mind rejoices; on the recipient's side those who have destroyed or are practicing to destroy greed, hatred, delusion. Giving complete with those factors is described as very great merit. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 6.37 (Chaḷaṅgadāna Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

Compare two cases: Person A possessing huge assets donates a very large sum yet inwardly thinks "I want everyone to know I am rich." Person B with low income dedicates a considerable share of their means without regret. Both "donate money," yet letting-go degree, motives, and clinging-degree differ. Hence: donating 100 million does not automatically merit more than donating 100 thousand — the sum is one factor, not the sole factor.

4. Donating motives — the discourses list very honestly

One discourse lists eight motives for giving, very worldly yet very true: giving from fear; giving because "this person gave me"; giving hoping the other will give back; giving thinking "giving is good"; giving because "I cook, they do not"; giving because "giving brings me fame"; giving to adorn the mind; giving to train and beautify one's own mind. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 8.31 (Dāna Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism] This list shows: the same money-giving act may stand on very different minds.

So does donating to pray for blessings still count as merit? It may still be merit. "I donate praying my family stays safe" — therein may simultaneously be reverence, goodwill, wishing relatives well, and result-seeking. No need to conclude "having wishes makes the whole donation unwholesome." But distinguish donating to have good fruits from donating to practice letting go, helping people, and lightening one's own stinginess — two motives of different depth, and practitioners can gradually shift from the former to the latter as inner maturation.

5. Three misunderstandings to avoid

  • "Donating to buy fortune": "I donate 5 million; the Buddha must make me rich in return" — picturing a money → Buddha → fortune transaction. The Buddha is not presented as a merit-selling merchant. If wholesome fruits arise, they result from karma and dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda - dependent origination), not "service fees" paid to a god (article Q.F2 already presented Buddhism's view of petitioning).
  • "Donating to erase all karma": picturing wrongdoing → karma-debt → temple-donating → wiped clean is also wrong. Giving creates new wholesome karma and helps transform life — but does not turn bad karma into "never happened." Deeper transformation needs: ending unwholesome behavior, repairing faults, keeping precepts, developing wholesome mind (article Q.D3 on changing karma, article Q.D5 on causality needing no judge).
  • "Karma is an addition-subtraction table": killing −10, temple-donating +50, remaining +35? Buddhism does not present karma as a score-table. Each action has its own volition, object, degree, circumstances, and fruit — "I donated much so all other faults are balanced" is not right understanding.

6. Donating to temples or helping the poor — which generates more merit?

There is no absolute formula. One discourse rejects the idea that only giving to practitioners bears fruit, saying preventing others' giving obstructs both givers' merit and recipients' benefit; while affirming giving to the virtuous bears greater fruit than giving to the immoral. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 3.57 - Theravāda Buddhism] Hence: helping a hungry person may be very meaningful giving; supporting a genuine practice community may also be very meaningful giving. Instead of "temples or the poor always better?", ask: "Whom is this gift truly benefiting, and how?"

And stay sober about recipients: if money is misused, wasted, or serving harmful purposes, one should not say "because I donated, merit is surely huge." The giver's wholesome mind is one separate factor; yet choosing recipients and how gifts are used also matter — wise donating is not synonymous with blind donating. Also know: one may donate hugely yet still deceive, exploit, hate — giving's wholesome karma is real, but it does not automatically erase all other unwholesome karmas of the same person.

7. Giving's greatest fruit lies right in the giver's mind

One habitually thinking "everything is mine," beginning to give practices "I can let go a part." Gradually: stinginess lessens, lack-fear lessens, sharing-capacity grows, mind widens. The discourses record the highest motive among the eight giving-motives precisely as "giving to adorn and train the mind." [Source: AN 8.31 - Theravāda Buddhism] Hence: giving benefits not only recipients; it trains the givers themselves.

From this a maturation ladder can be pictured: donating to pray for fruits → donating knowing doing good is good → donating from care → donating to reduce stinginess → donating without merit-clinging → seeing even the "giver" as clinging-place → giving becoming wisdom-means (in Mahāyāna Buddhism, the perfection of generosity (Sanskrit: dāna-pāramitā - perfection of generosity) presents that deep level; article group G.E2). The final goal is not "I have so much merit," but: "my mind is less and less ruled by craving."

8. Summary and next steps

  • Yes — giving is a merit-making ground (AN 8.36); donating money supporting genuine practice with wholesome mind is wholesome karma.
  • Value lies not only in sums: six giving-factors — mind before, during, after giving; recipients' qualities (AN 6.37, AN 3.57).
  • The discourses list eight giving-motives — from fear, fame-seeking to mind-training (AN 8.31); blessing-seeking donating may still be wholesome, yet can mature further.
  • Avoid three misunderstandings: more donating = more merit; donating = buying fortune; donating = erasing all karma.
  • Temple-donating and poor-helping may both be good giving — ask "whom does the gift benefit" instead of mechanical comparison.
  • The greatest fruit lies right in givers: mind less stingy, less possessive-clinging, wider.
  • Wise donating: researching recipients and use-purposes — no blind donating.

Glossary:

  • Merit (Pali: puñña): wholesome results of wholesome karma; not mechanical plus-minus "points."
  • Giving (Pali: dāna): the first merit-making ground — including material giving, Dhamma-giving, fearlessness-giving.
  • Virtue (Pali: sīla): the second merit-making ground.
  • Mental cultivation (Pali: bhāvanā): the third merit-making ground.
  • Intention (Pali: cetanā): karma's core — "Intention, I declare, is karma" (AN 6.63).
  • Field of merit (Pali: puññakkhetta): the canonical image — recipients' qualities affecting giving's fruit.
  • Gladness (Pali: pāmojja): the rightly-giving mind — before, during, and after giving.
  • Perfection of generosity (Sanskrit: dāna-pāramitā): the deepest giving-ideal in Mahāyāna Buddhism.
  • Dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda): the ground for understanding wholesome fruits arising — no reward-granting god.
