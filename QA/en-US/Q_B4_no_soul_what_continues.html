Q.B4. If there is no soul, what carries over from one life to the next?

The two previous articles have presented: death is not the full stop of the mental stream (article Q.B3 discussed buddha-nature; article Q.B1 discussed "where we go"; article Q.B2 discussed why Buddhism does not affirm a soul in the popular sense). But that leaves a very natural question: if there is no soul, what carries over from one life to the next? The Buddhist answer may surprise: it may not be about finding some "thing" carried across — but about learning to see continuity in a different way.

1. The short answer

Place three views side by side:

  • "An unchanging soul leaves this body and enters another" → the familiar answer of many cultures, but Buddhism does not use this model.
  • "No soul, so death is total extinction" → the annihilationist view, which Buddhism also does not follow.
  • The middle way: no fixed object needs to be "carried across"; there is continuity of a causal stream — karma (Pali: kamma - intentional action), craving, clinging, mental tendencies, and conditions of the mental stream — conditioning a new life to arise. That continuity is neither entirely one, nor entirely different.

In short: instead of asking "what carries over from one life to another?", the question more in the Buddhist spirit is "which conditions give rise to the next life?". The sections below unfold each step.

2. The first mistake: thinking some "thing" must be carried across

The human mind is used to objects: a ball passing from one hand to another, an item moved from one house to another. So hearing "rebirth from one life to another," we at once picture: something in this life → leaves → enters the next life — and that something is usually called a soul. But this is precisely the assumption the non-self (Pali: anattā - non-self) teaching asks us to re-examine: if an unchanging object is carried across, we have returned to the idea of an independently existing, permanent self. Buddhism needs no such assumption to explain cause and effect.

Two very different statements must be distinguished: "nothing is carried across" and "there is no continuity." Buddhism affirms the first, not the second. A process can continue without an unchanging object traveling from A to B.

3. Continuity without a moving object

The classical images already appearing in article Q.B1 (section 4) apply directly here:

  • Flame lighting flame: no "fire-object" can be found leaving the first candle and flying to the second, yet the second candle does not burn by itself without conditions from the previous flame.
  • Seed and tree: the seed does not "travel" into the tree, yet the tree is not entirely alien to the seed — the seed is one of the tree's conditions.
  • Echo: an echo is not the "original shout" flying to the cliff; but the shout is the condition for the echo to occur.

A dialogue text of the Pāli tradition sums up that relation for the reborn person: neither the same nor different — each stage forms dependent on the previous stage; nothing is carried over "intact" into the later stage. The dialogue's details were already presented in article Q.B1 (section 4).

4. Karma is not a bag carried into the next life

Many picture karma as an invisible bag: "I die; my karma-bag is carried into the new life." An easy image but a misleading one. Karma is not an object; it is the causal relation of deliberate actions together with the tendencies and conditions they create. Nor should karma be spoken of as "a kind of energy" — as if measurable, storable, transmittable through bodies; that is not how the discourses present karma (article G.C4 presents karma in full).

An everyday example: you flare up and speak a harsh sentence; the other person is hurt and reacts; the relationship worsens; the next day, recalling it, anger returns. What "was carried" from yesterday to today? No "yesterday-anger lump" was transported intact into today — but a chain of conditions continued. That is a very everyday way to understand causality: you do not carry karma — you are creating karma, in every moment.

5. What conditions a new life?

If no object travels, what joins two lives? The Pāli Canon has a priceless image: karma is the field, consciousness the seed, craving the moisture — where field, seed, and moisture meet, the sprout of a new life springs up. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 3.76 (Nidāna Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

Each tradition unfolds the details differently:

  • Theravāda Buddhism: the Abhidhamma (Pali: Abhidhamma) system describes that at the near-death moment, mind takes as object a karma, an image of a karma, or an image of the coming destination; and immediately after the death moment, rebirth-linking consciousness (Pali: paṭisandhi-citta - rebirth-linking consciousness) arises — a link in the conditionally-arisen stream, not a traveling soul (details in article Q.B1, section 7).
  • The Yogācāra school in Mahāyāna Buddhism: presents storehouse consciousness (Sanskrit: ālayavijñāna - storehouse consciousness) — as a ground of the mental stream, where seeds (Sanskrit: bīja - seed) are sown and condition later experience. This concept explains why habits and karma "remain" across time — but it is described as a continuously arising-and-ceasing stream, constantly modified by new seeds, not a permanent self (see also previous article - Q.B3, section 3 and the analysis "do not turn mind-stream concepts into a soul" in article Q.B2, section 7). [Source: Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra - Mahāyāna Buddhism]

The common point: these concepts describe the continuity of successive conditions, not a permanent entity.

6. Dependent origination — a round fueled by conditions

The theoretical key to all the above is dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda - dependent origination): "when this exists, that exists; when this arises, that arises; when this ceases, that ceases." The canon unfolds this into the twelve links of dependent origination: ignorance → formations → consciousness → ... → craving → clinging → becoming → birth → aging-and-death. Rebirth, in this view, is not a station to which a soul is herded, but a round fueled by conditions: ignorance (Pali: avijjā - ignorance), craving (Pali: taṇhā - craving), clinging (Pali: upādāna - clinging) and becoming (Pali: bhava - becoming) keep nourishing new arising. When the causes are destroyed, the round is no longer fueled — that is where nirvana appears in the story (article group Q.C). The whole dependent-origination chain is presented in article G.C3.

7. Is memory what carries over?

It should not be understood so. If memory were carried over intact, everyone born would remember old names, old families, old dwellings — yet most have no such memories. In the Buddhist model, memory is not the same as the whole of karma or continuity. A close image: a person who practiced an instrument for thousands of hours then paused for very long — many detailed memories may fade, yet capacities and habits still leave clear traces in their body-and-mind stream. There is no "skill-object" put into a bag — only traces of a process.

Balance requires saying the reverse too: remembering a story of a "past life" does not automatically prove it is a past life. The memory-and-past-lives theme is discussed in articles Q.G2, Q.G4.

8. Is the "next-life me" the same person?

If asking "entirely the same person?" — no. If asking "entirely unrelated?" — no either. The classical formula is: neither entirely one, nor entirely different. Continuity lies in causal relation — not in a soul, not in the body, not in intact memory, not in a name. Continuity does not mean permanence.

And responsibility still stands on this ground: you of ten years ago are not entirely you today, yet the consequences of what they did still belong to that same stream. This stream's actions condition this stream — as the meal you eat nourishes your body; it does not naturally "count" for your neighbor. Because conditionally arisen, transformation is possible: reactions, habits, orientations today can all be retrained — that is why practice is meaningful (article Q.D3 discusses separately: can past-created karma be changed?).

9. Teaching or science?

A clear distinction is needed: what this article presents is an explanation inside the Buddhist worldview — a model of causality and rebirth — not a physically confirmed scientific discovery. There is at present no widely agreed scientific basis for describing karma as a measurable thing or energy transferred between lives. But care is needed in the reverse direction too: "no scientific evidence yet for a mechanism" is not the same as "proven impossible." The thesis "does rebirth truly exist or is it only belief" is discussed separately in article Q.G1. The balanced Buddhist spirit: keep the question open, but do not let the question about the next life steal the opportunity to look into this life.

10. Summary and next steps

  • No "thing" needs to be carried across: no soul, no karma-bag, no invisible energy-packet.
  • There is continuity of a causal stream: karma the field, consciousness the seed, craving the moisture (AN 3.76); in Theravāda, rebirth-linking mind joining two lives; in Yogācāra, storehouse consciousness with seeds — all are arising-and-ceasing processes, not permanent entities.
  • Memory is not carried over intact; continuity carries tendencies and conditions, not all details.
  • Continuity lies in causal relation: neither entirely one, nor entirely different; both responsibility and transformability stand firm on this ground.
  • Instead of "what will I carry into the next life?", the more practical question: "right now, which conditions am I creating for continuity?".

Glossary:

  • Soul: a popular concept in many cultures; this article analyzes it in that sense.
  • Non-self (Pali: anattā): not meaning "does not exist"; meaning that no fixed entity can be found suitable to grasp as the "true self."
  • Rebirth: in Buddhism the continuation of a causal process when conditions suffice, not a soul stepping into a new body.
  • Dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda): "when this exists, that exists; when this ceases, that ceases" — the key to understanding non-self with causality intact.
  • Twelve links of dependent origination (Pali: twelve links of dependent origination): the twelve-link chain — ignorance, formations, consciousness, name-and-form, six sense bases, contact, feeling, craving, clinging, becoming, birth, aging-and-death — describing rebirth's round as a conditionally maintained process.
  • Karma (Pali: kamma): the causal relation of deliberate bodily - verbal - mental action together with the tendencies and results it creates; not an object, not energy.
  • Craving (Pali: taṇhā): in the AN 3.76 image — the "moisture" letting the consciousness-seed sprout on the karma-field.
  • Clinging (Pali: upādāna): the link after craving — the mind grasping and holding on; together with ignorance and craving, the "fuels" maintaining the round of birth.
  • Becoming (Pali: bhava): the link joining clinging with birth.
  • Ignorance (Pali: avijjā): the first link of the dependent-origination chain.
  • Near-death.
  • Rebirth-linking consciousness (Pali: paṭisandhi-citta): in Theravāda Abhidhamma, the first mind of a new life; a link in the conditionally-arisen stream, not a soul.
  • Abhidhamma (Pali: Abhidhamma): the third basket of the Tipiṭaka.
  • Mind-only school (Sanskrit: Yogācāra): the Mahāyāna school presenting storehouse consciousness.
  • Storehouse consciousness (Sanskrit: ālayavijñāna): described as a continuously arising-and-ceasing stream, always changing with new seeds — not a permanent self.
  • Seed (Sanskrit: bīja): the Yogācāra image for tendencies that karma leaves in the mental stream.
  • Memory: distinct from karma; not what is carried over intact between lives.
  • Nirvana (Pali: nibbāna): when ignorance, craving, clinging are destroyed, the round of birth is no longer fueled; discussed in article group Q.C.
