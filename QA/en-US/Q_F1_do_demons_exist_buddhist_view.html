Q.F1. From a Buddhist point of view, do demons and evil spirits really exist?

The Q&A group on Buddhism and theology opens with a question many carry from films, stories, and folk culture: "are demons real?". The question seems to need only yes or no, but the Buddhist teaching gives a subtler answer: yes — if "demons" is understood very broadly as beings (Pali: satta - sentient being) not belonging to humankind; but no — if understood as an "evil-demon" species born only to do evil. The full answer lies in distinguishing each kind of those beings, and in the attitude practitioners should keep toward them.

1. The short answer

Place the two meanings of "demons" side by side, and the answer appears clearly:

  • Broad sense — non-human beings: the discourses clearly speak of kinds such as hungry ghosts (Pali: peta - hungry ghost), yakkha (Pali: yakkha - powerful non-human spirit), devas (Pali: deva - celestial being) and Māra (Pali: Māra - the Evil One). In this sense the answer is yes — and not just one or two scattered stories, but a whole part of the canonical cosmology (section 2).
  • Narrow sense — "evil demons" as an absolutely evil species: a force confronting good on equal footing, born only to do evil and existing forever — this model is not Buddhism's model (sections 5, 8).

What Buddhism does not do is turn everything invisible into evil demons, nor teach people to live in fear. The discourses describe a rich and diverse non-human world — some fierce, some friendly — just as the human world has kind and unkind people. The teaching's focus still returns to karma (Pali: kamma - intentional action), mind, and the path ending suffering.

2. Which non-human beings do the discourses speak of

Theravāda Buddhist discourses, preserved in Pāli, reflect a cosmology far wider than the human world seen with ordinary eyes. Besides humans, the discourses speak of many other being-groups, each with its own place:

  • Peta (hungry ghosts): beings of a rebirth realm, usually described as suffering much deprivation — closest to folk "ghost" images, but not identical (section 3).
  • Yakkha: powerful non-human beings, very diverse in character — some wholesome, some neutral, some fierce (section 4).
  • Deva (devas): heavenly-realm beings, living longer and more blissfully than humans, yet still in rebirth (Pali: saṃsāra - cycle of rebirth).
  • Gandhabba (Pali: gandhabba - celestial musician): a non-human group often mentioned in deva assemblies.
  • Asura (Pali: asura - titan-like being): a powerful non-human group appearing in some realm-classifications; not reducible to "demons."
  • Nāga (Pali: nāga - serpent-being): dragon-formed non-humans mentioned many times in the discourses.

Dīgha Nikāya 20 (Mahāsamaya) describes a great assembly of non-human beings coming to meet the Buddha, listing many yakkha, gandhabba groups and each group's leaders. [Source: Dīgha Nikāya, DN 20 (Mahāsamaya Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism] Such a world was not "inserted" later by folk tales: it is present right in the canonical layer. Article G.C4 presents this cosmology and realms more fully.

This article's main source is the early canonical layer; the presentation keeps a non-sectarian spirit (article G.G2) — traditions may interpret some details differently.

3. Peta — are they folk "ghosts"?

In everyday speech, "ghost" lumps everything from "wandering souls" to "monsters." The discourses separate. First, the dead do not automatically become hungry ghosts. After death, a being is reborn (Pali: punabbhava - rebirth) into whichever realm fits karma: human, deva, animal, peta, or other suffering realms. "Dead person = ghost" is a formula incorrect to the rebirth teaching — article Q.B1 presents these possibilities, and article Q.B4 explains what passes from one life to another.

Second, the discourses describe petas as connected with the living. The Tirokuḍḍa Sutta tells of petas around old houses, doorways, crossroads — awaiting relatives' merit-making for them; when relatives give and dedicate that merit, petas may benefit under suitable conditions. [Source: Tirokuḍḍa Sutta (Khuddakapāṭha, Khp 7) - Theravāda Buddhism] This is the part closest to folk "house-ghost" concepts — yet still permits no conclusion that "every ghost is a peta" or "departed relatives are surely lingering." Questions on offerings and deliverance for the departed are discussed separately in articles Q.H3, Q.H4.

4. Yakkha — not synonymous with "evil"

This is one of the most common misunderstandings. Book 10 of the Saṃyutta Nikāya — an entire book of meetings between the Buddha and yakkhas — shows yakkha characters very differently:

  • Maṇibhadda (SN 10.4) appearing before the Buddha in a dialogue in which the Buddha stresses mindfulness's value — a yakkha approaching by questioning, not harming.
  • Āḷavaka (SN 10.12; also in the Minor Collection, Sn 1.10) initially fierce, threatening the Buddha to answer his questions; after answered with Dhamma, converted to the wholesome path.
  • Suciloma (SN 10.3; also in the Minor Collection, Sn 2.5) approaching the Buddha threateningly, declaring harm unless the Buddha answered.
  • Sivaka (SN 10.8) — the entirely reverse story: the householder Anāthapiṇḍika walking at night through a fearsome place, hearing sounds making him want to turn back; an invisible yakkha named Sivaka spoke up encouraging him onward to meet the Buddha. This yakkha harmed no one — on the contrary helped someone overcome fear.

Those four all belong to "yakkha," yet wholesome — evil — helping — threatening all appear. The discourse-fitting conclusion: yakkha is a kind of being, not a synonym of "evil" — exactly as humankind, comprising kind and unkind people. [Source: Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 10.3, 10.4, 10.8, 10.12 and Sutta Nipāta, Sn 1.10, 2.5 - Theravāda Buddhism]

5. Māra — not Buddhism's "Satan"

Māra is the Canon's most prominent non-human figure, yet also easiest to misunderstand. The discourses permit seeing Māra on two levels:

  • Character level: Māra appears, speaks, seeks to obstruct. For example, in SN 5.3, Māra came before the bhikkhunī (Pali: bhikkhunī - Buddhist nun) Kisāgotamī sitting in meditation, using dreadful words to frighten her out of concentration; she recognized Māra and stood unshaken. [Source: Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 5.3 (Kisāgotamī Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]
  • Symbolic level: in discourses, "Māra" is also used for whatever obstructs liberation — death, craving, clinging. Theravāda commentaries (Pali: aṭṭhakathā - commentaries) further analyze Māra into such meaning-layers.

What matters: Māra is not "Satan." Buddhism has no model of two equal opposing powers — "a god of the good side" and "a demon-king of the evil side" contending to the end. In AN 4.36, asked whether he was a deva, gandhabba, yakkha, or human, the Buddha answered: none — for he had destroyed the taints (Pali: āsava - taints) — the causes giving rise to such births. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 4.36 (Doṇa Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism] The Buddha stands in no "battle line" — he teaches only the suffering-ending path. The God question is discussed separately by the next article - Q.F2.

6. Can demons harm people?

In canonical stories, yes — such descriptions exist. Udāna 4.4 tells: a yakkha, irritated, tried punching venerable Sāriputta hard on the head; venerable Moggallāna — thanks to the divine eye (Pali: dibbacakkhu - divine eye) — clearly saw the event from afar. [Source: Udāna, Ud 4.4 - Theravāda Buddhism] So the discourses do not prettify the non-human world: there truly are violent beings.

But the reverse must not be inferred: not every strange phenomenon comes from demons. A person with fever, headache, insomnia, hearing midnight noises — the discourses permit no leap to "there is a ghost." The Buddhist cosmology shows all phenomena arising from very many causes and conditions; demon-stories are only one possibility among countless, and most everyday cases need no such possibility.

Reading demon-related content, three layers can be separated to avoid confusion:

  • Canonical layer: what scriptures record — peta, yakkha, deva, gandhabba, Māra, great non-human assemblies, dialogues.
  • Traditional layer: over time, traditions — both Theravāda and Mahāyāna Buddhism — developed protective rites, chanting, blessings concerning non-humans.
  • Folk layer: house-ghosts, road-ghosts, tree-ghosts, hungry-ghosts, possession, land-guarding demons... — very rich, but not all of equal value with the Canon.

On "spirits entering the living": the discourses describe non-human influence on humans, but medical and psychological phenomena — epilepsy, sleep disorders, psychological disorders — need examining by each field's fitting methods, rather than defaulting to supernatural causes (article Q.B8 specifically discusses this question).

7. Buddhism does not teach living by fear

Return to the Anāthapiṇḍika story in section 4: the story ends not with "turn back, demons out there," but with encouragement onward. This is a major signal of Buddhism's attitude: fear should not rule practitioners' minds.

Theravāda Buddhism has the protection (Pali: paritta - protective chanting) practice — chanting discourses tradition regards as protective; the Canon records a protection discourse directly connected with yakkhas. [Source: Āṭānāṭiya Sutta (Dīgha Nikāya, DN 32) - Theravāda Buddhism] But do not understand chanting as a "chant and demons flee" formula. Real value lies in the chanter's mind: calm, concentration, wholesome conduct, and especially loving-kindness — wishing all beings well (loving-kindness, Pali: mettā - loving-kindness; article M.D2.1 presents the practice). The Karaṇīya Metta Sutta teaches developing kindness to all beings without exception. [Source: Karaṇīya Metta Sutta (Khuddakapāṭha, Khp 9) - Theravāda Buddhism] Observing how mind reacts to fear — hearing a noise, imagining, then frightening oneself — is a concrete Meditation skill (article M.C2.2).

And here is a very real paradox: suppose late at night hearing a noise, mind at once fabricates, heart pounds, more thinking more fear, sleepless till dawn — what causes most suffering is sometimes no "ghost" at all, but the very fear-chain mind creates itself. The discourses permit believing in invisible beings; yet practitioners are advised against fear-rule — for greed, hatred, delusion are what bind long-term.

8. No permanent "demon-nature"

A major difference between Buddhism and many "evil-demon" models lies here. Buddhism teaches non-self (Pali: anattā - non-self) — no fixed, permanent nature in any being (article G.C5). Apply to non-humans: a yakkha too is born from karma and conditions, lives a term, makes new karma, then reborn onward; they may do good, do evil, hear Dhamma and change — as Āḷavaka followed the wholesome path after meeting the Buddha. No rule says "demons forever demons."

The reverse is also true: a human may make karma leading to rebirth as peta or yakkha. Realms are possibilities in rebirth, not "peoples" of eternally different natures. This view leads to two practice attitudes: first, despising or hating no being — even non-humans; second, no need to "worship to buy forgiveness" from any power — what is needed is keeping precepts, living wholesomely, developing loving-kindness, and non-harming (article group Q.H discusses offerings and deliverance deeply).

9. Summary and next steps

  • Asking "are demons real?" — in the broad sense of non-human beings, the discourses answer yes: peta, yakkha, deva, gandhabba, asura, nāga, Māra are all clearly mentioned in Theravāda discourses.
  • Yet "demons" are not one single evil species: some fierce, some friendly; peta is not every dead person's outcome; yakkha is not synonymous with "evil."
  • Māra is not "Satan": Buddhism has no equal-opposing-powers model; Māra is both character and symbol of what obstructs liberation.
  • The discourses describe non-human harm — but permit no reducing every strange phenomenon to demons; separate three layers: canonical — traditional — folk.
  • Buddhism does not teach living by fear: protection and chanting have value through settled mind, wholesome conduct, and loving-kindness; what most deserves facing remains greed — hatred — delusion within oneself.
  • No permanent "demon-nature": all beings — including non-humans — lie within rebirth and karma, all transformable.

Glossary:

  • Sentient being (Pali: satta): every conscious, feeling living being — humans, animals, devas, non-humans; as distinct from plants — only biological life in the canonical classification.
  • Non-human being: the general term for being-kinds other than humans in the Buddhist cosmology; not synonymous with "evil demons."
  • Hungry ghost (Pali: peta): a rebirth realm of much deprivation and suffering; one of many post-mortem rebirth possibilities — not every dead person's mandatory outcome.
  • Yakkha (Pali: yakkha): powerful non-human beings; in the discourses some wholesome, some neutral, some fierce; some converted after meeting the teaching.
  • Deva (Pali: deva): heavenly-realm beings, living longer and more blissfully than humans yet still in rebirth.
  • Gandhabba (Pali: gandhabba): a non-human group often mentioned in deva assemblies.
  • Asura (Pali: asura): a powerful non-human group in some realm-classifications; not reducible to "demons."
  • Nāga (Pali: nāga): dragon-formed non-humans mentioned many times in the discourses.
  • Māra (Pali: Māra): a non-human figure obstructing the practice path, simultaneously symbolic — death, craving, clinging; not identical with creator-religions' "Satan."
  • Cosmology: in Buddhism — the map of realms and being-kinds; a description of mind-states and lifeworlds, not an astronomical map.
  • Cycle of rebirth (Pali: saṃsāra): birth-and-death continuing per karma and conditions, with many possible realm-kinds (article G.C4).
  • Rebirth (Pali: punabbhava): rebirth after death per karmic conditions; rebirth realm depends on created karma, no fixed single outcome.
  • Karma (Pali: kamma): deliberate bodily - verbal - mental action with its natural results; applying to all beings, including non-humans; "Intention, I declare, is karma" (AN 6.63).
  • Non-self (Pali: anattā): no fixed, permanent "I" or "demon" nature — the ground for understanding why there is no permanent "demon-nature."
  • Taints (Pali: āsava): "flowing" defilements in mind; AN 4.36 says the Buddha destroyed the taints causing births — deva, gandhabba, yakkha, human.
  • Protection (Pali: paritta): chanting discourses tradition regards as protective; real efficacy joins with the chanter's loving-kindness, concentration, and wholesome conduct.
  • Loving-kindness (Pali: mettā): one of the four immeasurables (four mental qualities cultivated as meditation objects); article M.D2.1 of the Meditation section presents the practice.
  • Divine eye (Pali: dibbacakkhu): the capacity from concentrated mind; the discourses use it to describe venerable Moggallāna seeing the yakkha.
  • Bhikkhunī (Pali: bhikkhunī): a female monastic, like Kisāgotamī in SN 5.3.
  • Dedication of merit: offering wholesome deeds' merit to someone; in the Tirokuḍḍa Sutta — relatives dedicating merit to petas.
  • Commentaries (Pali: aṭṭhakathā): explanatory texts later than root discourses; some details (like analyzing Māra into meaning-layers) belong to the commentarial layer.
  • Pāli Canon: the Tripiṭaka in Pāli preserved by Theravāda Buddhism.
