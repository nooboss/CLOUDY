Q.D2. Is all suffering in life caused by one's own karma?

The previous article - Q.D1 presented: karma (Pali: kamma - intentional action) affects life but does not absolutely decide, and not everything comes from karma. This article enters the most sensitive face of the issue: is all suffering in life caused by one's own karma? I am ill — is it from karma? I was betrayed — did I deserve it? A child born into poverty — is that the child's karma? A single "that is your karma" may be spiritual teaching — but it can also become a way of blaming the suffering. Buddhism's compact answer: one cannot simply say all suffering comes from one's own karma — and this article will show why, by the discourses themselves.

1. The short answer

  • No — one cannot say all suffering comes from one's own karma. Suffering has many sources: body, age, germs, environment, accidents, others' actions, social circumstances — and among them, some parts connect with karma, some entirely not.
  • The discourses say this plainly: the Buddha once rejected "all feelings come from old karma" (section 3) — so "all your suffering comes from your karma" is not Buddhist teaching.
  • From a present circumstance, no one can know precisely what one sowed in the past — concluding "this person deserves it" is both groundless and compassionless.
  • And even for whatever suffering part has a karmic face: karma is an operating causal stream, not a punishment verdict — the useful question is not "whose fault?" but "what to do now to suffer less, and what is my mind doing with pain?".

2. Suffering is not synonymous with bad karma

An ill person should not automatically conclude "I am ill from bad karma." An accident victim should not hastily think "this must be a past-life fruit." A betrayed person should not conclude "I deserved this." Such conclusions go beyond what we truly know: life has countless causes and conditions — body, age, microorganisms, environment, accidents, others' actions, social circumstances, one's own choices — and in the rebirth worldview, karmic causes are among them. A painful event does not automatically reveal a person's "karma table."

In other words: one tree does not have a single cause — it needs seed, soil, water, light, temperature, air. Looking at one fruit and pointing straight at a single cause impoverishes the very dependent-origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda - dependent origination) view Buddhism builds.

3. The discourses reject "all suffering comes from old karma"

This is not a modern writer's "situational" reading — it stands ready in the Canon. The wanderer Sīvaka once presented the view: "whatever feeling one feels, all comes from old karma" — and the Buddha answered: that view is incorrect. A sick person's feeling may come from karma, but also from bile disorders, phlegm disorders, wind disorders, imbalance of the three, seasonal change, wrong care, or assault — apart from karmic cause. [Source: Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 36.21 (Sīvaka Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

The Devadaha Sutta goes further: criticizing paths holding "all pleasure-pain comes from old karma," arguing that were it so, purification practice would be meaningless — for with everything pre-decided, action would have no effect. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 101 (Devadaha Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism] These two discourses are canonical "fences" against two errors: taking karma as the answer to everything, and taking karma as fate.

4. The harm-doer's karma — the victim's pain: two different matters

Suppose one person strikes another. The striker: is creating karmic causes from their own action. The struck: is bearing a harming act. There is no need — and no permission — to conclude "the struck deserved striking from karma." Karma is no excuse erasing the harm-doer's responsibility. The violent, deceitful, exploitative remain fully responsible for their behavior, in whatever circumstances the victim "met" them.

Hence "that is your karma" said to one in pain can be very dangerous: it turns a complex issue into too simple an answer, turns victims into the at-fault, and makes speakers imagine they know things no one can actually know. The more compassionate question is not "what did you do in a past life?", but "what can I do to help?" — and for harm-doers, the question is how to stop harming behavior, not "consoling" with causality.

5. Illness, poverty, and children born into hardship

  • Illness: illness may involve genetics, age, infection, environment, nutrition, lifestyle, injury — many concrete biological mechanisms. Karma should not replace medicine: the ill need examination, treatment, and care — that is not "lacking faith," but action fitting present conditions.
  • Poverty: a person may be poor from family circumstances, war, limited education, economy, unequal opportunity, birthplace... Saying "you are poor from bad karma" turns a social issue into a personal moral verdict — not a responsible understanding.
  • Children born into war, poverty, congenital illness, or violent families — the hardest question. In the rebirth worldview, karma may be said to be one part of rebirth conditions (article Q.B1); yet from present circumstances, we cannot know precisely what the child "sowed" — and in any case, morally, what must come first is protecting and helping the child, not speculating about its past lives.

All three examples share one principle: karma explains "deliberate action has consequences," not "every event is punishment." The Canon even records the Buddha himself in old age with back pain, illness, and once a rock-splinter striking his foot — bearing bodily pain with unshaken mind (section 6) — bodily suffering is not automatically "bad retribution" for a wrong life. [Source: Dīgha Nikāya, DN 16 (Mahāparinibbāna Sutta); Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 1.38 (Sakalika Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

6. Two layers of suffering — two arrows

The Canon has a famous image separating suffering's two layers: two arrows. Ordinary people struck by painful feeling suffer doubly — both bodily and mental pain: they grieve, lament, grow bewildered, and thus cling more, dread more. While the instructed — taught the Dhamma — struck by painful feeling bear only one: bodily pain, but not the second mental suffering. [Source: Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 36.6 (Sallatha Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

Put in everyday language: layer one is pain from events — illness, loss, separation, injury; layer two is suffering from mind's clinging and reactions — "why me?", "unacceptable", "I must make them pay", "never trust anyone again." The initial event may last only seconds, yet mind can prolong it for years by rethinking, imagining more, nourishing anger. The second layer can multiply the first manyfold — and that second layer is precisely the part made by oneself, the part practice can transform. Buddhism therefore asks not only "what happened to me?", but: "what am I doing with what happened?"

7. So what does karma really say?

If karma is not the answer to all suffering, what does it teach? Something very concrete: deliberate action has consequences — and consequences first of all in one's own mind. Habitually flaring up: mind flares more easily. Habitually lying: lying grows easier. Habitually forgiving: mind grows less ruled by hatred. This is causality visible right in life, needing no wait for a next life. Karma does not only "make circumstances" — it makes habits, tendencies, the person you are becoming: repeated anger becomes habit; habit becomes character; character leads further choices.

This depth matters far more than using karma to guess circumstances. And karma operates as a causal stream — action creates conditions, conditions birth results, results become new conditions — not a god sitting on high deciding who deserves reward or punishment (article Q.D5 separately discusses "who judges").

8. Luck is also no moral ranking table

The symmetrical face of the "suffering is bad karma" error is "luck is good karma" — and equally wrong in its own way. A rich person is not necessarily "karmically better"; a healthy person not necessarily "more moral"; a successful person not necessarily "more favored." Outer conditions are no moral ranking table. Equating rich = good karma, poor = bad karma turns Buddhism into a very simple reward-punishment system — which it is not.

Judging karmic quality from one moment of life — in either direction — is methodologically wrong: we see one fruit, not the whole field and sowing seasons (article Q.D1, section 4). The classic "why do good people meet misfortune, bad people live in comfort?" needs a longer answer — discussed separately in article Q.I4.

9. Summary and next steps

  • Not all suffering comes from one's own karma: the discourses reject "all feelings from old karma" (SN 36.21; MN 101); suffering has many sources — body, environment, others, society.
  • Suffering is no proof of low morality — and luck no proof of high morality; outer conditions are no karmic ranking table.
  • The harm-doer's karma and the victim's pain are two different matters; karma does not erase the harm-doer's responsibility.
  • Do not use karma to replace medicine or social action; with the suffering — especially children — help first, speculate later.
  • Two layers of suffering (SN 36.6): pain from events and suffering from mind's reactions — the second is the "self-made" part practice transforms.
  • What karma really teaches: deliberate action has consequences — first on the actor's own mind and habits.

Glossary:

  • Karma (Pali: kamma): deliberate bodily - verbal - mental action together with the tendencies and results it creates; neither the answer to every event, nor a verdict.
  • Intention (Pali: cetanā): the core of karma — "Intention, I declare, is karma" (AN 6.63).
  • Feeling (Pali: vedanā): per SN 36.21, feeling may come from karma or from other causes (bodily disorders, weather, wrong care, assault...).
  • Dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda): all phenomena — including pain — have many conditions, not a single cause.
  • Wandering ascetic (Pali: paribbājaka): Sīvaka was a wanderer in the Buddha's time.
  • Two arrows: the SN 36.6 image — the first arrow is bodily pain; the second is mental suffering from clinging and reactions; the instructed bear only the first.
  • Clinging (Pali: upādāna): the source of the "second suffering layer" — mind grasping events and prolonging them.
  • Cycle of rebirth (Pali: saṃsāra).
  • Rebirth: in the rebirth worldview, karma is one part of rebirth conditions — yet no one looking at present circumstances can know past karma precisely.
  • Loving-kindness (Pali: mettā): the right attitude meeting the suffering.
