Q.D3. Can karma created in the past be changed?

"I did wrong in the past — will that karma follow me forever? Can repentance, chanting, merit-making dissolve it?" If karma were an unchanging verdict, people would have almost no hope; but if karma could be erased with one rite, causality would be too easy. Buddhism offers a view subtler than both: the past cannot be fixed in the sense of making it "never happened," but how the past keeps affecting present and future can be transformed — sown causes remain, but the conditions for those causes to ripen are being newly created every day (article Q.D1 laid the ground; this article goes deep into the mechanism).

1. The short answer

  • Created karma (Pali: kamma - intentional action) cannot be "erased" in the sense of making that deed never happened: what happened happened; consequences may already be running.
  • Yet karma's ripening is no mechanical matter apart from conditions: with the same cause, different conditions yield different expressions. In one with virtue, concentration, wisdom — old karma ripens very differently from in one without practice (section 5).
  • Hence the correct answer: the past cannot be erased, but how the past keeps acting can be transformed — by changing oneself and creating a new causal stream. That is the deep meaning of "transforming karma."

2. "Created karma" is no debt lying in a safe

Karma should not be pictured as a debt lying in the universe's safe, or a number written into an invisible account — no one stands outside recording "this person deducted points." Karma concerns intentions, deliberate actions, habits, tendencies — and their consequences. A very everyday example: a person often flaring up, after many times growing readier to flare — repetition itself has created a mind-structure quick to react with hatred. That is already part of the fruit — karma's first fruit always blooms right in the sower's own mind.

This view matters because it shows "changing karma" is first of all not a matter of trading with some power, but changing one's own mind-structure and habits — what each present moment can still take part in.

3. First principle: the past does not become "never happened"

This must be said plainly to stay honest: if you hurt someone, you cannot turn back time; harsh words were spoken; trust was broken; consequences may have formed. "Changing karma" is not erasing history — the past remains past. One who erred yet lives as if "nothing ever happened" is not transforming karma, but evading it.

This principle also protects responsibility's meaning: precisely because done deeds are real, acknowledging, admitting fault, and not repeating gain real value (section 8). And it explains why those who caused harm need patience: some consequences of the past still continue by their momentum — even when the main stream has changed direction.

4. Second principle: fruit does not fall apart from conditions

But inability to erase the past does not mean the future is locked. Think of a sown seed: you cannot make it "never sown," but you can change soil, water, light, environment. Some seeds lack conditions to sprout; some grow weakly; some yield differently by conditions. With the same cause, different conditions yield different expressions — this is precisely dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda - dependent origination), and why karma is not fate.

A simple example: a person once hot-tempered — the past created a proneness to hatred. But they begin practice: each anger, pausing, breathing, observing, not speaking at once. After some years, they may still flare, but no longer ruled by anger as before. The past does not vanish — but the present causal stream has changed, and the old tendency loses "soil" to keep growing.

5. The lump of salt and the river — what the discourses say

This is one of the most important discourses for this question. The Buddha asked: if a lump of salt is thrown into a cup of water, does the cup's water turn salty, undrinkable? — Yes. But if that salt-lump is thrown into the Ganges, does the river turn salty? — No; the river's water remains drinkable. Then he applied it to karma: the same small unwholesome karma, in one undeveloped — without virtue, without wisdom — leads to great fruit, hell-like feeling; but in one developed, regularly meditating, nourishing a vast mind, that karma ripens only small, almost unfelt. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 3.99 (Loṇakapalla Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

The discourse shows the pivotal point: what decides whether old karma ripens great or small is not only the past deed, but the "water" of the present person — virtue, largeness of mind, wisdom. A cup holds a salt-lump; a river does not. We cannot pull the salt-lump out — but we can turn ourselves from cup into river.

6. The Aṅgulimāla story: great karma, small fruit

A canonical story illustrates the same principle at a whole-life scale. Aṅgulimāla was a murderous robber, wearing victims' finger-garlands — nearly a thousand people, the discourse recounts. The Buddha alone met and converted him; he went forth, practiced diligently, and realized arahantship (Pali: arahant - liberated one). But the story does not stop there: going alms-round, villagers still struck him with stones and sticks until his head bled — and the Buddha told him he was experiencing right in this life the fruit of created karma, which would otherwise have borne hell-fruit for many aeons. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 86 (Aṅgulimāla Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

The meaning is very deep: practice does not make the killing "never happened" — that karma still ripens; but in one completely transformed, its fruit changes shape: from multi-aeon hell-fruit into small fruits borne in the final life. The Aṅgulimāla story is the greatest illustration of AN 3.99's formula: old causes unerased, but the new "water" changes the fruit's intensity.

7. Does doing good "erase" bad karma?

It should not be understood by "good deeds − bad deeds = 0" — karma is no addition-subtraction spreadsheet. One good deed does not make a bad deed never happened. But good deeds can: create new wholesome causes, change mind, change habits, create good conditions — and in the karma worldview, help affect how fruit ripens (like adding water to the salt-cup, like nourishing a larger river). Hence: doing good redirects; it does not "bribe" causality.

And a very small wholesome act also matters greatly in its own way: helping one person — they may suffer less; but at the same time one's own mind is practicing giving: next time helping grows easier; after many times, compassion becomes a tendency. Good deeds matter not only in outer results — they are making the person you will become, i.e. making the very "water" into which old salt-lumps will fall.

8. Right repentance — unlike remorse

Repentance (recognizing faults and resolving not to repeat them) has real transforming power — if rightly understood. Repentance is not "I recite a rite, so everything vanishes"; deeper repentance comprises: seeing faults clearly; not deceiving oneself; taking responsibility; stopping repeated behavior; repairing what can be repaired; creating better new causes. The difference from remorse matters greatly: remorse is "if only I had not done it" — then doing it again, with self-reproach only adding pain; repentance is "I see clearly I did wrong" — then changing action. One nourishes self-reproach, the other creates transformation.

Having caused harm, the practical way has four steps: admitting fault (not blaming circumstances); apologizing (without "but because of you..."); repairing (compensating if possible); and most importantly — not repeating (apologizing then doing exactly the same means transformation has not occurred). And one honesty must be added: your repentance does not oblige others to forget — the harmed have the right not yet to forgive, even to keep distance; that is their causal stream. One traditional limit must also be stated plainly: some extremely grave karmas — like the five immediate-retribution deeds (Pali: ānantarīya-kamma - acts with immediate retribution): harming mother, father, an arahant, wounding a Buddha's body, splitting the Saṅgha — are described by traditions as certainly ripening, with no rite replacing; but even in ordinary cases, the right boundary is: rites have value only with real change of mind. [Source: Vinaya and commentarial tradition - Theravāda Buddhism]

9. Summary and next steps

  • The past unerased — done deeds are real; "transforming karma" is not erasing history.
  • Yet fruit does not ripen apart from conditions: with the same cause, in the "river" of virtue - concentration - wisdom, fruit differs greatly from in the "cup" of the undeveloped (AN 3.99).
  • Aṅgulimāla (MN 86): great karma still ripens but changes shape in one completely transformed — from many-aeon hell into small fruits in the final life.
  • Doing good redirects, not bribes: wholesomeness creates a new water-stream and the person you will become.
  • Right repentance: seeing clearly — taking responsibility — apologizing — repairing — not repeating; the harmed have the right not yet to forgive; some extremely grave karmas per tradition still certainly ripen.
  • A sentence to remember: the salt-lump cannot be pulled out, but one can become the river.

Glossary:

  • Karma (Pali: kamma): deliberate bodily - verbal - mental action together with tendencies and consequences; not a material debt stored somewhere.
  • Condition (Pali: paccaya): "changing karma" means changing conditions — the conditions in which old causes will ripen.
  • Dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda): with the same cause, different conditions yield different expressions — the mechanism of karma-transformation.
  • Arahant (Pali: arahant): a noble one who has destroyed all defilements, fully liberated.
  • Virtue - concentration - wisdom (Pali: sīla, samādhi, paññā): the "river" making old karma ripen smaller (AN 3.99).
  • Repentance (recognizing faults and resolving not to repeat them): unlike self-reproaching remorse; comprising seeing clearly, taking responsibility, apologizing, repairing, not repeating.
  • Five immediate-retribution deeds (Pali: ānantarīya-kamma): harming mother, father, an arahant, wounding a Buddha's body, splitting the Saṅgha; per tradition certainly ripening — a Vinaya and commentarial concept.
  • Saṅgha (Pali: saṅgha).
  • Feeling (Pali: vedanā): per AN 3.99, the same karma ripens into "hell-like" or gentle feeling depending on the recipient.
  • Hell realm: a rebirth realm in the Buddhist cosmology, not a prison ruled by a judging lord.
  • Alms-round (Pali: piṇḍapāta): the daily life of monastics in the Buddha's time.
