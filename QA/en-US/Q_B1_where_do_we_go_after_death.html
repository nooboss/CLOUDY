Q.B1. Where does a person actually go after death?

The group of articles on death and the unseen world opens with perhaps humanity's oldest question: when we die, where do we actually go? Many cultures answer with the image of a soul leaving the body and continuing its journey somewhere else. The Buddhist answer is neither simply "death is the end," nor "an intact soul moves to another world." And following the Buddhist way of reflection, this question opens an even deeper issue: if there is no unchanging soul, what continues after death? This article works through that step by step.

1. The short answer

Place three answers side by side, and the Buddhist position becomes clear:

  • "Death is the end; nothing carries over to a later life" → Buddhism does not answer this way. This is called annihilationism (Pali: ucchedavāda - annihilationism) — one of the two extremes the Buddha criticized.
  • "An intact, unchanging soul leaves the body and goes to another place" → Buddhism does not answer this way either. This is called eternalism (Pali: sassatavāda - eternalism) — the other extreme.
  • The middle way: there is continuity of a conditioned body-and-mind stream — like one flame lighting another — but no unchanging entity "moves" from one life to another. The direction of that continued stream is shaped by karma (Pali: kamma - intentional action) and conditions of mind.

In short: the question "where do we go" becomes more useful when reframed as "what is being continued, and through which conditions?". The sections below unfold each step.

2. Death in the Buddhist view — the body disintegrates, but the question does not end

When a person dies, what we see very clearly is the body ceasing to function: breathing stops, the heart stops, the body gradually returns to the material elements of nature. Buddhism does not deny that obvious fact. But Buddhism poses a different question: does the ending of the body mean the complete ending of the mental stream?

To answer, Buddhism analyzes a "person" into the five aggregates (Pali: pañcakkhandha - five aggregates): form (the material body), feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness (Pali: viññāṇa - consciousness). None of these five groups is an unchanging "soul"; all are continuously arising-and-ceasing processes. Looking directly into oneself, one finds no fixed "self" standing behind those processes — this is the teaching of non-self (Pali: anattā - non-self). [Source: Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 22.59 (Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

It must be said at once to avoid misunderstanding: non-self does not mean "you do not exist." At the conventional level, each person still has a name, memories, responsibilities, and relationships. Non-self says that this "self" is more like a flowing stream than a fixed block of matter: it has continuity, but not permanence. This theme is presented in full in article G.C5 (the five aggregates and non-self).

3. If there is no soul, what continues?

This is the hardest question, and the canon records a very telling story. A bhikkhu (Pali: bhikkhu - Buddhist monk) named Sāti held that: "it is this very consciousness that runs and wanders from one life to another, not another consciousness." That is, he pictured consciousness as a fixed lump traveling from one body to another. The Buddha immediately reproved that reasoning: consciousness is no migrant; consciousness arises only when conditions are present — eye-consciousness arises dependent on eye and visible forms, ear-consciousness dependent on ear and sounds. The discourse ends with an image: as fire burns dependent on fuel, when the fire goes out and one asks "did that fire go east, west, south, or north?" the question does not apply. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 38 (Mahātaṇhāsaṅkhaya Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

Yet the discourses also do not teach that "death is a full stop with nothing following." The Buddhist view lies in the middle, and the key is dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda - dependent origination): phenomena arise dependent on conditions, and while conditions continue, phenomena continue in their own way (article G.C3 presents dependent origination in full).

Hence, to the question "who is it that is reborn?", the answer in the spirit of the discourses is: there is no fixed "who" traveling from one life to another; there is a conditioned stream continuing (article Q.E3 goes specifically into this question).

4. An easy image: one flame lighting another

To picture a continuity that needs no "migrant," the discourses use several very everyday images:

  • Candles: use the flame of the first candle to light the second. Is the second flame the same as the first? Not quite. Is it entirely unrelated? Not that either. It appears thanks to the first flame. The continuity of lives is pictured similarly: neither entirely one, nor entirely different.
  • Seed and tree: the seed does not "travel" into the tree. But given soil, water, and light, the tree appears. The tree is not the seed (materially), yet it is not entirely alien to the seed.
  • A bell's sound: a bell rings and its sound travels. One cannot take "the sound at the far end of the hall" and say it is the original bell-stroke intact; but neither can one say it has nothing to do with that stroke. Continuity exists through conditions, needing no unchanging soul standing in the middle.

A famous dialogue text of the Pāli tradition records the conversation between King Milinda and the elder Nāgasena. The king asked: is the one who is reborn the same person or not? The elder answered: neither the same nor different — offering the image of one lamp lighting another, or milk turning into curd and curd into butter: each stage forms dependent on the previous stage, yet nothing is carried over "intact" into the later stage. [Source: Milindapañhā - Theravāda Buddhism]

5. Karma — the condition orienting continuity

Continuity by what, and in which direction? The Buddhist answer: by karma. A discourse sums it up: "Intention, I declare, is karma" — the core of karma is intention (Pali: cetanā - intention), together with the tendencies that deliberate bodily, verbal, and mental actions nourish in the mind. A person who habitually nourishes hatred grows ever readier to flare up; one who habitually nourishes compassion grows ever readier to empathize. Karma is shaping the mental stream right in the present, and that stream is itself the condition for later continuity. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 6.63 (Nibbedhika Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism] This theme is presented in full in article G.C4 (karma, rebirth (Pali: saṃsāra - cycle of rebirth) and the realms).

Hence, instead of asking "where will I go?", a more useful question is: "which conditions am I creating for my own continuity?". The future — put this way — is not a place already waiting, but a direction being formed by how one lives today.

One thing must be stated clearly: karma is not a judge. No power sits on high recording deeds and handing out rewards and punishments. Karma operates by conditional law: deliberate action creates conditions; conditions meeting suitable circumstances yield results — closer to a causal process than to anyone's reward-and-punishment system (article Q.D5 discusses "who judges human beings" separately).

6. The six realms of rebirth — how to understand them correctly

Many Buddhist traditions draw a "map" of the destinations of rebirth. The familiar listing in Mahāyāna Buddhist countries is the six realms:

  • Heavenly realm: the abode of devas (Pali: deva - celestial being) — living far more blissfully than humans, yet still impermanent, still within birth and death.
  • Human realm: the realm we live in — regarded as a rare and precious realm for practice, offering enough ease and enough difficulty for the mind to awaken.
  • Asura realm (Sanskrit: asura - titan): contentious beings full of rivalry.
  • Animal realm: non-human animals.
  • Hungry-ghost realm (Pali: peta - hungry ghost): beings suffering hunger and thirst, hard to satisfy.
  • Hell realm: the realm of extreme suffering.

Two notes for reading this map correctly:

  • Traditions list them differently. The Pāli Canon and commentaries of Theravāda Buddhism usually speak of five destinations (Pali: pañcagati - five destinies) — hell, animal, hungry ghost, human, heaven — without separating the asura realm. Vivid descriptions of suffering realms in the East Asian Mahāyāna tradition can be found in the Kṣitigarbha Sūtra. [Source: Kṣitigarbha Sūtra - Mahāyāna Buddhism]
  • The map is not a fixed railway. The realms present kinds of lives and the kinds of mind that go with them, not "stations" to which some passenger is pushed. Hence, alongside the traditional reading (realms existing according to karma), many teachers also offer a contemplative reading: one ruled by craving is living in a "realm" of craving; one burned by hatred is living in a "realm" full of suffering; one sunk in ignorance is like one locked in a world with no exit. This reading does not replace the traditional understanding, but helps us see that rebirth is not only something after death — it is also happening in every moment of life.

On hell: the teaching does describe realms of suffering, but not to frighten in the style of "one wrong deed guarantees demons drag you to hell." As section 5 presented, karma is no judge — there are only causes and conditions. And what the discourses stress most is: the seeds of those destinations, or of liberation, are being sown in the present mind.

7. What the traditions say about the dying process

If continuity follows karma, then the near-death process is described in some detail by the traditions — and it is precisely here that traditions differ most clearly:

  • Theravāda Buddhism: the Abhidhamma (Pali: Abhidhamma - systematic analysis of mind and matter) system presents that at the moment approaching death, the mind may take one of three kinds of objects: a karma once done, an image connected with that karma, or an image of the coming destination; and immediately after the death moment, rebirth-linking consciousness (Pali: paṭisandhi-citta - rebirth-linking consciousness) arises in continuity — in this model, there is no resting gap between two lives. [Source: Abhidhammattha Saṅgaha (by Ācariya Anuruddha) - Theravāda Buddhism]
  • Vajrayāna Buddhism: describes an intermediate state (Sanskrit: antarābhava - intermediate state) — the time between two lives, during which the mind passes through stages and moves toward rebirth according to karma and guidance received. The best-known text on this theme is the Bardo Thödol. [Source: Bardo Thödol - Vajrayāna Buddhism]
  • In Mahāyāna Buddhist countries — including Vietnam: the practice closest to lay life is Buddha-recitation at the deathbed — relatives and monastics chant to help the dying keep a calm mind, turned toward mindfulness (article Q.H5 discusses separately whether this really helps the dying).

The detailed descriptions differ, but the common point is very clear: karma and state of mind are regarded as never negligible. A life built on virtue, concentration, and wisdom creates entirely different conditions from a life built on greed, hatred, and delusion. Hence, instead of obsessing "where will I go when I die?", the practitioner returns to the question: "right now, where is my mind heading?"

8. Why the Buddha did not answer "exists or not"?

A wandering ascetic (Pali: paribbājaka - wandering ascetic) named Vacchagotta once asked the Buddha questions such as: after death, does the Fully Awakened One still exist, or not exist? The Buddha set these questions aside — in the discourses they are classed among the unanswered questions (Pali: avyākata - unanswered questions). Instead of answering in that style, he led Vacchagotta to look at a flame: fire burns dependent on oil and wick; when oil and wick are exhausted, the fire goes out — to ask "did that flame go east, west, south, or north?" is a misplaced question. Likewise, questions of the form "after death, exists or not?" already carry an assumption: that there is a fixed "I"-entity to exist or not exist — an assumption the teaching of dependent origination does not accept. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 72 (Aggivacchagotta Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

This does not mean Buddhism "evades" the question. It means: the question needs to be put correctly. Instead of "after death, do I exist or not?", the question compatible with the dependent-origination view is: "right now, what is arising? What is being nourished? What is being clung to?" — for this is where human beings can see and transform. A distinction is also needed: the final goal of Buddhism is not "going to a better place after death," but nirvana (Pali: nibbāna - liberation) — discussed separately in article group Q.C; and the Buddha's handling of such questions is further discussed in article Q.F3.

9. Death as teacher — a more useful question

Buddhism has a special practice with this theme: mindfulness of death (Pali: maraṇassati - mindfulness of death) — one of the recollection objects, presented in article M.D3.7.5. The purpose of this practice is not to nourish fear, but to plant wakefulness: knowing life is finite, one learns to order priorities, waste less time on what does not matter, and neglect less what deserves doing.

Death, seen this way, is a strict yet honest teacher:

  • It shows that impermanence (Pali: anicca - impermanence) is no distant idea: "all conditioned things are impermanent" — a truth each person will confirm with their own life. [Source: Dhammapada, verse 277 - Theravāda Buddhism]
  • It exposes what we have clung to. If a whole life accumulates without learning to let go, death poses the question: what remains to hold? If a whole life nourishes resentment, death poses the question: when no time remains to repair, which momentum will that mind follow?
  • It returns us to the present. The question "where will I go when I die?" is exchanged for: "while still alive, where am I heading?" — toward greed or letting go, hatred or compassion, ignorance or understanding, fear or wakefulness. Today's direction of the mental stream is precisely the condition for later continuity.

If reading this far you feel anxious, pause a little: no one must solve the whole mystery of death in one day, and Buddhism does not encourage blind belief out of fear. What can be done at once is to live step by step more kindly, with less hatred, less greed, less clinging, more gratitude and wakefulness — those are precisely what, according to Buddhism, truly travel with a person.

10. Summary and next steps

  • Buddhism answers "where do we go at death" with the middle way: neither death-is-the-end (annihilationism), nor an unchanging soul migrating (eternalism), but continuity of a conditioned body-and-mind stream according to karma.
  • Consciousness is no migrant: consciousness arises only conditionally (MN 38). Continuity is like flame lighting flame, seed and tree: neither entirely one, nor entirely different.
  • Karma orients continuity; karma is no judging lord but a causal process of deliberate actions.
  • The six realms are a map of rebirth's destinations in the familiar presentation; traditions list them differently (Theravāda often speaks of five destinations); alongside the traditional reading there is a contemplative reading of mind.
  • Descriptions of the near-death process differ among traditions; the common point: karma and state of mind are conditions never to be taken lightly.
  • The question "exists or not after death" belongs to the unanswered group: it carries a built-in assumption of a fixed "I." The more useful question: "right now, where is the mind heading?".
  • A compact principle to remember: do not be so busy about where you will go after death — look at where your mind is heading today.

Glossary:

  • Annihilationism (Pali: ucchedavāda): the extreme viewing death as the absolute ending of a person; one of the two extremes the Buddha did not follow.
  • Eternalism (Pali: sassatavāda): the opposite extreme — believing in an eternal entity traveling from one life to another.
  • Non-self (Pali: anattā): not meaning "does not exist"; the "I" has continuity like a flowing stream, but no permanence.
  • Five aggregates (Pali: pañcakkhandha): form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness; all five arise and cease; none is a "soul."
  • Consciousness (Pali: viññāṇa): the knowing activity arising conditionally (eye meeting forms, ear meeting sounds...), not a fixed lump moving from one body to another.
  • Dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda): all phenomena arise dependent on conditions and cease when conditions cease — the key to understanding continuity without a "migrant."
  • Karma (Pali: kamma): intentional bodily - verbal - mental action together with the tendencies and results it creates; "Intention, I declare, is karma" (AN 6.63); not reward and punishment by any lord.
  • Intention (Pali: cetanā): the core of karma — with the same outward result, different intentions mean different karma.
  • Cycle of rebirth (Pali: saṃsāra): the round of birth-and-death continued, fueled by craving, ignorance, and clinging.
  • Rebirth: in Buddhism not "a soul stepping into a new body," but a causal stream continuing when conditions suffice.
  • Rebirth-linking consciousness (Pali: paṭisandhi-citta): in Theravāda Abhidhamma, the first mind of a new life, arising immediately after the death moment.
  • Abhidhamma (Pali: Abhidhamma): the third basket of the Tipiṭaka; concepts such as "rebirth-linking mind" and "death-proximate objects" belong to its commentarial layer.
  • Six realms: heaven, human, asura, animal, hungry ghost, hell — the familiar listing in the East Asian Mahāyāna tradition; Pāli texts and commentaries usually list five destinations (Pali: pañcagati), without separating asuras.
  • Devas (Pali: deva): beings in higher realms; living far more blissfully than humans yet still impermanent, still within birth and death.
  • Asura (Sanskrit: asura): contentious, rivalry-filled beings.
  • Hungry ghost (Pali: peta): beings suffering hunger and thirst, hard to satisfy.
  • Hell realm: a realm of extreme suffering; the teaching describes it as a result of karma, not a prison ruled by a judging lord.
  • Intermediate state (Sanskrit: antarābhava): the period between death and rebirth described by Vajrayāna Buddhism; in the Theravāda model rebirth follows immediately, without this interval.
  • Near-death.
  • Unanswered questions (Pali: avyākata): questions the Buddha set aside, not answering in "yes-no" form — e.g. "is the world eternal," "does the Fully Awakened One exist after death" — regarded as wrongly assumed.
  • Wandering ascetic (Pali: paribbājaka): a common class of free practitioners in the Buddha's time; Vacchagotta was one.
  • Bhikkhu (Pali: bhikkhu): a fully ordained monk under the Pāli Vinaya.
  • Mindfulness of death (Pali: maraṇassati): one of the ten recollection objects; its purpose is to live more wakefully, not to nourish fear.
  • Impermanence (Pali: anicca).
  • Conditioned (Pali: saṅkhata): whatever is formed from conditions — like body, mind, the surrounding world.
  • Nirvana (Pali: nibbāna): the complete ending of the causes keeping rebirth going; the goal of the Buddhist path; not a "heaven for the self."
  • Buddha-recitation: recollecting and chanting the Buddha's name; in Vietnam, at the deathbed usually the name of Amitābha Buddha; a practice helping the dying keep a calm mind turned toward mindfulness.
  • Virtue - concentration - wisdom (Pali: sīla, samādhi, paññā): the three foundational trainings of Buddhism.
