Q.I4. Why do good people still meet with misfortune, while bad people live in comfort?

Perhaps no question torments people about causality more than this. A kind person, harming no one, always helping — may still meet illness, loss, betrayal. Meanwhile, some selfish, deceitful person — still rich, successful, seemingly living very comfortably. Seen from outside, life seems unfair. Causality in Buddhism is no instant reward-punishment system, and what we see at one moment is not necessarily the whole story.

1. The short answer

The "good meet misfortune, bad live comfortably" paradox truly troubles only when attached to three tight assumptions — all three untrue to how the Canon presents karma:

  • The assumption "all suffering comes from those persons' own karma" — the Canon plainly states suffering has many causal sources (section 2); article Q.D2 separately discusses this question.
  • The assumption "fruits must ripen immediately" — karma ripens over time needing conditions, like seeds needing soil and seasons (section 3).
  • The assumption "we can see others' whole karmic streams" — the discourses themselves class karmic-fruit conjecture among unconjecturables (section 5).

Removing these three assumptions, the paradox largely dissolves: good people meeting misfortune are not therefore bad; bad people enjoying advantages prove not their rightness — nor exempt them from still-unripe fruits. Thence the question wholly changes: no longer "when will bad people meet retribution?", but "what am I sowing through how I live?" (section 7).

2. Suffering has not one cause alone

Living well is no suffering-exemption ticket. For life's suffering has not one source alone: a very kind person still has an impermanent (Pali: anicca - impermanence) body; may still meet unkind people; still bears economic circumstances' effects; still cannot control others' choices.

This is no modern interpretation — it has clear canonical ground. When other wanderers taught that all feelings (Pali: vedanā - feeling) come from past karma, the Buddha rebutted stating other causes: bodily disorders, weather-changes, carelessness, others' attacks — besides karma. The discourse concludes: one should not generalize that all feelings come from karma. [Source: Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 36.21 (Sīvaka Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

This has a very important moral consequence: one meeting suffering does not mean they live wrongly. The failing are not necessarily bad; the poor not necessarily lazy; the ill not "receiving retribution." Outer circumstances are no direct measure of inner quality — turning causality into a tool judging the suffering is a misuse (article Q.D5).

3. Karma needs time and conditions to ripen

Picture sowing a tree-seed. One cannot look on day one saying: "No tree yet — so seeds are useless." Seeds need time, soil, water, seasons. Karma (Pali: kamma - intentional action) likewise: one action creates subsequent conditions; those conditions need conditions (Pali: paccaya - condition) converging to manifest as fruits.

The Canon's ancient commentaries analyze karmic fruits by ripening-time: some karma ripening in the present life, some in the next life, some in further lives; and some karma losing ripening-opportunity if the birth-and-death chain ends before conditions suffice. This analysis is the Pāli commentarial system's work, not one single discourse's verbatim. The meaning for this article's question: unseen fruit does not mean no causes — yet one present event cannot be used to peer backward seeking sufferers' "sins," for we know not which causes are ripening (section 5).

And the discourses add patience's reverse dimension: good-doers seeing no good fruits at once growing disappointed still let outer recognition decide good deeds' value (article Q.I2). One kind word may not return from the very helped person — yet it changes one soul, and sometimes that is already a very beautiful fruit.

4. Those enjoying blessings may be sowing unwholesome causes

This is the part many find hardest to accept. Some deceive yet still earn much money, hurt others yet still loved. Looking only at outer results, "causality does not exist" easily seems. But two streams may run parallel: yesterday's fruits and today's causes appearing simultaneously.

Picture one with an account still holding prior money yet today continuously spending wrongly. Their still having money does not prove their spending right. Likewise, one enjoying favorable conditions formed from many prior causes — simultaneously creating new unwholesome causes. The Aṅguttara presents along the same direction: different beings born differently from their own behaviors — habitual killers bearing short-life fruits, killing-abandoners long-life fruits; habitually conceited born into low lines, unconceited into high lines; and at rebirth, each carrying their behaviors and characters. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 135 (Cūḷakammavibhaṅga Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism] Each person is heir to their own karma (article G.C4).

Another "fruit"-kind ordinary eyes hardly see. Looking at bad yet rich persons, we usually see only money, houses, cars, power, images they post online. But we do not see: are they truly peaceful, living in doubt and fear of losing what they have, able to trust anyone. Part of unwholesome living's fruit is quietly forming right in their minds: habitually hating creates a more hate-prone mind; habitually lying creates a life maintaining many concealment-layers; habitually exploiting others creates relations based only on benefit. Those fruits do not await next lives to begin.

5. Do not look at one slice then conclude anyone's karma

The Canon places peering into karmic fruits on the list of unconjecturables (Pali: acinteyya - imponderables): four things that, speculated repeatedly, lead only to vexation and madness — including karmic fruit (Pali: kammavipāka - result of kamma). [Source: AN 4.77 (Acintita Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism] Not that the discourses deny causality — but warn: no one, even very good practitioners, can look at someone then surely declare their karmic stream. We see one slice; not the whole journey. We see exteriors; not interiors. Social media strengthens this further: people show us their lives' most beautiful moments; very few post "I am afraid," "I do not trust those beside me."

Hence, before others' events — accidents, grave illness, separation — the more careful, compassionate view is: "This is a circumstance happening. From this circumstance, how to reduce suffering and create better causes?" — instead of "that is their retribution." The second phrasing is both knowledge-uncertain and further pains the suffering.

6. So why live well?

If living well guarantees no wealth, health, success, love — why live well? The Canon's answer lies elsewhere than outer rewards: living wholesomely is first shaping one's own mind.

Not lying, mind needs less concealing. Not injuring people, we need not live with self-created consequences. Knowing forgiveness, mind grows less past-bound. Knowing giving, heart grows less lack-ruled. If living well only when rewarded, that remains an exchange-form (articles Q.I1 and Q.I2 already discussed) — yet life signs no "I am kind to you so you must be kind back" contract. Goodness's value lies right in goodness changing its doers.

Also distinguish kindness from weakness: living wholesomely does not mean letting others exploit; forgiving does not mean permitting others to keep injuring; non-retaliation does not mean boundarylessness. Goodness walks with wisdom, unlike goodness from self-protection inability. And one memorable thing: seeing others wrong yet successful, choosing wrong ourselves hands our character-deciding power to others. How others live is their choice; which causes I sow is my responsibility.

7. The deeper question: what am I sowing?

From all analyses above, questions can gradually shift:

  • Instead of "Why are bad people comfortable?" → "What am I sowing through how I live?"
  • Instead of "Why do good people like me still suffer?" → "In these circumstances, which goodness can I keep?"
  • Instead of "When will their karma come?" → "Why do I need others' suffering to feel life fair?"

The last is very deep. Sometimes what pains us is not only our own misfortune, but our wanting the world to punish those who hurt us. Yet if our peace depends on others receiving retribution, our minds remain ruled by them. Buddhism offers another direction: replacing envy of others' advantages with sympathetic joy (Pali: muditā - appreciative joy) — and replacing mind-spending on "others' punishment" with caring for causes we sow today (article M.D2.3 on sympathetic-joy mind).

Finally, distinguish past fruits from present causes: created past causes' fruits may lie beyond our choice-capacity — but present causes still lie in each of our thoughts, words, and deeds. That is precisely human freedom's place (article Q.D3 on changing karma, and article Q.E1 on free choice).

8. Summary and next steps

  • Living well is no suffering-exemption ticket: suffering has many causes — body, circumstances, others, weather, carelessness — besides karma (SN 36.21).
  • Karma ripens over time and conditions: unseen fruit does not mean no causes; and present events cannot peer backward into sufferers' "sins."
  • Enjoying advantages proves not right behavior: yesterday's fruits and today's causes run parallel; part of fruits quietly forms right in unwholesome persons' minds.
  • Karmic fruit is unconjecturable (AN 4.77): do not use causality to judge; use it to wakefully watch oneself.
  • The pivotal question: not "when will bad people meet retribution?", but "how can I live so that however life treats me, I still lose neither loving-kindness nor wisdom?".

Glossary:

  • Karma (Pali: kamma): deliberate bodily - verbal - mental action with its natural results; neither fate, nor anyone's judgment, nor an instant reward-punishment system.
  • Condition (Pali: paccaya): seeds needing soil and water; karma needing converging conditions to manifest as fruits.
  • Dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda): the cause — condition network; helping understand why suffering has many sources and fruits do not ripen at once.
  • Feeling (Pali: vedanā): the Sīvaka Sutta uses feeling as example showing not every bodily experience comes from karma.
  • Impermanence (Pali: anicca): aging - ailing bodies, changing relations — suffering-parts no one exempted, even the very good-living.
  • Imponderables (Pali: acinteyya): four things the Canon advises against conjecturing for they lead only to vexation — including karmic fruits.
  • Heirs of their kamma (Pali: kammadāyāda): the Canonical image — each being inheriting their own behavior.
  • Sympathetic joy (Pali: muditā): instead of envy seeing others advantaged; one of the four immeasurables.
  • Wholesome karma (Pali: kusala kamma): wholesome behavior — with wholesome fruits, yet no impermanence-exemption ticket.
  • Kindness vs. weakness: living wholesomely is not synonymous with letting others exploit; goodness needs walking with wisdom and boundaries.
