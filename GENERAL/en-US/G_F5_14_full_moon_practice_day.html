G.F5.14. (Phugpa) Full-Moon Practice Day (Pūrṇimā)
Fifteenth day of each Tibetan month

The 15th of each month — the full moon day (Sanskrit: pūrṇimā - English: full moon) — is in the Tibetan Buddhist calendar an intensified practice day: monastics recite the scriptures and the major precepts; lay followers keep the precepts (the eight precepts for one day and night — according to the transmission of the traditions), recite scriptures, and recite mantras more than on ordinary days. This is a practice rhythm following the phases of the moon — common to all three Buddhist traditions (article G.D3 on the observance days following the lunar phases in the Southern tradition).

1. The full moon in the practice rhythm

Since the time of the Buddha, the full-moon and new-moon days have been the days on which monastics recite the precepts (article G.D3) and lay followers keep intensified precepts (article G.D3 on the observance day). Tibetan Buddhism keeps this rhythm: the 15th of each month is the day of strongest merit in the month (in the traditional understanding) — suitable for wholesome deeds: keeping the precepts, making offerings, and life release (Tibetan: tsetar - English: life release — already explained in article G.F5.5). The Düchen days of the year (G.F5.4, G.F5.6, G.F5.8, G.F5.9) all fall on important full-moon days — this monthly rhythm is the "small version" of the great festivals.

2. What people do on the day

  • At monasteries: reciting scriptures and reciting the precepts; many monasteries organize the taking of the eight precepts (in Tibetan nyungne is the more austere form — fasting — observed in a short retreat - English: nyungne fasting retreat — depending on the lineage); reciting shared prayers.
  • Lay followers: keeping the precepts (no killing — no alcohol... during the day), reciting scriptures and reciting mantras (Sanskrit: mantra - English: mantra), making offerings, and doing wholesome deeds.
  • Common view: the mind on the full-moon day is believed to be clearer and more sensitive — favourable for practice and dedication (the traditional understanding).

3. Three layers when reading the 15th day

  • Root doctrinal layer: the rhythm of the lunar phases — the rhythm of reflection — keeping the precepts; of the same root as the observance days (article G.D3).
  • Shared practice layer: reciting scriptures, keeping the precepts, making offerings, tsetar.
  • Customary layer: cultural customs attached to the full moon (regional festivals); the idea of a "day of strong merit" belongs to the traditional understanding — to be read in the spirit of "intensified practice" rather than superstition (article G.E6).

4. Summary and next step

  • The 15th of each month in the Tibetan Buddhist calendar = the full-moon practice day (Pūrṇimā) — strengthening the precepts — increasing wholesome deeds.
  • The rhythm shared by the three traditions (article G.D3) — the Tibetan version has its own liturgies and rites.

Glossary:

  • Full moon (Sanskrit: pūrṇimā - English: full moon): the full moon.
  • Observance day (Pali: uposatha - English: observance day): the practice day following the lunar phases — see article G.D3.
  • Taking the eight precepts (English: the eight precepts for a day): the eight trainings kept for one day and night by lay people — the observance-day rhythm.
  • Nyungne (in Tibetan - English: nyungne fasting retreat): the short retreat combined with fasting — silence — the rite of thousand-armed thousand-eyed Avalokiteśvara (depending on the lineage) — the austere practice form of Tibetan Buddhism.
  • Tsetar (in Tibetan - English: life release): ransoming and releasing creatures about to be killed — see article G.F5.
  • Mantra (Sanskrit: mantra - English: mantra): the short phrase repeated in practice — see article G.F5.
