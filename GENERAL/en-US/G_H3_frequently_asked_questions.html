1. Becoming a Buddhist and the precepts

1.1. What does it take to become a Buddhist?

The entrance to Buddhism is taking refuge in the Three Jewels (Buddha, Dhamma, Saṅgha) — a short ceremony that can be held at a temple with a monk or nun (article G.A2). On that basis, lay followers commonly undertake the five precepts (five trainings) as a commitment to a wholesome life (article G.C6). You do not need to know many scriptures before taking refuge, and no one demands an instant "change of life" — Buddhism treats taking refuge as the starting point of learning, not a graduation examination.

1.2. May a Buddhist drink alcohol?

The fifth of the five precepts is abstaining from alcohol and intoxicants — alcohol, drugs, and whatever clouds clear awareness (article G.C6). The reason is not that "alcohol is unconditionally evil" but that mindfulness is the tool of the path: whatever dulls mindfulness loosens all the other precepts as well. In practice, lay followers choose their own level of observance — many keep this precept strictly, some keep it only during specific training periods; what matters is understanding the spirit of the precept: keeping the mind clear, without bargaining with oneself. [Source: Sigālovāda Sutta, DN 31 — Theravāda Buddhism] — a discourse for lay followers listing six harms of alcohol: wasting wealth, causing quarrels, harming health, losing good reputation, causing shame, and weakening wisdom; and AN 5.168–175 lists five dangers for one who breaks the precepts — the reverse being five assurances for one who keeps them.

1.3. If I do not eat vegetarian, am I not yet a Buddhist?

No. Eating vegetarian is not a condition for becoming a Buddhist — taking refuge and the precepts are; vegetarianism is strongly encouraged in Mahāyāna Buddhism (article G.E6), and not compulsory in Theravāda Buddhism. Many people read the scriptures, keep the precepts, and do good all their lives without being full-time vegetarians; conversely, eating vegetarian while harbouring resentment and anger toward others is not "training" at all. Article G.E6 clarifies this misunderstanding in detail.

1.4. Is taking refuge in the Three Jewels the same as ordaining?

No. Taking refuge in the Three Jewels means vowing to rely on the Buddha, the Dhamma, and the Saṅgha; one who takes refuge can still live an ordinary lay life, work, and care for family (article G.A2). Ordination is a path of intensive study and practice with the distinct lifestyle and discipline of a monastic. Taking refuge therefore does not mean leaving one's family.

1.5. What are the Three Jewels?

Buddha is the awakened one, Dhamma is the path and teaching that lead to awakening, and Saṅgha is the community of those who practise and preserve the Dhamma. Taking refuge in the Three Jewels is not merely believing in a symbol, but learning from awakening, practising the teaching, and living in a wholesome learning community (article G.A2).

1.6. Are the five precepts absolute, compulsory laws?

The five precepts are understood first of all as five ethical trainings for lay followers, not as a criminal code with external punishment (article G.C6). One who undertakes them should understand each precept clearly, keep them within one's capacity, and see them as a gradual training of body, speech, and mind.

1.7. What should a Buddhist do after breaking a precept?

What matters is not hiding the fault or despairing, but recognizing the mistake, stopping the wrongdoing, repairing the harm where possible, and resolving to practise better. The spirit of keeping the precepts is to transform conduct and mind, not to turn a single lapse into a feeling of "I am no longer a Buddhist".

1.8. Must a Buddhist visit the temple regularly?

There is no general rule fixing how often a Buddhist must visit the temple. What matters more is studying the Dhamma, keeping the precepts, and practising regularly; the temple is a very supportive environment for doing so. Busy people can still practise at home with a small but regular routine (article G.E6).

1.9. Does Buddhism encourage doing good deeds?

Yes. Doing good is an important part of a Buddhist's life, but Buddhism does not stop at "accumulating merit": the learner should also observe motives, speech, and mind in order to reduce greed, hatred, and delusion. A good deed done with loving-kindness and mindfulness thus both helps others and becomes an opportunity to train oneself.

2. At the temple

2.1. How do I bow when visiting a temple?

The simplest steps: join the palms (both hands pressed together before the chest), stand before the Buddha altar, kneel and bow three times — each bow lowering the body until both hands touch the ground — then stand up. The three bows are commonly understood as bowing to the Buddha, the Dhamma, and the Saṅgha. You do not need a technically "perfect" bow to be sincere; sincerity and a settled mind are what count, and monks and nuns are always willing to guide newcomers (article G.E6 — "newcomers joining a ceremony — no need to fear mistakes"). One common courtesy: do not walk across in front of someone who is bowing.

2.2. How many incense sticks should I offer?

The common custom: one stick (one pure mind), three sticks (Buddha, Dhamma, Saṅgha), or five sticks (depending on the temple and occasion). The scriptures prescribe no number of sticks — incense is simply an expression of sincerity, and the word "fragrance" in the scriptures also carries a symbolic meaning: the fragrance of virtue (the scent of a life of good conduct) spreads farther than any scent (article G.C6). Use little incense in a ventilated room — safe and true to the spirit of "the mind is what counts". This belongs to the level of custom, not scriptural command (editorial note 3).

2.3. What do we ask for when bowing to the Buddha?

Many people come to the temple hoping for health or success at work — and temples receive such wishes with compassion. But Buddhist teaching does not present the Buddha as granting blessings in exchange for offerings: bowing to the Buddha is first of all an expression of reverence and reminds us to turn toward the Buddha's qualities — wisdom, compassion, and purity (article G.A1). Practices of "blessing and memorial ceremonies" in temples are rightly understood through faith, aspiration, and practice: faith and vows must go together with one's own actions (article G.E6). As teachers often say: the Buddha points the way; the steps are ours to take.

2.4. What should I wear when visiting a temple?

Choose clothing that is clean, modest, and simple, suited to a solemn space. You do not need a special outfit to enter a temple; what matters is keeping a respectful attitude and avoiding dress that disturbs the dignity of the place.

2.5. Does a newcomer need to know the rituals beforehand?

You do not need to know all the rituals before entering a temple. A newcomer can observe those around, join the palms, keep silent when needed, and ask a monk, nun, or attendant when unsure. The guiding spirit is learning gradually with reverence, not fearing a small mistake in gesture.

2.6. May I take photographs inside a temple?

This depends on each temple and each area. Check the notice boards, ask the person in charge, and especially avoid photographing during ceremonies, meditation, or chanting, or when photography would disturb others. Courtesy and respect for the practice space matter more than getting a photograph.

2.7. Why are flowers, fruit, and water placed on the Buddha altar?

Flowers, fruit, water, and other offerings generally carry a commemorative and symbolic meaning, reminding practitioners to cultivate wholesome qualities. They are not goods used to "buy" blessings. A simple offering made with a reverent mind therefore means more than an elaborate one without practice.

2.8. Is donation money in temples money that buys merit?

Offerings and donations should not be seen as a transaction of "giving money in exchange for luck". If given for the right purpose, within one's means, and with a mind of supporting the Three Jewels, giving can be a form of sharing and practising generosity. The value of the act also depends on the mind behind it and on how the resources are used, not only on the amount.

2.9. May I enter a temple if I am not a Buddhist?

Usually, those who have not taken refuge may still visit a temple to look around, pay respects to the Buddha, listen to the Dhamma, or join activities open to the community. You do not need to call yourself a Buddhist to begin learning. A temple visit can simply be a first step for learning and observing.

3. Concepts

3.1. How do Buddhas and bodhisattvas differ?

A Buddha is one fully awakened who has completed the path; a bodhisattva (Pali: bodhisatta, Sanskrit: bodhisattva - enlightenment being) is one who vows to bring all beings to awakening together — still walking the path, or nearly at the summit yet delaying the final step out of great vow (article G.E2). In Mahāyāna temples, bodhisattvas such as Avalokiteśvara and Kṣitigarbha are honoured almost like Buddhas — because of their great vows; but strictly speaking: the Buddha is the summit, the bodhisattva is one on the way who stays behind for the sake of beings. Theravāda Buddhism does not emphasize bodhisattva imagery, but the bodhisattva ideal is still present in the Pali canon (Jātaka — article G.G1).

3.2. Are Amitābha Buddha and Śākyamuni Buddha one or two Buddhas?

Two different Buddhas, but "many Buddhas" does not mean polytheism. Śākyamuni Buddha is the historical Buddha — born at Kapilavatthu, the ancient capital of the Sakya kingdom, awakened and teaching in India; Amitābha Buddha (Sanskrit: Amitābha - Infinite Light) is the Buddha of the Land of Bliss according to the Pure Land sūtras — the focus of Buddha-recitation practice (article G.E4). Mahāyāna Buddhism understands Buddha-fields as different "fields of awakening" within an interdependent universe, not competing gods (article G.E2 — the three bodies of a Buddha).

3.3. What is karma? Is everything that happens due to past karma?

Karma is first of all intentional action through body, speech, and mind; such actions contribute to corresponding results in the stream of life (article G.C4). Karma should not be turned into fatalism, as if everything in the present were decided by the past. Buddhism also emphasizes present conditions, how we act today, and the possibility of transformation.

3.4. What does cause and effect mean in Buddhism?

Cause and effect is a shorthand for the relationship between causes, conditions, and results. A result usually does not come from a single cause alone, so one cannot look at an event and simply conclude that "this person is suffering a result because of some past deed". The more practical approach is to attend to the causes and conditions we are creating right now in the present (article G.C4).

3.5. Does not-self mean "there is no self"?

It should not be understood so simply. Not-self means seeing that the "self" is not an independent, unchanging, self-existing entity; body, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness all arise from many conditions and constantly change. Not-self therefore does not deny life or responsibility, but helps the learner cling less to a fixed self.

3.6. What is rebirth?

Rebirth refers to the continuing stream of arising and ceasing within cyclic existence, shaped by karma and by mental and existential conditions. Buddhist traditions explain the details of this process in somewhat different ways, but the common point is that this continuity is bound up with ignorance and craving; the goal of practice is the ending of suffering and liberation.

3.7. Is nirvāṇa a place to go to?

Nirvāṇa should not be pictured simply as a location somewhere. Nirvāṇa (Pali: nibbāna, Sanskrit: nirvāṇa) is the term for the ending of greed, hatred, and delusion and liberation from suffering through the path of awakening. The concrete descriptions may differ among traditions, but nirvāṇa is not a "paradise" in the ordinary sense.

3.8. Is Buddhism only about meditation?

No. Meditation is an important part, but the Buddhist path also includes virtue, concentration, and wisdom, together with studying the Dhamma, developing compassion, and transforming one's way of life. Some practise through meditation, others through Buddha-recitation, chanting, or serving the community; what matters is that these practices lead toward less suffering and greater wisdom.

4. Scriptures and study

4.1. Which sūtra should I read first?

The most common choices: the Dhammapada (423 verses, readable at once), the Heart Sūtra (260 words — prepare a little on emptiness first), or short selections from the Middle-Length and Numbered Discourses in Ven. Thích Minh Châu's Vietnamese translation. The full list with difficulty levels and a study-oriented path is in article G.H1; if you have not yet chosen a tradition, article G.A4 helps orient you.

4.2. Can I practise as a lay follower at home?

Yes — and this is the form of most Buddhists in every age. Four elements of a lay life of practice: taking refuge, keeping the precepts at the level you commit to, studying regularly (scriptures or talks), and one small but daily practice — a few minutes of mindfulness, Buddha-recitation, or chanting a short sūtra (articles G.A2, G.E6). Ordination is an intensive path, not a "higher" path meant for everyone — each life has its own circumstances (editorial note 1 — no ranking).

4.3. How do sūtras, discipline texts, and treatises differ?

Simply put: sūtras are discourses transmitted as teachings; discipline (Vinaya) texts focus on the principles and discipline of the monastic community; and treatises (Abhidhamma/śāstra) are works that systematize, analyse, and explain the teaching. The exact classifications and lists differ among Buddhist traditions.

4.4. How do Pali and Sanskrit differ?

Pali is the canonical language especially associated with the Theravāda tradition, while Sanskrit plays a major role in many canonical and scholarly sources of Buddhism, especially in the Mahāyāna traditions. Many Buddhist terms have closely related Pali and Sanskrit forms, though not fully identical in spelling and pronunciation.

4.5. Must I read the whole Tripiṭaka from the start?

No. The Tripiṭaka is vast, and beginners are easily overwhelmed if they try to read it all at once. Start from one short sūtra, one introductory anthology, or one systematic explanation, then gradually expand by topic and by the tradition you are studying (article G.H1).

4.6. May I read the scriptures in Vietnamese translation?

Yes. Reading a Vietnamese translation is very suitable for beginners, provided you choose a translation with a clear source and read it in an appropriate context. When studying more deeply, you can compare Pali or Sanskrit terms to understand words that are hard to translate or passages open to several readings.

4.7. Why does the same Buddhist story have many versions?

Buddhism has many traditions, canons, languages, and transmission histories, so some stories or interpretations may not be fully identical. When you meet differences, consider which tradition, which text, and which context a source comes from, rather than hastily concluding that one version is "wrong" merely because it differs from the one you know.

4.8. Must I memorize many scriptures to learn Buddhism?

No. Memorizing scriptures can support recollection and practice, but understanding and living by the teaching is the more important goal. One short passage rightly understood and applied to speech, action, and view can be more helpful than remembering many verses without practising.

5. Customs and culture

5.1. What does the number 108 mean? Does Buddhism have "lucky numbers"?

108 is grounded in the teaching: the tradition counts 108 afflictions (mental states that cause suffering — Sanskrit: kleśa - afflictions) — hence the 108-bead rosary used for counting recitations (articles G.E6, G.F2). Other "beautiful number" customs — choosing auspicious dates, avoiding the number 4, seeking the number 9 — are folk culture meeting temple life, not doctrine: the scriptures teach no "lucky" number. Buddhism looks at what is substantial: a good deed is wholesome whenever it is done; and true "luck" is the wholesome conditions we sow (article G.C4).

5.2. Are star worship and star-offering rituals Buddhism?

No — star worship for warding off bad fortune belongs to the folk-astrological system, not to the Buddhist scriptures (editorial note 3: layering scriptures versus customs). Some temples offer star rituals in response to devotees' needs; learners should know its level clearly: it is East Asian cultural custom meeting the modern Saṅgha, not the Buddha's teaching. The doctrinal spirit offers a replacement: the karma we sow — good deeds are the true "amulet" (articles G.C4, and the "amulet" misunderstanding addressed in article G.A1).

5.3. What are "Buddha commemoration days" and "god of wealth days"?

"Commemoration day" is a Vietnamese folk term for the anniversary of a revered figure or sacred power. "Buddha commemoration days" — birth, awakening, and final passing anniversaries, and full- and new-moon observance days — are where Vietnamese custom meets the real festival calendar of Buddhism (article G.E7); "god of wealth days" belong to folk belief, not Buddhism. The rule for reading them: look at the content of the day — whether it connects with the teaching (remembering the Buddha, practising) or only with rituals for wealth and fortune.

5.4. Is animal release a compulsory practice in Buddhism?

There is no general requirement that every Buddhist must organize animal releases. The intention to protect life and nurture compassion accords with the spirit of non-killing, but the method must avoid causing further harm to animals or creating a cycle of capture, purchase, and release. Doing good must therefore go together with understanding and care for consequences.

5.5. Is burning votive paper a Buddhist teaching?

Burning votive paper is a folk custom common in some East Asian cultural regions, not a teaching of Buddhism. Some temples or families still maintain certain traditional rites, but not every worship custom should be identified with the teaching in the scriptures.

5.6. Must I eat vegetarian on full-moon and new-moon days?

There is no general rule binding all Buddhists to eat vegetarian on every full- or new-moon day. Eating vegetarian daily, periodically, or on specific days depends on tradition, family, and each person's vow. If you eat vegetarian, see it as an opportunity to nurture compassion and restraint, not merely as a "taboo day".

5.7. Does Buddhism teach praying for wealth and fortune?

In cultural life, many people come to temples hoping for peace, smooth livelihood, or family prosperity. The teaching, however, does not present paying respects to the Buddha as a transaction exchanging offerings for wealth. Closer to the teaching is the spirit of creating wholesome causes, working honestly, keeping the precepts, and developing wisdom.

5.8. Is a Buddha commemoration day a day when the Buddha "descends to grant blessings"?

Commemoration days in Buddhist life are usually occasions for remembering important events connected with the Buddha or with Buddhas and bodhisattvas, depending on the tradition (article G.E7). One may use the occasion for increased homage, chanting, or good deeds, but should not understand simplistically that only that single day carries special power to exchange for merit.

5.9. Why can each temple have different rituals?

Buddhism has passed through many traditions, cultures, and histories, so rituals may differ in bowing, chanting, incense offering, music, or festivals. A difference in ritual does not necessarily mean a wholly different goal of practice. When attending a new place, the best approach is to respect the local ritual and learn about its origins.

6. Summary and next steps

Glossary:

  • Taking refuge in the Three Jewels (Buddha, Dhamma, Saṅgha): the entrance ceremony — declaring reliance on the awakened one, his teaching, and the practice community.
  • Five precepts (five trainings): not killing, not taking what is not given, no sexual misconduct, no false speech, no alcohol or intoxicants.
  • The fifth precept — "abstaining from intoxicants" (Pali: surāmerayamajjapamādaṭṭhānā - abstaining from intoxicants): not using what clouds clear awareness — keeping the mind clear.
  • Bodhisattva (Pali: bodhisatta, Sanskrit: bodhisattva - enlightenment being): one who vows to bring all beings to awakening together — the central ideal of Mahāyāna Buddhism.
  • Amitābha (Sanskrit: Amitābha - Infinite Light; also Amitāyus — Infinite Life): the Buddha of the Land of Bliss in the Pure Land sūtras — the focus of Buddha-recitation practice.
  • Afflictions (Sanskrit: kleśa - afflictions): mental states that cause suffering — greed, hatred, delusion and their branches; the tradition counts 108 afflictions — the basis of the 108-bead rosary.
  • Faith – aspiration – practice: the three axes of the Pure Land path — faith, vows, and action must go together (article G.E6).
  • Fragrance of virtue: a symbolic image — the "scent" of a life of good conduct spreading farther than any burning incense; the inspiration behind incense offering.
  • Star worship and star offering: a folk-astrological system linking birth years with "good" and "bad" stars; absent from the Buddhist scriptures — the custom level.
  • Commemoration day: a Vietnamese folk term for the anniversary of a revered power; "Buddha commemoration days" are where custom meets real Buddhist festivals.
  • Dhammapada (Pali: Dhammapada): 423 verses — the most common starting scripture.
  • Kapilavatthu: the city-state where Prince Siddhattha was born — now on the Nepal–India border (article G.B1).
