G.D5.31. Cambodia: Chenh Vossa — the Invitation Ceremony
Full moon of the eleventh Buddhist month

Chenh Vossa (Khmer: “leaving the rains”) marks the end of the rains retreat. Its monastic core is Pavāraṇā, the Invitation Ceremony, in which monastics invite one another to point out faults after three months of living together. Its core meaning is explained in G.D5.6, in the Vietnamese calendar group. In Cambodia it falls on the full moon of the eleventh Buddhist month, completing the three months since Chol Vossa (G.D5.30).

1. What is the full moon of the eleventh Buddhist month?

The full moon of the eleventh Buddhist month closes the three-month rains retreat. Thailand, Laos, and Cambodia record this as the full moon of the eleventh month; Vietnam records the same occasion as the fifteenth day of the ninth lunar month (G.D5.6). It is the same full moon expressed through different calendar notations (G.D5.1). The following day opens Cambodia's robe-offering season (G.D5.32).

2. Chenh Vossa in Cambodia

Three layers for understanding Chenh Vossa in Cambodia

  • Foundational meaning: the Pavāraṇā ceremony, in which monastics invite mutual correction (G.D5.6); this core is the same throughout Theravāda countries.
  • Shared practice: laypeople visit the temple, make offerings, and hear the Dhamma, while monastics complete the rains retreat and conduct the Invitation Ceremony.
  • Custom: Chenh Vossa closes Cambodia's major festival season. It follows Bon Phchum Ben, the rains-retreat festival of offerings and merit dedication for ancestors, and is followed by the robe-offering season (G.D5.32) and late-rains public festivals such as the Water Festival, Bon Om Touk. These activities belong to the cultural and social layer.

As noted in G.D5.6, Pavāraṇā is an internal monastic procedure; laypeople participate through offerings and Dhamma listening. For Khmer communities in southern Vietnam, Chenh Vossa opens the sequence “leaving the rains → robe offering → Ok Om Bok,” introduced in G.D5.6 and G.D4.

3. What newcomers should know

  • If you attend at a temple, you may hear monastics candidly point out one another's faults during Pavāraṇā. This is the ceremony's core (G.D5.6).
  • It is a good time to prepare for the kaṭhina robe-offering season, which begins the following day (G.D5.32).
  • For the spirit of learning without anxiety about mistakes, see G.E6.

4. Summary and next steps

  • Chenh Vossa marks the end of Cambodia's rains retreat on the full moon of the eleventh Buddhist month; its Pavāraṇā core is explained in G.D5.
  • It closes the major Khmer festival season: Bon Phchum Ben precedes it, while the robe-offering season and Water Festival follow.

Next: G.D5.32 introduces Cambodia's kaṭhina robe-offering season, the one-month period from the eleventh-month full moon to the twelfth-month full moon. See G.D5.7 for its core meaning.

Glossary:

  • Chenh Vossa (Khmer): “leaving the rains,” the Khmer name for the end of the rains retreat; Thailand calls it Ok Phansa (G.D5.13) and Laos Boun Ork Phansa (G.D5.21).
  • Pavāraṇā (Pali): the Invitation Ceremony that ends the rains retreat, when monastics invite others to point out faults (G.D5.6).
  • Bon Phchum Ben (Khmer): a rains-retreat festival of offerings and merit dedication for ancestors; a customary observance introduced in G.D5.
  • Bon Om Touk (Khmer): Cambodia's Water Festival, a major late-rains public festival associated with the eleventh- and twelfth-month full moons.
  • Completed the rains retreat: having resided for the full rains retreat, a condition for post-retreat privileges (G.D5.6, G.D5.7).
  • Buddhist Era (BE): a calendar era traditionally reckoned from the Buddha's final passing.

Source notes

  • The core Pavāraṇā ceremony is presented fully in G.D5.6, with its corresponding sources (Vinaya Piṭaka, Mahāvagga - Theravāda Buddhism).
  • The name Chenh Vossa and the seasonal sequence around the end of the retreat are described in accounts of Cambodian Buddhism; customary and cultural layers are distinguished from the foundational teaching.
  • The full moon of the eleventh Buddhist month is the standard festival-date notation used in this subsection; it is not converted into a fixed Gregorian date, which varies by year.
