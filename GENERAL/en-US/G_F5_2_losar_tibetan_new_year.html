G.F5.2. (Phugpa) Losar — Tibetan New Year
First day of the first Tibetan month

Losar (Tibetan: Losar, "lo" is year, "sar" is new — Tibetan New Year) is the New Year of the Tibetan calendar, falling on the first day of the first Tibetan month — usually one to two months later than Vietnamese–Chinese Lunar New Year (article G.E7.1 on calendar frameworks). Losar opens the whole festival year of group G.F5; immediately after Losar come the fifteen days of Miracles (next article — G.F5.3).

1. What is Losar

Losar is the New Year of the Tibetan calendar — the greatest social festival of Tibetans, Bhutanese, and some Himalayan communities within the world of Vajrayāna Buddhism. Its full historical name is Nava-saṃvatsara (Sanskrit: nava-saṃvatsara — new year) — reflecting the Indian origin of calendrical ideas. Main activities: cleaning houses, decorating, increasing offerings to monasteries, chanting New Year aspiration prayers, and visiting family and friends. A few days before Losar comes Gutor (article G.F5.11) — the cleansing of the old year.

2. Losar at monasteries and at home

  • At monasteries: New Year aspiration chanting sessions; many monasteries hold the cham dance (Tibetan: cham — masked ritual dance) — a didactic dance; and large offering ceremonies opening the year of study and practice.
  • At home: preparing traditional feasts, offering khapse (Tibetan: khapse — distinctive fried pastries of Tibetan New Year); hanging new prayer flags (the customary layer — see article G.F2 on prayer flags); greeting one another with "Losar Tashi Delek" (auspicious New Year greetings).

3. Three layers for understanding Losar

  • Doctrinal–practice layer: opening the year with offerings, aspiration chanting, and renewed commitment to practice; the spirit of reviewing the old year to correct oneself — like the year-end dedication–repentance spirit of other traditions.
  • Shared practice: chanting, prostrations, offerings to monasteries, and observing short precepts at New Year.
  • Custom: khapse, new prayer flags, cham dance, and gatherings with greetings — Tibetan–Himalayan culture.

4. What newcomers should know

  • Losar is not a purely religious day: it is the cultural New Year of the whole community — Buddhism is central but not everything.
  • Tibetan years are counted by a twelve-animal cycle (like the East Asian zodiac) and a sixty-year cycle; each year has its own name (for example, "Wood Snake year").
  • For the spirit of learning without anxiety about mistakes, see G.E6.

Glossary:

  • Losar (Tibetan, "lo" is year, "sar" is new — new year — Tibetan New Year): the New Year of the Tibetan calendar.
  • Nava-saṃvatsara (Sanskrit: nava-saṃvatsara — new year): the original Sanskrit name of the New Year — reflecting the Indian origin of the calendar.
  • Cham (Tibetan — cham dance): the masked ritual dance — performed at monasteries on major festivals, with a didactic purpose.
  • Khapse (Tibetan — khapse): distinctive fried pastries — offered and served to guests at Losar.
  • Tashi Delek (Tibetan — auspicious greetings): words of auspicious greeting — the common Tibetan salutation, widespread at Losar.
  • Sixty-year cycle (the sixty-year cycle): the year-counting cycle of the Tibetan calendar — combining the twelve animals and the five elements.
