G.F5.3. (Phugpa) Fifteen Days of Miracles (Prātihārya)
First to fifteenth days of the first Tibetan month

The Fifteen Days of Miracles — miracle display (Sanskrit: prātihārya) — is the festive season lasting from the first to the fifteenth day of the first Tibetan month — beginning right after the Losar New Year (previous article — G.F5.2). These fifteen days commemorate the period when the Buddha displayed miracles at Shravasti to win the miracle contest with non-Buddhist teachers, according to the traditional account of the Tibetan tradition (article G.F5.4 on the main feast day).

1. Core event — the miracle contest at Shravasti

According to the popular account in the Mahayana sutras and the Tibetan tradition: at Shravasti (ancient capital of Kosala — Pali: Sāvatthī, Sanskrit: Śrāvastī), the Buddha took part in a miracle contest with six non-Buddhist teachers (in the Tibetan telling often called the "six heretical teachers"). Over fifteen days, each day the Buddha displayed a different miracle — manifesting supernormal powers, teaching the Dharma, and emanating images to tame beings; as a result, the non-Buddhist teachers were defeated and many took refuge in the teachings. The final day — the fifteenth — is the climax, becoming the Great Miracle Festival (Chotrul Düchen) (next article — G.F5.4). [Source: the account of the miracle contest at Shravasti — the sutra collections and commentaries of the Tibetan tradition — Vajrayana Buddhism]

2. What people practise during the fifteen days

Because the whole fifteen-day season is regarded as an occasion of multiplied merit, devotees use it to perform wholesome deeds:

  • Sutra chanting — mantra recitation (Sanskrit: mantra) more than on ordinary days; many monasteries hold continuous chanting.
  • Lamp offering (the rite of offering lamps — light symbolises wisdom, see article G.E7.25); the Miracle season falls in the Tibetan winter — the image of thousands of butter lamps glowing in the monasteries.
  • Offerings and life release (Tibetan: tsetar, ransoming and releasing creatures about to be killed — a compassionate action common in Tibetan Buddhism).
  • Prayers for peace — well-being for the New Year.

3. Three layers for reading the Miracle season

  • Foundational teaching layer: commemorating the period when the Buddha tamed beings through supernormal powers and wisdom — the true strength of the Enlightened One is the Dharma, not miracles (see this understanding in article G.F5.4).
  • Shared practice layer: sutra chanting, mantra recitation, lamp offerings, giving, and tsetar — on the occasion of multiplied merit.
  • Custom layer: the festive season tied to the Tibetan New Year — festivities, butter lamps, and public chanting assemblies.

4. Summary and next steps

  • The Fifteen Days of Miracles = 1st → 15th/01 of the Tibetan Buddhist calendar — right after Losar; commemorating the miracle contest at Shravasti.
  • The 15th is the climax — the Great Miracle Festival (Chotrul Düchen), one of the four Düchen days (article G.F5.1, section 3).

Next: the following article — G.F5.4 — is the Great Miracle Festival (Chotrul Düchen) — the 15th/01 of the Tibetan Buddhist calendar.

Glossary:

  • Miracle display (Sanskrit: prātihārya): miraculous display — the wondrous manifestation of the Enlightened One for teaching beings.
  • Shravasti (ancient capital of Kosala — Pali: Sāvatthī, Sanskrit: Śrāvastī): the city where the Buddha spent many rains retreats and — according to the Tibetan account — displayed miracles to win the contest.
  • Mantra (Sanskrit: mantra): a short phrase repeated in practice — see article G.F5.
  • Butter lamps (made with yak butter for candles): lamps fuelled by yak butter — a familiar offering of Himalayan monasteries.
  • Life release (Tibetan: tsetar): ransoming and releasing beings about to be killed — a compassionate action common in Tibetan Buddhism.

Source notes

  • Tsetar and the custom of lamp offerings in the festive season — the practice layer — customs of the Tibetan tradition.
  • The festival dates 01/01 – 15/01 of the Tibetan Buddhist calendar — the standard festival calendar notation of this subsection (Phugpa system); not converted into a fixed Gregorian date for every year.
