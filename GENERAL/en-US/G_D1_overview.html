G.D1. Overview

The shared foundational teachings — teachings accepted across all three major traditions — ended with the previous article, G.C7. From here, the next three groups sketch the three major traditions of Buddhism today (groups G.D/G.E/G.F), and this article begins with the first: Theravāda Buddhism — the tradition that preserves its complete canon in Pali and is especially widespread in Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand, Laos, and Cambodia, along with Theravāda communities in Vietnam. The article has two parts: first, a general portrait — what Theravāda means, where it is found, and its four identifying features; second, a historical timeline of more than twenty-two centuries — from Sri Lanka in the 3rd century BCE to global Theravāda today.

1. Theravāda — an overview

1.1. The name: "the teaching of the elders"

Theravāda is formed from two Pali words: thera (elder) and vāda (doctrine) — hence "the teaching of the elders." "Elders" here refers to the respected senior practitioners of the early generations — those regarded as preserving the teaching close to the Buddha's original teaching through an oral lineage reaching back to his time (article G.B3).

In terms of lineage, as article G.B4 described, Theravāda identifies itself as a direct heir of the Sthavira (Sanskrit: Sthavira) — the early "assembly of elders" that maintained strict disciplinary texts during the early schisms — through the Vibhajyavāda (Sanskrit: Vibhajyavāda). Thus, when reading different sources, you may encounter several "labels" for this tradition — Sthavira, Theravāda, Southern tradition, Southern school — referring to related naming conventions in Sino-Vietnamese, Pali, and geographical usage. In this group of articles, the standard name is Theravāda Buddhism.

1.2. Where Theravāda is found

The heartland of Theravāda is the South and Southeast Asian region: Sri Lanka — its historical cradle; Myanmar, Thailand, Laos, and Cambodia. Beyond this core region, Theravāda monasteries exist in Vietnam and among diaspora communities around the world. Each country has its own history, monastic organization, and customs — covered in article G.D4; the tradition's festivals are covered in article G.D5.

1.3. Four identifying features

One — the Pali Canon. The entire Tipiṭaka (Pali: tipiṭaka) of this tradition has been preserved in a single language — Pali. It is the most complete surviving body of early Buddhist scripture available today (article G.B4); the Chinese and Tibetan canons preserve corresponding translations through different layers of transmission. The structure and reading of this canon are the subject of the next article — G.D2.

Two — the arahant ideal. The Theravāda path aims at the goal taught in the Buddha's lifetime: achieving complete liberation in this very life. The culmination of the path is the arahant (Pali: arahant, Sanskrit: arhat) — one who has completely ended the mind's defilements and passed through the four stages of awakening from stream-entry to arahantship (article G.C7). Mahāyāna Buddhism later places greater emphasis on the bodhisattva ideal (Pali: bodhisatta, Sanskrit: bodhisattva) — one who vows to lead all beings toward awakening. These are different emphases within Buddhism, not higher and lower levels — a detailed comparison appears in article G.G1. It is also important to remember that the Pali canon itself contains the Jātaka (Pali: Jātaka) — hundreds of stories of the Buddha's previous lives as a bodhisattva before awakening — so the bodhisattva ideal is not entirely foreign to Theravāda (article G.E2).

Three — the gradual training. The Pali Canon presents the path as a step-by-step progression, called the gradual training (Pali: anupubbasikkhā): listening to the Dhamma to give rise to confidence, keeping the precepts, guarding the senses, practicing tranquillity meditation (Pali: samatha) to steady the mind, then insight meditation (Pali: vipassanā) to see reality clearly, and realizing the stages of awakening one by one. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 27 (Cūḷahatthipadopama Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]. This framework is the same three trainings of ethical conduct — concentration — wisdom presented in article G.C6; the full practice-and-life picture of the tradition is in article G.D3.

Four — a major social role for the Saṅgha. In Theravāda countries, the Saṅgha (Pali: saṅgha) is a central institution of community life: each morning monastics go on an alms-round (Pali: piṇḍapāta), laypeople offer food and receive the Dhamma in return; during the three-month rains retreat, monastics remain in one place for intensive practice. In many Thai and Burmese villages, the temple serves not only as a religious center but also as the village school, library, and meeting hall — the Saṅgha is simultaneously "teacher, custodian of knowledge, and keeper of ritual" for the community (article G.B6).

1.4. Who is called "Southern tradition" in Vietnam?

In Vietnam, Theravāda is commonly called the Southern tradition or Southern school — in contrast to the familiar Northern tradition associated with Mahāyāna Buddhism. But "Southern tradition" can refer to three different things, which beginners often confuse:

  • The Southern tradition in general: the Theravāda tradition as a whole, regardless of country — from Sri Lanka to Cambodia.
  • Ethnic Vietnamese Theravāda: Theravāda monasteries followed by Vietnamese practitioners, developing from the early 20th century, with recitation in Vietnamese and Pali.
  • Khmer Theravāda: Theravāda monasteries of the Khmer community in the Mekong Delta, with recitation in Khmer and Pali.

A note on terminology: some older books use "Hīnayāna" (Sanskrit: Hīnayāna) in contrast with "Mahāyāna." The term arose in some early Mahāyāna scriptures and carries a derogatory implication, so it is not used today as a neutral name; the standard neutral terms are Theravāda and Theravāda Buddhism. The distinction among Theravāda communities in Vietnam is discussed through temple life in articles G.B7 and G.D4.

2. The history of Theravāda

Before entering the timeline, one note about the sources — following the same method practiced in article G.B4: much of the information about early Theravāda comes from two Sri Lankan chronicles — the Dīpavaṃsa and Mahāvaṃsa — written centuries after the events they describe. The dates in this section should therefore be read as "according to the tradition's account"; archaeological evidence, such as Aśoka's pillars and monastery remains, helps confirm the broad historical picture.

2.1. From Vibhajyavāda to Sri Lanka (3rd century BCE)

Theravāda chronicles relate that under King Aśoka — the great patron-king introduced in article G.B4 — the community held the Third Buddhist Council (Pali: saṅgāyana) at the capital Pāṭaliputta to clarify the orthodox teaching and add the Kathāvatthu (Pali: Kathāvatthu) to the Abhidhamma corpus; the recognized lineage called itself Vibhajyavāda (introduced in article G.B3). According to the tradition, the missionary effort that followed brought Buddhism to Sri Lanka through Aśoka's son, the elder Mahinda, and daughter Saṅghamittā, during the reign of King Devānaṃpiya Tissa (around 250 BCE). The king adopted Buddhism, and the ancient capital Anurādhapura established the Mahāvihāra (Pali: Mahāvihāra) — the "mother house" of the Theravāda tradition that developed afterward. [Source: Mahāvaṃsa, chapters XIII–XVII - Theravāda Buddhism].

2.2. The Tipiṭaka is written down (1st century BCE)

For more than two centuries, the Tipiṭaka was preserved through memorization and oral recitation — the mechanism described in article G.B3. During the 1st century BCE, Sri Lanka experienced warfare; fearing that the orally preserved canon might be lost, elders of the Mahāvihāra gathered and wrote the entire Tipiṭaka down on palm leaves at Aluvihāra (in what is now central Sri Lanka), during the reign of King Vaṭṭagāmaṇī Abhaya (around 89–77 BCE). This is regarded as the first complete written record of an early Buddhist canon — the Pali canon survives today in large part because of that decision. [Source: Dīpavaṃsa, chapter XX; Mahāvaṃsa, chapter XXXIII - Theravāda Buddhism].

2.3. Buddhaghosa and the Pali commentarial tradition (5th century)

Around the 5th century, a young scholar from India (tradition says from the Bodh Gaya region) came to the Mahāvihāra: Buddhaghosa (meaning "voice of the Buddha"). There he composed the Visuddhimagga (Pali: Visuddhimagga) — a work that systematized the entire path of practice according to ethical conduct — concentration — wisdom and became a standard map still used today; he also systematized the commentaries (Pali: aṭṭhakathā) into a common Pali language. From Buddhaghosa's time onward, the pair "Pali canon + Pali commentaries" became a standard textual curriculum studied across Theravāda monasteries from Sri Lanka to Myanmar, Thailand, Laos, and Cambodia; the canon and commentarial tradition are the subject of article G.D2. [Source: Mahāvaṃsa, chapter XXXVII; the Visuddhimagga itself - Theravāda Buddhism].

2.4. Decline and revival, and expansion across Southeast Asia (6th–18th centuries)

Sri Lanka did not retain its position continuously: internal wars and invasions from South India (the Cōla dynasty) devastated Anurādhapura in the 11th century; Buddhism weakened so severely that the monastic community had to restore the ordination lineage from abroad — relying on monks from other countries to re-establish the ordination ceremony. Under King Vijayabāhu I in the 11th century, the ordination lineage was restored with help from the Burmese Saṅgha; under King Parakkamabāhu I in the 12th century, the Saṅgha was unified under the Mahāvihāra tradition. The pattern of "decline — seek ordination lineage — revival" repeated several times; the best-known late example came in 1753, when the Sri Lankan court invited a Saṅgha from Siam (Thailand) and established the Siyam Nikāya in Kandy — still one of Sri Lanka's two major orders today.

While Buddhism in its Indian homeland declined (12th–13th centuries, article G.B6), Theravāda spread widely across Southeast Asia: in Myanmar, King Anawrahta of Bagan accepted Theravāda through the monk Shin Arahan and made it the kingdom's principal religion in the 11th century; in Thailand, the Sukhothai kingdom in the 13th century established Theravāda as the main tradition; and in Laos and Cambodia in the 13th–14th centuries, Theravāda gradually replaced earlier traditions of the Angkor empire. Details for each country are in article G.D4.

2.5. 19th-century reform and global Theravāda today

The colonial period (Portuguese, Dutch, British) greatly weakened Buddhism in Sri Lanka; but contact with the West also opened a new period. The 19th century saw major reform movements: in Thailand, Prince Mongkut — a monk for many years who later became King Rama IV — founded the Dhammayuttika Nikāya, emphasizing strict study of the Vinaya; together with the Mahānikāya, it remains one of the two main monastic orders in Thailand today. In Myanmar, meditation increasingly became "popularized" through the scholar-meditation master Ledi Sayādaw in the late 19th century — the insight-meditation movements that followed later gained global influence. In Sri Lanka, the revival movement of Anagarika Dharmapāla in the late 19th and early 20th centuries connected Theravāda Buddhism with the Western world, where scholars had begun translating the Pali canon after the Pali Text Society was founded in 1881.

In the 20th and 21st centuries, Theravāda became a global phenomenon: diaspora communities from Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand, Laos, Cambodia, and Vietnam established temples in the United States, Europe, and Australia; insight meditation and mindfulness (Pali: sati) entered Western popular culture; the World Fellowship of Buddhists held its first meeting in Colombo in 1950. In Vietnam, ethnic Vietnamese Theravāda developed during the same period (early 20th century, article G.B7). Today, together with the other two major traditions, Theravāda is present on most continents — the overall picture of the three traditions is summarized in article G.G1.

3. Summary and next steps

  • Theravāda means "the teaching of the elders"; the tradition identifies itself as inheriting Sthavira through Vibhajyavāda; familiar names in Vietnam include Southern tradition and Southern school.
  • Core region: Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand, Laos, and Cambodia; in Vietnam there are ethnic Vietnamese Theravāda and Khmer Theravāda; diaspora communities have carried Theravāda around the world.
  • Four identifying features: the Pali Canon (the most complete surviving body of early Buddhist scripture), the arahant ideal, the gradual training structured through ethical conduct — concentration — wisdom, and the Saṅgha as a major social and cultural institution.
  • The history in a few milestones: Mahinda arrives in Sri Lanka (around 250 BCE) → Mahāvihāra at Anurādhapura → the Tipiṭaka is written down (1st century BCE) → Buddhaghosa systematizes the commentarial tradition (5th century) → expansion across Southeast Asia (11th–14th centuries) → revival and reform (1753; Dhammayuttika in the 19th century) → globalization in the 20th–21st centuries.
  • When reading this history, remember the source issue: the Sri Lankan chronicles were written centuries after the events — dates should be read as "according to the tradition's account."

Glossary:

  • Theravāda Buddhism: the tradition that preserves its canon in Pali, centered historically in Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand, Laos, and Cambodia; in Vietnam it is commonly called the Southern tradition.
  • Elder (Pali: thera): a respected senior member of the Saṅgha; the root of the name Theravāda — "the teaching of the elders."
  • Sthavira (Sanskrit: Sthavira): the early assembly of elders known for strict preservation of disciplinary texts during the early schisms — a predecessor in Theravāda's traditional lineage.
  • Vibhajyavāda (Sanskrit: Vibhajyavāda): the lineage recognized in the Third Council; according to Theravāda sources, the direct predecessor of Theravāda.
  • Tipiṭaka (Pali: tipiṭaka): the complete canon — Vinaya Piṭaka, Sutta Piṭaka, and Abhidhamma; the Pali canon is the most complete surviving body of the early canon.
  • Communal recitation (Pali: saṅgāyana): a collective recitation by the community to establish the standard form of the teaching and discipline.
  • Arahant (Pali: arahant, Sanskrit: arhat): one who has completely ended the mind's defilements — the culmination of the four stages of awakening, and the liberation ideal emphasized in Theravāda.
  • Bodhisattva (Pali: bodhisatta, Sanskrit: bodhisattva): one who aspires to awakening with the vow to lead all beings toward awakening — the central ideal of Mahāyāna Buddhism.
  • Jātaka (Pali: Jātaka): stories of the Buddha's previous lives as a bodhisattva, found in the Khuddaka Nikāya.
  • Gradual training (Pali: anupubbasikkhā): the presentation of the path step by step — hearing the Dhamma, keeping the precepts, guarding the senses, meditative training, insight meditation, and realization.
  • Tranquillity meditation (Pali: samatha): a practice for stabilizing and calming the mind; insight meditation (Pali: vipassanā): a practice of observing experience to see reality clearly.
  • Saṅgha (Pali: saṅgha): the community of Buddhist practitioners; in Theravāda it has a major social and cultural role.
  • Alms-round (Pali: piṇḍapāta): the practice of monastics receiving food from laypeople each morning, with the reciprocal sharing of the Dhamma.
  • Rains retreat (Pali: vassa): the three-month rainy-season period when monastics remain in one place for intensive practice; it ends with a robe-offering ceremony.
  • Mahāvihāra (Pali: Mahāvihāra): the monastery at Anurādhapura in Sri Lanka — the "mother house" of the Theravāda tradition.
  • Dīpavaṃsa / Mahāvaṃsa: the two Sri Lankan chronicles — major historical sources for early Theravāda, written centuries after the events they describe.
  • Buddhaghosa: the 5th-century scholar associated with the Mahāvihāra who systematized the Pali commentaries and composed the Visuddhimagga.
  • Visuddhimagga (Pali: Visuddhimagga): the standard work systematizing the Theravāda path through ethical conduct — concentration — wisdom.
  • Commentary (Pali: aṭṭhakathā): texts that explain the canonical scriptures — the second textual layer after the primary canon.
  • Kathāvatthu (Pali: Kathāvatthu): a work dealing with points of doctrinal controversy, traditionally associated with the Third Council.
  • Siyam Nikāya: a Sri Lankan monastic order established in 1753 with an ordination lineage from Siam (Thailand).
  • Dhammayuttika Nikāya: the Thai reform order associated with Mongkut in the 19th century; together with Mahānikāya it remains one of the two principal Thai monastic orders.
  • Ethnic Vietnamese Theravāda / Khmer Theravāda: two Theravāda communities in Vietnam — one followed primarily by Vietnamese practitioners (Vietnamese and Pali liturgy, developing in the early 20th century), and the other by the Khmer community in the Mekong Delta (Khmer and Pali liturgy).
  • Hīnayāna (Sanskrit: Hīnayāna): a term with a derogatory implication found in some early Mahāyāna texts; not used as a neutral name — use "Theravāda" instead.
  • Mindfulness (Pali: sati): remembering clearly and keeping attention appropriately on what is occurring — a core part of meditation.
