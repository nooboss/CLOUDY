G.B6. Buddhism Spreads

After the three traditions took shape in India (the previous article), the teachings began their journey across Asia — not by the sword, but through trade routes, missionary missions, journeys to seek scriptures, and cultural marriages. Over more than a thousand years, three major streams shaped the map of Buddhism today: Theravāda Buddhism southward to Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia; Mahāyāna Buddhism northward along the Silk Road into East Asia; and Vajrayāna Buddhism into the Himalayan region and Mongolia. This article follows each route; the D, E, and F groups then explore each tradition in greater depth.

1. The Theravāda route — Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia

1.1. Sri Lanka — the cradle of Theravāda

The first and most important base of the Theravāda route was Sri Lanka. According to tradition, in the 3rd century BCE, during the reign of King Devānaṃpiya Tissa, the elder Mahinda — son of King Aśoka (Pali: Asoka) — arrived on the island with a mission, and the Sri Lankan king took refuge; Mahinda's sister, Saṅghamittā, later brought the lineage of bhikkhunīs (Pali: bhikkhunī) to the island. At Anurādhapura, Mahāvihāra became a major center for learning Pali, translating, and preserving the teachings. In the 1st century BCE, it was here that the Pali Three Baskets (Pali: tipiṭaka) were written down — a decisive event that made the Pali scriptural tradition the most complete surviving collection of early Buddhist texts (article G.B3).

From Sri Lanka, Theravāda spread into Southeast Asia through missionary missions from India, journeys by Sri Lankan monks, and merchant networks. Southeast Asian kingdoms adopted Theravāda Buddhism one after another — and each built its Saṅgha on a Sri Lankan model: Pali scriptures, Theravāda Vinaya, and the Saṅgha as a major cultural and educational institution.

1.2. Southeast Asia — country by country

  • Myanmar: Buddhism arrived early through the south (Mon city-states); in the 11th century, King Anawrahta of Bagan received Sri Lankan and Mon monks and aligned the kingdom with Theravāda; thousands of temples and stupas were built from Bagan onward. Myanmar later became a major center of Pali scholarship and helped popularize meditation in the 20th century (article G.D4).
  • Thailand: the Sukhothai and Ayutthaya kingdoms followed Theravāda; kings acted as patrons of the Saṅgha; two major monastic orders later developed (Mahānikāya and Dhammayuttika — a reform movement of the 19th century); Thailand today has the world's largest Theravāda Buddhist population.
  • Laos and Cambodia: received Theravāda through routes from Thailand, Myanmar, and Sri Lanka; Buddhism became deeply woven into village culture — seasonal festivals and temples as community centers; Cambodia also inherited Buddhist traditions from the Angkor period.
  • Vietnam: Theravāda has been present in the south from an early period, developing into two important communities — Kinh Theravāda (Vietnamese, from the early 20th century) and Khmer Theravāda (the Khmer community in southwestern Vietnam) — discussed in the next article and the G.D4 group.

A common feature of the region is the Pali scriptural tradition, the Saṅgha's major social role (monks serving as teachers in village communities), and lay life connected to temples through giving, ethical training, and observance days (days for keeping precepts and listening to the Dhamma — Pali: uposatha) (see the G.D1 group).

2. The Mahāyāna route — the Silk Road and East Asia

2.1. The Silk Road — the intermediate stations

When Mahāyāna emerged in India (previous article), a trade corridor was already in place to carry it outward: the Silk Road (a network of trade routes connecting India, Central Asia, and China). Monks traveled with merchant caravans; temples and cave shrines appeared along the routes as "rest stops" that both welcomed travelers and supported scripture translation. The most important intermediate region consisted of the Central Asian kingdoms, among them Kucha: it was here that Kumārajīva (344–413) — one of the great translators — studied Mahāyāna scriptures in Sanskrit before being brought, under coercion, to China.

2.2. China — translation and absorption

Buddhism entered China around the 1st century CE through the Silk Road. From a foreign religion carried partly by Central Asian merchants, Buddhism was gradually translated, explained through Chinese concepts, and patronized by successive courts. Three major developments stand out:

  • Scripture translation: great translation campaigns — Kumārajīva (4th–5th centuries) translated Prajñāpāramitā and Lotus scriptures; Xuánzàng (602–664) — who traveled on foot to India and studied there for 17 years before bringing back hundreds of texts — translated Yogācāra scriptures; the Chinese Buddhist canon that emerged became the largest scriptural corpus of Mahāyāna.
  • Schools: based on the translated scriptures, Chinese Buddhist schools emerged: Chan (meditation school), Pure Land, Tiantai, Huayan, Faxiang, and others (article G.E4).
  • Cultural integration: Buddhism entered into dialogue with Confucianism and Daoism — debating and absorbing ideas in both directions; at the same time, a class of Buddhist scholars and artists flourished: the Yungang and Longmen cave sculptures, temples, and religious art spread across the country.

2.3. Into East Asia

From China, Mahāyāna spread together with its own schools and rituals: to Korea (from the 4th century, through Chinese monks; major schools formed, and later Chan Buddhism in the Jogye tradition became dominant), to Japan (from the 6th century through the Korean Peninsula; major schools were transmitted, followed by the strong popular growth of Zen and Pure Land), and southward into Vietnam — through a distinct route involving both maritime connections with China and Indian Buddhist traditions arriving by sea; Vietnamese Buddhism became part of Northern-tradition Mahāyāna, with Chan, Pure Land, and combined Chan–Pure Land practice (next article).

A common feature of East Asian Buddhism is scriptures translated into Chinese; the Dharmaguptaka Vinaya for monastics (monks and nuns) (article G.B4); the bodhisattva ideal and rich festivals; and Buddhist practice woven into daily life through liturgies, temples, and diverse methods — from seated meditation to Buddha-recitation.

3. The Vajrayāna route — the Himalayas and Mongolia

3.1. Tibet — two major transmission waves

Tibet became the major center of Vajrayāna, and Buddhism entered through two major transmission waves:

  • First wave (7th–8th centuries): King Songtsen Gampo and especially King Trisong Detsen invited two major Indian teachers: Śāntarakṣita — a Mahāyāna scholar — and Padmasambhava — a tantric master — to establish Buddhism; Samye, the first monastery, was built. [Source: Tibetan chronicles and lineage texts - Tibetan tradition].
  • Second wave (10th–11th centuries): after a period of decline, teachers such as Atiśa from India and Marpa — who traveled to India to study and bring the teachings back — re-established the lineages; from this period, the major schools of Tibetan Buddhism gradually formed (article G.F2).

The Tibetan route has several distinctive features: the Sanskrit Buddhist corpus was translated into Tibetan script rather than Chinese, forming a distinct collection of Kangyur (the Buddha's words) and Tengyur (treatises and commentaries); the teacher–student relationship (Sanskrit: guru) is central to the path; and tantric methods (mantra, maṇḍala, visualization) are practiced on a Mahāyāna foundation.

3.2. Into neighboring countries

  • Bhutan: received Vajrayāna from Tibet; Buddhism functions as the de facto state religion; fortified monasteries (dzong) serve as both administrative centers and monasteries.
  • Nepal: the Newar community preserves a distinct Sanskrit Buddhist tradition alongside the surrounding Hindu religious culture.
  • Mongolia: from the 13th century, through relations with Tibetan monastics, Vajrayāna became the principal Buddhist tradition; later, Tibetan schools (especially Gelug — one of the four major schools of Tibetan Buddhism) spread widely among the population.
  • Russian communities: Vajrayāna is practiced in the republics of Buryatia, Kalmykia, and Tuva — the result of transmission from Mongolia.

4. Modern Buddhism — a journey into the world

From the 19th century onward, a new current emerged: Buddhism began to move beyond Asia into Europe and the Americas. The main routes included:

  • Scholarship: Western scholars learned Pali and Sanskrit and translated scriptures; some — and later some Asian teachers and practitioners as well — established Buddhist societies in the West (including figures such as Colonel Olcott, who contributed to the Buddhist revival in colonial Sri Lanka).
  • Meditation and mindfulness: Asian teachers — from both East Asian Zen traditions and Theravāda meditation traditions — traveled abroad to teach; in the 20th–21st centuries, mindfulness (Pali: sati) became widely studied and applied in Western medicine and education.
  • Migration: Asian migration, including major refugee and diaspora movements in recent centuries, brought temples to Western cities; migrant communities — including Vietnamese communities — maintained Buddhism in new temples.
  • Engaged Buddhism: the idea of combining spiritual practice with social action — associated with figures such as Thích Nhất Hạnh and other movements.

The result today: Buddhism is present on every continent; in the West, many people practice meditation and mindfulness outside formal religious institutions; and apps — including the one you are using — continue this journey through digital language. From a region of northeastern India (article G.B1), the teachings have traveled across cultures and languages — and today they have reached your phone.

5. Summary and next steps

  • Theravāda: Sri Lanka (Mahinda, Mahāvihāra, the written Pali Canon) → Myanmar (Bagan), Thailand, Laos, Cambodia; Pali scriptures and the Saṅgha as a cultural center.
  • Mahāyāna: the Silk Road → China (scripture translation: Kumārajīva, Xuánzàng; Buddhist schools) → Korea, Japan, Vietnam; Chinese scriptures and the Dharmaguptaka Vinaya.
  • Vajrayāna: two transmission waves into Tibet (Śāntarakṣita — Padmasambhava; Atiśa — Marpa) → Bhutan, Nepal, Mongolia, Russian communities; Tibetan scriptures and a central teacher–student relationship.
  • Modern period (from the 19th century): scholarship, meditation and mindfulness, migration, engaged Buddhism — Buddhism is now present on every continent.
  • The route into Vietnam — through both Theravāda and Mahāyāna — is the subject of the next article.

Glossary:

  • Silk Road: a network of ancient trade routes linking India, Central Asia, and China — the main corridor by which Mahāyāna Buddhism reached East Asia.
  • Kumārajīva (344–413): a major translator born in Central Asia who translated Prajñāpāramitā and Lotus scriptures into Chinese; a key figure in the great Chinese translation movement.
  • Xuánzàng (602–664): a Chinese monk who traveled on foot to India, studied there for 17 years, brought back hundreds of texts, and translated them; his journey later became famous through the novel Journey to the West.
  • Mahāvihāra: a major Buddhist center in Anurādhapura, Sri Lanka, where the Pali Canon was written down in the 1st century BCE.
  • Anawrahta: the 11th-century king of Bagan who aligned Myanmar with Theravāda and began an era of monumental temple and stupa construction.
  • Dhammayuttika: a reform movement within the Thai Saṅgha in the 19th century, initiated by King Mongkut and emphasizing strict study and observance of the Vinaya.
  • Śāntarakṣita: an Indian Mahāyāna scholar invited to Tibet in the 8th century who helped establish the doctrinal foundations of Tibetan Buddhism.
  • Padmasambhava: an 8th-century Indian tantric master, regarded in Tibetan traditions as a major figure in establishing Buddhism and especially revered by the Nyingma tradition.
  • Atiśa: an Indian master (982–1054) who went to Tibet during the second transmission wave and helped re-establish Buddhism there; associated with the Kadampa lineage.
  • Marpa: a Tibetan translator (1012–1097) who traveled to India three times to study tantric teachings, founded the Kagyu lineage, and taught Milarepa.
  • Kangyur and Tengyur (Tibetan: bka' 'gyur, bstan 'gyur): the two main parts of the Tibetan Buddhist canon — Kangyur contains translations of the Buddha's words, while Tengyur contains treatises and commentaries.
  • Mindfulness (Pali: sati): remembering and clearly knowing what is happening in the mind and present moment; widely studied and applied in medicine and education in the 20th–21st centuries.
  • Engaged Buddhism: an approach combining spiritual practice with social action in areas such as peace, the environment, and justice.
  • Guru (Sanskrit: guru): a spiritual teacher; in Vajrayāna, the teacher has a central role in transmitting the path.
