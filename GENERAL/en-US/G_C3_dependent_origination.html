G.C3. Dependent Origination

The previous article showed that suffering has causes, and that the path works by intervening in those causes. But why is intervention possible? Because suffering does not simply fall upon us — it is produced by a process that can be seen. That process follows a principle the texts call Dependent Origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda, Sanskrit: pratītyasamutpāda) — regarded as a core law of Buddhism: whoever sees dependent origination sees the Dharma. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 28 (Mahāhatthipadopama Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism].

1. The principle — "when this is, that is"

The formula for Dependent Origination in the texts is very concise and has two directions:

  • "When this exists, that comes to be; when this arises, that arises."
  • "When this does not exist, that does not come to be; when this ceases, that ceases."

[Source: Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 12.20 (Paṭiccasamuppāda Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]. In other words: all phenomena arise in dependence on conditions — the texts call those conditions conditions — and when the conditions change or dissolve, the phenomenon changes or dissolves as well. Nothing stands entirely by itself or generates itself.

A familiar everyday example is a seed and a tree. A seed does not simply become a tree by itself — it needs water, soil, light, and suitable weather; when those conditions come together, a sprout appears; when conditions are reduced or lost (drought, flooding), the tree dies or stops growing. The tree does not "naturally" become a tree on its own — it arises through the combination of conditions. Dependent Origination applies the same principle to all phenomena — from plants and social life to each moment of sadness and each moment of happiness in the mind.

The place of Dependent Origination in the map of Buddhist teaching: it provides a common explanation for three major themes — karma (actions producing results, article G.C4), rebirth (the cycle of birth and death, article G.C4), and the path to liberation (because causes can be removed, as the Truth of Cessation showed in the previous article). These themes look different, but they operate under one underlying principle.

2. Dependent Origination in everyday life

Two practical examples — one social and one directly within the mind:

  • Anger does not simply appear on its own: you come home tired (condition 1), hear an offhand remark that touches an old wound (condition 2), and have slept too little that day (condition 3) — anger flares up. Remove one condition: sleep enough that day, or have a steadier mind — and the same remark may pass like wind. Anger is not your "nature" — it is a conditioned phenomenon and therefore something you can intervene in.
  • A habit does not simply appear on its own: the habit of scrolling on a phone at midnight is fed by conditions — the phone beside the bed (condition 1), messages designed to trigger curiosity (condition 2), and a restless workday that creates a wish to escape (condition 3). To change the habit, change the conditions — exactly as Dependent Origination suggests, without needing to "fight" the will.

This way of seeing things is one reason Buddhism is so practical: instead of asking "Why am I like this?" (a question about essence), Dependent Origination leads to the question "What conditions are feeding this?" (a question that allows action).

3. Ignorance — and why liberation is possible

But why does suffering keep being produced? The texts point to the first condition: ignorance (Pali: avijjā, Sanskrit: avidyā) — not seeing reality clearly: not seeing change, not seeing how things depend on one another, and not seeing how suffering is produced (article G.C5 will clarify the non-self dimension of this).

Here an important question arises: if ignorance — a form of not knowing — is the root of suffering, then that is actually good news. Because not knowing can be replaced by knowing — no one has to fight the nature of the universe; one only needs to see clearly. This is where Dependent Origination makes the path of liberation intelligible: suffering is conditioned; ignorance is the first condition; when ignorance is replaced by wisdom, the whole process that produces suffering loses its fuel — just like the image of a fire going out in the Truth of Cessation. In other words, the path of practice is, at its core, a work of seeing clearly.

4. The twelve links of Dependent Origination

The texts develop Dependent Origination into a chain of twelve links — called the twelve links of Dependent Origination (Pali: dvādasanidāna, nidāna) — describing how suffering is produced from ignorance through aging-and-death. [Source: Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 12.1 (Paṭicca-samuppāda-vibhaṅga Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]. The twelve links in order are:

  • 1. Ignorance — not seeing clearly, as described in section 3.
  • 2. Formations (Pali: saṅkhāra) — from ignorance arise formations that leave imprints: bodily, verbal, and mental activities — a source of karma.
  • 3. Consciousness (Pali: viññāṇa) — awareness directed toward an object.
  • 4. Name-and-form (Pali: nāmarūpa) — the full psycho-physical process of experience.
  • 5. Six sense bases (Pali: saḷāyatana) — eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind, the six doors through which the world makes contact.
  • 6. Contact (Pali: phassa) — a sense base meets an object and contact occurs (eye with a form, ear with a sound, and so on).
  • 7. Feeling (Pali: vedanā) — contact gives rise to a feeling: pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral.
  • 8. Craving (Pali: taṇhā) — pleasant feeling stimulates thirst or craving; this is the craving identified by the Truth of the Origin of Suffering.
  • 9. Clinging (Pali: upādāna) — craving turns into clinging.
  • 10. Becoming (Pali: bhava) — through clinging, a mode of existence is formed.
  • 11. Birth (Pali: jāti) — that mode of existence comes into being, whether understood as a rebirth or as a new state of mind.
  • 12. Aging-and-death (Pali: jarāmaraṇa) — whatever arises changes and passes away, along with the suffering connected with it. The cycle of producing suffering is completed.

A modern trial run using a message notification during work: the ear encounters a sound (contact) → "that sounds interesting" (feeling) → wanting to check it immediately (craving) → becoming caught by the temptation (clinging) → "becoming the person who is scrolling" (becoming — birth) → an hour disappears, along with frustration because work remains unfinished (aging-and-death of that moment). The twelve links are not a remote theory — they are a map of the small mental cycles repeatedly occurring throughout the day.

5. Three ways of reading the twelve links

The twelve links have three major interpretive approaches in Buddhist traditions — knowing this helps prevent confusion when different books seem to explain them differently:

  • The three-lives model (the traditional Theravāda commentarial reading): ignorance and formations belong to the past life; consciousness through feeling belong to the present life (results of the past); craving, clinging, and becoming create conditions for the future life; birth and aging-and-death belong to the future. This reading answers the question "How does rebirth operate across lives?".
  • Moment-to-moment observation (a reading associated with Madhyamaka — Sanskrit: Madhyamaka — the Mahāyāna Middle Way school associated with Nāgārjuna): all twelve links recur within each moment of experience — whenever the mind contacts the world, a small cycle can be completed. This reading answers "How is suffering being produced right now?".
  • Practical application: one does not need to solve all twelve links at once — practice simply looks for a point of intervention. A particularly emphasized point is between feeling and craving: feelings arise naturally, but whether we turn a feeling into craving is a place where there is still room for choice. Right mindfulness and right effort (from the previous article) are precisely the tools for intervention here — seeing feeling as feeling without automatically allowing it to become clinging.

The three readings do not necessarily exclude one another — they answer different questions using the same map: the first looks at past — future, the second at the present, and the third answers "What do I do now?". 6. Summary and next steps

  • Dependent Origination = the principle that "phenomena arise through conditions" — in both directions: when conditions are present, the result can arise; when conditions cease, the result ceases; a seed needs water — soil — sunlight.
  • In practice: anger and habits are both conditioned — the practical question is "What conditions are feeding this?" rather than asking about an underlying "essence."
  • The root condition in the chain is ignorance — something that can be replaced by clear seeing; therefore liberation is possible.
  • The twelve links: ignorance → formations → consciousness → name-and-form → six sense bases → contact → feeling → craving → clinging → becoming → birth → aging-and-death — a map of how suffering is produced, from the large scale down to each moment.
  • Three readings: three lives (rebirth), moment-to-moment (the present), and practical application — intervention between feeling and craving is the key point.

Suggested next reading: within the chain of Dependent Origination, "formations" — activities that leave imprints — open the door to the subject of karma: what our actions create and how. The next article explains karma — what it means and what it does not mean — followed by rebirth and the realms. When the twelve links are examined closely in practice, that becomes the work of Insight Meditation; this article has built the conceptual map needed for that practice.

Glossary:

  • Dependent Origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda, Sanskrit: pratītyasamutpāda): the principle that phenomena arise in dependence on conditions; also called dependent arising, and the twelve links when referring specifically to the twelve-link formulation.
  • Conditions: factors that come together to allow a phenomenon to arise — such as water, soil, and sunlight for a seed.
  • Ignorance (Pali: avijjā, Sanskrit: avidyā): not seeing change, interdependence, and how suffering is produced; the first link in the chain.
  • Twelve links (Pali: nidāna): twelve links describing the process by which suffering is produced, from ignorance to aging-and-death.
  • Formations (Pali: saṅkhāra): formative activities of body, speech, and mind; connected with karma (article G.C4).
  • Consciousness (Pali: viññāṇa, Sanskrit: vijñāna): awareness distinguishing an object; the third link.
  • Name-and-form (Pali: nāmarūpa): mind (name) and body (form) — the full psycho-physical process of experience.
  • Six sense bases (Pali: saḷāyatana): the six sense doors — eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind.
  • Contact (Pali: phassa): contact between a sense base and its object.
  • Feeling (Pali: vedanā): pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral feeling; the key point of intervention lies between feeling and craving.
  • Craving (Pali: taṇhā): thirst or craving — the root of suffering identified by the Truth of the Origin of Suffering; the eighth link.
  • Clinging (Pali: upādāna): grasping — turning craving into something one holds onto as "mine."
  • Becoming (Pali: bhava): the process of "becoming" — a mode of existence being formed.
  • Birth and aging-and-death (Pali: jāti and jarāmaraṇa): the final two links — what comes into being ages and ends, along with related suffering.
  • Madhyamaka (Sanskrit): the Mahāyāna Middle Way school associated with Nāgārjuna; the moment-to-moment reading of Dependent Origination is often associated with this tradition.
  • Moment: an extremely brief unit of time used in some commentarial descriptions of mental processes.
