G.D5.2. Vietnam: Māgha Pūjā — Saṅgha Day
Fifteenth day of the first lunar month

An important early-year observance in Theravāda Buddhism, often called Nam tông in Vietnam, is Māgha Pūjā, or Saṅgha Day. It commemorates the traditional gathering of 1,250 arahants (Pali: arahant, one fully liberated from the mental defilements) and the teaching known as the Ovāda Pāṭimokkha. In the Vietnamese calendar notation used in this article, the observance falls on the fifteenth day of the first lunar month.

1. What event does this observance commemorate?

In the first year after the Buddha's awakening and the beginning of his teaching career, on the full moon of Māgha, tradition remembers an unusual event at Veluvana (Pali: Veḷuvana, Bamboo Grove) near Rājagaha, the ancient capital of Magadha: 1,250 bhikkhus gathered without prior notice or a shared appointment. Theravāda tradition identifies four distinctive features of this gathering:

Four rare features of one day

  • The full moon of Māgha: also an uposatha observance day in the lunar calendar.
  • No prior arrangement: the bhikkhus came together without an invitation or pre-arranged plan.
  • All were arahants: people who had ended the mental defilements and whom tradition describes as fully liberated.
  • All were ehi-bhikkhu: they had received the Buddha's early form of ordination, expressed in the words “Ehi, bhikkhu”, “Come, bhikkhu.”

Traditional commentaries commonly explain the 1,250 as about 1,000 disciples of the three Kassapa brothers and 250 associated with the disciples of Sāriputta and Moggallāna. This is a commentarial explanation, not a detail to treat as independently established historical fact. At the gathering, the Buddha held a pure uposatha observance and gave the Ovāda Pāṭimokkha, a concise teaching on the path of practice. The most frequently quoted part is found in verses 183–185 of the Dhammapada. Some English-language sources therefore call this Saṅgha Day or Fourfold Assembly Day. [Source: Māgha Pūjā recitation tradition; Dhammapada, verses 183–185]

2. Why is it kept on the fifteenth day of the first lunar month?

The name consists of Māgha, the name of a month in the ancient Indian calendar, and pūjā, meaning paying homage or making an offering. The observance is determined by the full moon of Māgha. This can be confusing because Southeast Asian countries use different local calendars and month-numbering systems, so the same full moon may be recorded under different month names. The following comparison is helpful:

The Māgha full moon — date notation in different calendars

  • Thailand: full moon of the third month in the Thai Buddhist calendar — Makha Bucha.
  • Laos: full moon of the third month in the Lao calendar — commonly associated with Māgha Pūjā / Boun Makha Bousa.
  • Cambodia: full moon of the third month — Meak Bochea.
  • Vietnam: fifteenth day of the first lunar month.

Put simply, the Māgha full moon usually falls around February or early March in the Gregorian calendar. In the Vietnamese notation used here, it is recorded as the fifteenth day of the first lunar month, while Thailand, Laos, and Cambodia commonly call it the full moon of the third month in their own systems. Because local calendars do not align perfectly, do not assume that the Gregorian date is always identical in every country. When an exact date is needed, check the particular year, following the principle introduced in G.D5.

3. The observance's three central teachings

The three lines most often mentioned in connection with the gathering's teaching are from the Ovāda Pāṭimokkha. They are a memorable summary, not the whole of that teaching:

Three lines — three practices

  • “Avoid all evil”: refrain from harmful actions and speech, and from feeding unwholesome tendencies.
  • “Cultivate what is good”: actively develop wholesome actions, speech, and states of mind.
  • “Purify the mind”: gradually weaken and abandon greed, hatred, and delusion. Mindfulness (Pali: sati) is an important part of this process, but not its only method.

The passage concludes with the sense that this is the teaching of the Buddhas. Dhammapada verses 184–185 immediately add further important points: patience as a highest practice, non-harming, ethical restraint, and moderation. The three lines should therefore be understood as the most concise and memorable part, not as the entire Ovāda Pāṭimokkha. [Source: Dhammapada, verses 183–185]

4. How is the observance kept in Vietnam?

The approach to reading a Buddhist observance was introduced in G.D5.1: distinguish its foundational teaching, shared practice, and custom. Applied to Saṅgha Day in Vietnam:

Three layers for understanding Saṅgha Day in Vietnam

  • Foundational meaning: remembering the gathering of 1,250 arahants and the three central teachings. This core does not change from country to country.
  • Shared practice: depending on the monastery, people may chant, hear the Dhamma, make offerings, meditate, perform good deeds, or take part in commemorative rites. Some monasteries offer the eight precepts (Pali: aṭṭhasīla) for one day and night or longer periods of practice.
  • Custom: the first lunar full moon is an important date in Vietnamese culture. In many places, people visit temples, do good deeds, eat vegetarian food, or pray for well-being. In Mahāyāna communities, it is also associated with the First Full Moon Festival, Tết Nguyên Tiêu. This is a cultural and customary layer, not a compulsory doctrinal requirement.

Keeping the three layers distinct helps newcomers avoid confusion: the event and its teaching are foundational, while rites and customs vary by monastery, tradition, and locality. Thus, the same first lunar full moon can be observed quite differently in Vietnamese Theravāda and Mahāyāna monasteries.

5. What newcomers should know

  • Arrive a little early and choose a quiet place to sit. When monastics enter for the service, stand and join your palms.
  • Offerings are voluntary; you may simply attend and listen to the Dhamma.
  • The Dhamma talk often centres on the three teachings, which can be put into practice during the following week.
  • The fifteenth lunar day is a full-moon uposatha day; some Buddhists use it to undertake precepts or chant (G.D3).
  • No rite needs to be performed “perfectly.” If you are unsure, observe the shared rhythm of the service and follow along; the spirit of learning without anxiety about mistakes is explained in G.E6.

6. Summary and next steps

  • Saṅgha Day commemorates the gathering of 1,250 arahants and the traditional Ovāda Pāṭimokkha teaching, with its four distinctive features preserved in Māgha Pūjā tradition.
  • Its three central teachings are: avoid evil, cultivate good, and purify the mind.
  • In Vietnam it is recorded on the fifteenth day of the first lunar month. Thailand, Laos, and Cambodia commonly record the same occasion as the third-month full moon in their local calendar systems; because these calendars differ, do not assume that their Gregorian dates always coincide.
  • Read the observance through three layers: foundational teaching → shared practice → custom.

Glossary:

  • Theravāda Buddhism (Pali: Theravāda): a Buddhist tradition whose scriptures are preserved chiefly in Pali. It is prominent in Sri Lanka and several Southeast Asian countries, including Thailand, Myanmar, Laos, and Cambodia; in Vietnam it is often called Nam tông.
  • Māgha Pūjā (Pali): Saṅgha Day, the Māgha-full-moon observance of the gathering of 1,250 arahants.
  • Arahant (Pali: arahant): one who has completely ended the defilements of mind and the round of rebirth.
  • Ehi bhikkhu (Pali: “Come, bhikkhu”): a direct form of ordination by the Buddha in the early period.
  • Veluvana (Pali: Veḷuvana): Bamboo Grove, a monastery near Rājagaha and one of the earliest residences offered to the Buddha.
  • Rājagaha (Pali): the ancient capital of Magadha, where the gathering is traditionally placed.
  • Ovāda Pāṭimokkha: the teaching associated with the gathering of 1,250 arahants. Dhammapada verses 183–185, especially the three themes of avoiding evil, cultivating good, and purifying the mind, are its most frequently quoted part.
  • Saṅgha (Pali: saṅgha): the Buddhist monastic community.
  • Mindfulness (Pali: sati): the capacity to know clearly what is occurring in body and mind, without losing the object of attention.
  • Uposatha (Pali): lunar observance days. In Theravāda tradition, the new and full moons, as well as intervening lunar markers, have particular significance. Monastics recite the Pātimokkha according to the discipline; laypeople may choose these days to undertake precepts or strengthen their practice.
  • Eight precepts (Pali: aṭṭhasīla): eight training rules observed for one day and night, allowing laypeople to follow a more disciplined way of practice; explained in G.D3.
  • Pūjā (Pali): paying homage or making a respectful offering; the origin of the endings Bucha / Bochea in Thai, Lao, and Khmer festival names.
  • First Full Moon Festival (Vietnamese: Tết Nguyên Tiêu): the first full moon in East Asian cultures; many Vietnamese Mahāyāna communities also hold observances at this time.
  • Vietnamese Theravāda: a term for Theravāda monasteries serving chiefly ethnic Vietnamese communities. It exists alongside Khmer Theravāda monasteries, particularly in southern Vietnam (G.D4).

Source notes

  • The gathering of 1,250 arahants and the Ovāda Pāṭimokkha tradition are preserved in Theravāda Māgha Pūjā recitation and explanation.
  • The three teachings, together with the related points on patience, non-harming, and ethical restraint, are found in the Dhammapada, verses 183–185.
  • The traditional account of the 1,250's composition—1,000 associated with the Kassapa elders and 250 others—is a common commentarial explanation and is presented as supplementary information.
  • Festival dates in the different calendar systems—the Vietnamese first-lunar-month full moon and the third-month full moon commonly used in Thailand, Laos, and Cambodia—are local calendar notations. They are not converted into one fixed Gregorian date for every year and country.
  • The first lunar full moon and Tết Nguyên Tiêu are cultural and customary layers, distinguished from the festival's foundational teaching.
