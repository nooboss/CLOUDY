G.A3. Lay Buddhist Life

The previous article introduced different levels of commitment — and most app readers will be at the level of the lay Buddhist: living an ordinary life, raising a family, working, while maintaining the path. This article looks at what that life involves: taking refuge — the foundational ceremony and the meaning of each phrase; the five precepts — five training commitments, why they matter, and how to apply them; what everyday life can look like in practice; and the foundational relationship between confidence and wisdom — so that one can begin well without stopping at blind belief.

1. Threefold Refuge — taking refuge in Buddha, Dhamma, and Saṅgha

The ceremony by which a person formally enters lay Buddhist life is called taking refuge (turning toward — relying on) or the Threefold Refuge (Pali: tisaraṇa, Sanskrit: triśaraṇa - Threefold Refuge): a decision to turn toward the Three Jewels (the three precious supports) — Buddha, Dhamma, and Saṅgha — as the three supports of the path. The ceremony is very simple: state one's name, make the aspiration, recite the refuge formulas, and then undertake the five precepts — the heart of the ceremony is the spoken commitment, not complicated procedure.

The traditional refuge formula is preserved in its familiar Pali form:

"Buddhaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāmi — Dhammaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāmi — Saṅghaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāmi" — meaning: "I go to the Buddha for refuge; I go to the Dhamma for refuge; I go to the Saṅgha for refuge." The word saraṇa means refuge — a place of shelter; gacchāmi means "I go to". This "going to" describes something continuous, not something completed once and for all: each day the Buddhist turns again toward the Buddha, learns the Dhamma, and relies on the community — like a plant continuing to turn toward the light.

What do the three refuges mean, one by one?

  • Taking refuge in the Buddha: honoring the awakened teacher and remembering the path he walked — not asking to be blessed without walking it. The scriptures encourage disciples to make themselves their own refuge and the Dhamma their refuge; in this spirit, taking refuge in the Buddha means learning from the light, not waiting to be carried.
  • Taking refuge in the Dhamma: taking the teaching as a map for practice — this is the primary refuge; the Buddha is the guide, while the Dhamma is the path itself.
  • Taking refuge in the Saṅgha: relying on the community of those who have gone before — just as learning any profession requires a school, teachers, and fellow students; the Saṅgha preserves the teachings and is a "field of merit" for laypeople to cultivate generosity (article G.C4).

The distinction that must be kept clear is: taking refuge is not the same as asking for supernatural protection. Taking refuge is a voluntary decision to rely on the three supports as a way of training oneself; asking for protection is the wish to be sheltered from harm. Buddhism does not deny folk prayer practices found alongside it in many places (article G.A1 has already distinguished these layers), but the core of taking refuge is self-direction: the person taking refuge says to themselves — from now on, I will walk this path, with the Buddha as example, the Dhamma as map, and the Saṅgha as companions. The Dhammapada compares this with the way fearful people run for refuge to other places — mountains, forests, tree shrines, and sacred sites — yet these are not secure refuges; taking refuge in Buddha — Dhamma — Saṅgha leads to seeing the Four Noble Truths. [Source: Dhammapada, verses 188–192 - Theravāda Buddhism].

The Threefold Refuge is common to all three traditions — the three-refuge formula appears in Theravāda Buddhism (in Pali), Mahāyāna Buddhism (in Chinese liturgical forms such as "I take refuge in the Buddha..."), and Vajrayāna Buddhism (often embedded in a transmission and ritual context with a teacher); only the surrounding ritual details vary by culture. Taking refuge also does not "close the door": a person who has taken refuge can still live an ordinary life and continue learning elsewhere — like someone with a family who can still make new friends; it is a direction of life, not a wall.

2. Five Precepts — five training commitments, not five punishments

The five precepts (Pali: pañcasīla - five precepts) are ethical commitments for lay Buddhists — and the key is visible in the original wording: each is phrased as "I undertake to refrain..." (Pali: veramaṇī) — a voluntary training commitment, not a prohibition imposed by a sacred authority. The scriptures present each precept with its reasons and results. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 41 (Sāleyyaka Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 41) - Theravāda Buddhism]. The five trainings are:

  • 1. I undertake to refrain from killing — valuing life. The reason: living beings value their lives just as we value ours; this trains a non-harming mind. Scope: not killing or destroying the life of humans and animals; today this can also include everyday violence and the destruction of habitats. Note: this precept does not require vegetarianism — as the previous article explained (according to tradition and one's capacity).
  • 2. I undertake to refrain from taking what is not given — respecting what belongs to others. The scope is broader than ordinary theft: fraud, tax evasion, misuse of public resources — and, in an expanded modern interpretation, even "stealing someone's time" through avoidable negligence in responsibilities.
  • 3. I undertake to refrain from sexual misconduct — fidelity and respect in intimate relationships. This is a foundation of trust within the family; traditions differ somewhat in their detailed interpretation, but the common core is not betraying the trust placed in us.
  • 4. I undertake to refrain from false speech — the foundation of right speech (article G.C2): truthful speech is the basis of relationships; today this includes double-dealing, misinformation, and deceptive advertising. The positive side is also important: train speech to be truthful, harmonious, gentle, and timely.
  • 5. I undertake to refrain from intoxicants — the basis of clear awareness. The special reason is that intoxication undermines self-control — the other precepts also become easier to break under its influence; today this can include addictive substances and products designed to make people "not fully awake" (and, more broadly, any form of manipulation that causes a person to lose self-command).

Ways of observing the precepts differ across traditions and cultures — something worth knowing so that the differences are not surprising: Theravāda Buddhists may formally undertake them on Uposatha observance days using the traditional wording "I undertake to refrain"; East Asian traditions may receive precepts as lifelong commitments together with practices of repentance when one falls short — recognizing what one did wrong, feeling remorse, making amends, and continuing; everywhere, teachers give beginners the same underlying message: the five precepts are five trainings — everyone starts somewhere; when you break one, see it, correct it, and continue — do not abandon all five because one was broken, nor look down on those who are still learning to keep them.

3. Everyday life of a lay Buddhist

Lay Buddhist life has no single "mandatory template" — the scriptures do not prescribe one daily schedule, and traditions and regions have different customs. It can be imagined as a menu — practices commonly found across different periods and places:

  • Morning: a short act of homage to the Buddha — offering incense (with a double meaning: reverence and a reminder to let go as the smoke rises), reciting a few familiar verses, and taking a quiet minute to set an intention for the day.
  • Visiting the temple on the full-moon and new-moon days: a particularly Vietnamese custom; listening to a Dharma talk, observing Uposatha precepts if appropriate, and meeting the community — the temple becomes a place to periodically renew one's "mental energy".
  • Making offerings and practicing generosity: offerings to the Saṅgha (the "field of merit" — article G.C4) and generosity beyond the temple — toward poor or struggling people; generosity does not require wealth — the scriptures praise even a small amount of food given with a pure mind.
  • Attending observances and festivals: major days of one's tradition — Buddha's Birthday, Ullambana / filial piety observances, and so on (festivals are presented in the festival articles for each tradition).
  • Studying the Dhamma: reading scriptures using the source-layer skills taught in the previous article, attending Dharma classes, and listening to Dharma talks online.
  • Raising a family: teaching children to greet and say thank you, sharing simple Buddhist stories, and going to the temple together; scriptures portray a good layperson as someone who "leads the whole family and relatives toward practice." The family itself is each person's "first temple".

The principle to remember — and the point this article emphasizes exactly as required by the taxonomy — is that customs differ by tradition and region; there is no rule that says "it must be done this way to be correct". Practices are means for cultivating the mind, not "assignments" to be graded; the two core standards for a lay Buddhist are simply living by ethical precepts and developing the mind — someone with few rituals who truly lives by these two has not missed the heart of the path; someone with many rituals who forgets them has lost the center.

4. Confidence and wisdom — confidence through verification

Article G.A1 promised: "confidence is the beginning of the path, while wisdom is what later confirms it" — this section develops that idea. First, there are two extremes to avoid:

  • Believe everything — easily sliding into blind faith: assuming everyone is right, being afraid to ask questions, and rejecting questions in order to "protect one's faith." This extreme leaves the path standing still — because without asking, one cannot see clearly.
  • Doubt everything — standing on one side of the river and analyzing the bridge forever without stepping onto it: rationalizing every crack, waiting for perfect evidence — while the years pass and one remains on the same bank.

Buddhism places confidence (Pali: saddhā, Sanskrit: śraddhā - confidence based on verification) between these two extremes. The root sense is "placing confidence" — confidence placed in what is trustworthy, like trusting that a bridge is sound enough to begin walking: someone who has not crossed the river must trust at least a little at first — if you wait for absolute certainty before taking the first step, no one crosses; but after walking to the middle and finding the bridge sound, confidence becomes direct knowledge: you know for yourself rather than merely believing. This is the path's cycle: enough confidence to begin → practice → experience confirms → deeper confidence through verification → continue — confidence comes first, wisdom gradually confirms it.

And there is a distinctive point in Buddhism: the Buddha himself encouraged people to verify even his own teaching. The scriptures record an instruction that disciples should examine the Teacher — observing qualities carefully — in order to develop well-founded confidence, rather than believing because they have merely heard. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 47 (Vīmaṃsaka Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]. Article G.A1 already introduced the Kālāma principle — do not accept something merely because of tradition, custom, or scripture; investigate through experience. Together, these two passages convey a central message: in Buddhism, the deepest confidence is confidence that has passed through verification — and the path is where that verification happens.

For beginners, this becomes a very practical suggestion: allow yourself to "trust provisionally in order to test" — try keeping one precept, try mindfulness for two weeks, try listening to Dharma talks for a season — like stepping onto a staircase to test its strength; then judge by the actual results — is the mind less scattered? Are you living more kindly? — and decide whether to take the next step. Keeping enough confidence to get started, while keeping enough questioning to illuminate the way — that is confidence in the Buddhist sense.

5. Summary and next steps

  • Threefold Refuge = the decision to turn toward Buddha — Dhamma — Saṅgha as three refuges; the heart is the repeated act of "going to," not the ceremony itself; unlike asking for protection, it emphasizes self-direction; it is common to all three traditions.
  • Five precepts = five commitments to "undertake to refrain" — training rather than punishment; each has a reason and a modern scope; traditions observe them differently; when one is broken, notice — correct — continue.
  • Lay life = a flexible menu: morning homage, temple visits on full-moon and new-moon days, offerings and generosity, festivals, Dharma study, and family life; customs vary by region — there is no "one correct ritual template"; the core is simply ethical conduct + mind development.
  • Confidence = confidence through verification — enough to take the first step, gradually confirmed by wisdom; the Buddha encouraged people to verify even his own conduct (MN 47); avoid the extremes of blind faith and endless skepticism.

Glossary:

  • Threefold Refuge (Pali: tisaraṇa, Sanskrit: triśaraṇa - Threefold Refuge): the three refuges — Buddha, Dhamma, and Saṅgha; also called taking refuge in the Three Jewels.
  • Three Jewels: the Buddha (awakened teacher), Dhamma (teaching), and Saṅgha (community of practitioners) — the three precious supports of Buddhism.
  • Taking refuge: the ceremony and commitment of turning toward the Three Jewels as one's refuge.
  • Refuge formula (Pali: buddhaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāmi... - I go to the Buddha for refuge): the threefold formula — "I go to the Buddha / Dhamma / Saṅgha for refuge."
  • Saraṇa (Pali - refuge): the key term underlying the concept of taking refuge.
  • Five precepts (Pali: pañcasīla - five precepts): refraining from killing, taking what is not given, sexual misconduct, false speech, and intoxicants.
  • Undertake to refrain (Pali: veramaṇī - I undertake to refrain): the phrase at the end of each precept — what makes the precepts training commitments rather than prohibitions.
  • Uposatha day (Pali: uposatha - observance day): days for observing precepts and hearing the Dhamma according to the lunar calendar (new moon, full moon, etc.).
  • Generosity (Pali: dāna - generosity): giving — the first foundation of merit; scriptures praise even small gifts given with a pure mind.
  • Repentance (recognizing that you did something wrong, feeling sorry, making amends, and sincerely deciding to change): the practice of seeing, correcting, and continuing after falling short of a precept.
  • Island / lamp (Pali: dīpa): the scriptural image "be an island unto yourself" — make yourself your own support and let the Dhamma be your lamp.
  • Confidence (Pali: saddhā, Sanskrit: śraddhā - confidence based on verification): confidence placed in what is trustworthy — the beginning of the path, gradually confirmed by wisdom.
  • Blind faith (blind faith): believing without verification and fearing questions — the extreme distinguished from confidence.
  • Direct knowledge: knowledge gained through one's own practice and experience, different from believing something because it was reported by others.
  • Four Noble Truths (Pali: cattāri ariyasaccāni - Four Noble Truths): suffering — its origin — its cessation — the path; the central framework of Buddhist teachings (article G.C1 presents it in detail).
  • Right speech (Pali: sammā-vācā - right speech): speech oriented in the right direction — truthful, harmonious, gentle, and timely; its foundation is refraining from false speech.
  • Field of merit (Pali: puññakkhetta - field of merit): the image of the Saṅgha as a fertile field in which laypeople sow merit through offerings.
