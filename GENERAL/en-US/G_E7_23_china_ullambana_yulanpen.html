G.E7.23. China: Ullambana Festival (Yúlánpén Jié)
Fifteenth day of the seventh lunar month

China's Ullambana Festival (Chinese: Yúlánpén Jié, Ullambana Festival) falls on the fifteenth day of the seventh lunar month — the same date and the same core as Vietnam's Vu Lan Festival (core article G.E7.15). The festival's name comes from the Ullambana Sūtra (Sanskrit: Ullambana Sūtra) — the sutra telling how Maudgalyāyana, one of the Buddha's two chief disciples, made offerings to the Saṅgha to rescue his mother from the realm of hungry suffering — the model of "great filial devotion" in Buddhism.

1. The observance in China

The fifteenth day of the seventh lunar month in China carries two layers: a Buddhist layer (Ullambana — offering to the Saṅgha and dedicating merit to parents and ancestors) and a folk layer (Chinese: Zhōngyuán Jié — the Zhongyuan Festival, linking Chinese ancestral veneration with outdoor rites). Chinese Buddhist monasteries hold sutra chanting and dedication of merit (transferring blessings to others), offerings to the Saṅgha, and aspirations for parents. The sutra's core — that offering made exactly in the rains-retreat season (Pali: vassa, rains retreat) carries the greatest merit because the Saṅgha has just completed the retreat — is presented fully in the core article G.E7.15 and the source sutra in article G.B7.

2. Ullambana and the Chinese "Ghost Month"

In Chinese folk culture, the seventh lunar month is called the "Ghost Month" (Chinese: Guǐ Yuè, Ghost Month) — when the gates of the underworld open and wandering spirits return to this world. This is a folk-custom layer, distinct from the foundational teaching: Buddhism does not teach a superstitious "descent of ghosts"; all Buddhist-oriented activities in this month return to filial piety — honouring parents and dedicating merit to ancestors (article G.C5).

Three layers for understanding Ullambana in China

  • Foundational meaning: the story of Maudgalyāyana rescuing his mother and the teaching on the merit of offering to the Saṅgha at the end of the retreat (articles G.B7, G.E7.15).
  • Shared practice: offering to the Saṅgha, chanting sutras and dedicating merit to parents and ancestors.
  • Custom: the folk Zhongyuan Festival, the "Ghost Month," and outdoor rites — Chinese culture, not doctrine.

3. What newcomers should know

  • Ullambana is an occasion for filial gratitude — see the Buddhist way of honouring parents in article G.E7.
  • No "ritual remedy" is needed against "ghosts": the Buddhist core is to live with filial respect and dedicate merit — without fear (article G.E6).

4. Summary and next steps

  • China's Ullambana Festival falls on the fifteenth day of the seventh lunar month — the same date as Vietnam (G.E7.15); its Chinese name is Yúlánpén Jié.
  • Two layers meet here: Buddhist (Ullambana — filial gratitude) and folk (Zhongyuan — the "Ghost Month"); read them through the three layers so as not to confuse them.

Glossary:

  • Yúlánpén Jié (Chinese, Ullambana Festival): the Chinese name for the Ullambana Festival.
  • Maudgalyāyana (Sanskrit: Maudgalyāyana; Pali: Mahāmoggallāna): one of the Buddha's two chief disciples — the central figure of the mother's-rescue story in the Ullambana Sutra (article G.B7).
  • Ullambana Sūtra (Sanskrit: Ullambana Sūtra): the Ullambana Sutra — a Mahāyāna sutra.
  • Mahāyāna Buddhism: the Buddhist tradition spreading from India to China, Japan, Korea, and Vietnam.
  • Dedication of merit: transferring the merit of wholesome deeds to others — parents and ancestors.
  • Filial piety: gratitude toward and care for parents and ancestors.
  • Zhongyuan Festival (Chinese: Zhōngyuán Jié): the Chinese folk festival on the fifteenth day of the seventh lunar month — a customary layer.
  • Ghost Month (Chinese: Guǐ Yuè): the folk name for the seventh lunar month in China — a customary layer.
