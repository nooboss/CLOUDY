G.D5.25. Laos: That Luang Festival
Full moon of the eleventh Buddhist month

The That Luang Festival is Laos's largest Buddhist festival. It is held at Pha That Luang (Lao: “Great Stupa”) in the capital, Vientiane, on the full moon of the eleventh Buddhist month—the same day as Boun Ork Phansa, which ends the rains retreat (G.D5.21), and Boun Khao Salak (G.D5.24). The festival extends over several days around the full moon and combines Buddhist rites with public cultural festivities.

1. What is That Luang, and why does it matter to Lao people?

That Luang, or Pha That Luang (Lao: “Great Stupa”), is the great stupa in Vientiane. It is a national symbol of Laos and appears on the country's coat of arms and currency. Traditional accounts associate the stupa with the time of the Buddha and say that it once enshrined relics of the Buddha (Pali: sārīra; bodily remains after cremation, G.B2). The present structure has taken its form through centuries of construction and restoration; historical details should be read with the care for degrees of certainty introduced in G.B2.

Because it is a national symbol, the That Luang Festival has a national character. It draws many thousands of people from across Laos and from Lao Buddhist communities abroad, and its ceremonies are attended by national leaders—an aspect of the cultural and social setting of Lao Buddhism.

2. How is the festival observed?

The festival's two rhythms

  • Buddhist and ceremonial rhythm: early-morning offerings to hundreds of monastics, wian tian (three circumambulations of the stupa while carrying candles, incense, and flowers, G.D5.8), Dhamma listening, and precept observance; formal services are held around the full moon.
  • Public and customary rhythm: fireworks and lantern processions, fairs, traditional games, and traditional sporting events. In rural areas, seasonal buffalo-racing festivals may also occur. These activities attract people beyond the Buddhist community.

These two rhythms should be clearly distinguished: rites at the stupa are Buddhist practice, while the festive activities belong to the cultural and social setting. Visitors can choose the rhythm that fits their purpose: attend the early services for practice, or visit the festival grounds for a cultural experience.

3. What newcomers should know

  • The festival is crowded and travel is difficult. If you are attending chiefly for the Buddhist observance, arrive early for the morning offerings and choose the full-moon day.
  • The full-moon evening wian tian around the stupa is the festival's most distinctive solemn occasion.
  • Offer according to your means, and keep personal belongings secure in the crowds.

4. Summary and next steps

  • The That Luang Festival is Laos's largest Buddhist festival, held at the Great Stupa in Vientiane on the full moon of the eleventh Buddhist month; it extends for several days around the full moon.
  • Its two rhythms should be distinguished: Buddhist observance (offerings, wian tian, and Dhamma listening) and public custom (processions and fairs).
  • Traditional accounts connect the stupa with relics of the Buddha; the present building is the result of repeated construction and restoration (G.B2).

Glossary:

  • That Luang / Pha That Luang (Lao): “Great Stupa,” the great stupa in Vientiane and a national symbol of Laos.
  • Stupa (Pali: thūpa; Sanskrit: stūpa): a monument that enshrines relics or other venerated objects for communal reverence.
  • Relics (Pali: sārīra): bodily remains after cremation that are treated as objects of veneration (G.B2).
  • Wian tian (Thai-Lao): three circumambulations of a shrine or Buddha hall while carrying candles, incense, and flowers (G.D5.8).
  • Boun (Lao): a merit-making festival; introduced in G.D5.
  • Buddhist Era (BE): a calendar era traditionally reckoned from the Buddha's final passing.

Source notes

  • The national-symbol role of That Luang and the scale of its festival are described in accounts of Lao Buddhism; these belong to the cultural and social layer.
  • The account associating the stupa with relics of the Buddha is presented as tradition, not as independently established historical fact; see G.B2 for how to read such accounts.
  • The full moon of the eleventh Buddhist month is the standard festival-date notation used in this subsection; it is not converted into a fixed Gregorian date, which varies by year.
  • The public customary festivities are distinguished from the Buddhist rites of the festival.
