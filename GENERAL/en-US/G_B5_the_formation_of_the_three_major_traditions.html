G.B5. The Formation of the Three Major Traditions

From the period of the early schools (the previous article), Buddhist traditions continued to develop and gradually converged into the three major traditions that exist today: Theravāda Buddhism, Mahāyāna Buddhism, and Vajrayāna Buddhism. This article is the "master map" for the rest of the history section: the timeline of their formation, the names used for the traditions, and three separate stories — the rise of Mahāyāna, the formation of Tantric Buddhism and Vajrayāna, and a thousand years of Buddhism's rise and decline in its Indian homeland.

Before going into detail, one important principle: the three traditions are not three separate religious institutions — they grew from the same roots, continuously interacted throughout history, and all recognize Śākyamuni Buddha and a common Buddhist foundation (articles G.C1 → G.C7). "Basic → advanced" in the app is a learning path, not a ranking: no tradition is "higher" than another.

1. The panoramic map — timeline of formation

1.1. From one source, three streams

Look at the whole journey as a timeline:

  • 5th century BCE — The Buddha taught in central India; the teachings were transmitted orally.
  • 4th–3rd centuries BCE — The early schools began to branch out (previous article); King Aśoka (Pali: Asoka) supported missionary activity, and Buddhism spread beyond India — including a mission to Sri Lanka.
  • 1st century BCE — The Pali Three Baskets (Pali: tipiṭaka) were written down in Sri Lanka; the Pali scriptural tradition took shape.
  • From around the beginning of the Common Era through the 1st century CE — the bodhisattva movement (Sanskrit: bodhisattva) and Prajñāpāramitā scriptures appeared in India; these developments gradually led to the formation of Mahāyāna Buddhism as a scriptural and doctrinal movement — developing within existing monastic institutions, not as a single "split".
  • 6th–7th centuries — Tantric Buddhism (Sanskrit: tantric Buddhism) emerged in India and developed on a Mahāyāna philosophical foundation; it spread to Tibet.
  • From the 12th–13th centuries onward — Buddhism declined in India; the three traditions continued to flourish elsewhere in Asia: Theravāda in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia, Mahāyāna in East Asia, and Vajrayāna in the Himalayas and Mongolia.

1.2. Three traditions — three scriptural streams, three cultural regions

Each tradition can be summarized as follows:

  • Theravāda Buddhism — scriptures in Pali; preserves early Buddhist teachings in one of their closest surviving forms; emphasizes gradual liberation through practice; regions: Sri Lanka, Thailand, Myanmar, Laos, Cambodia (and Theravāda communities in Vietnam). It is presented here as the direct continuation of Vibhajjavāda (previous article).
  • Mahāyāna Buddhism — scriptures originally composed in Sanskrit, transmitted extensively through Chinese and Tibetan translations; the bodhisattva ideal: awakening in order to lead all beings out of suffering; many Buddhas and bodhisattvas; rich ritual traditions; regions: China, Vietnam, Korea, Japan.
  • Vajrayāna Buddhism — Sanskrit scriptures translated into Tibetan; esoteric practices transmitted through teacher–student relationships (introduced in section 4); the spiritual teacher has a central role; regions: Tibet, Bhutan, Nepal, Mongolia, and some Russian Buddhist communities.

An important point: all three traditions share a foundational body of teachings — the Four Noble Truths, the Noble Eightfold Path, dependent origination, karma, the five aggregates, non-self, and the basic precepts. Their main differences lie in the scriptures each tradition preserves, the ideals of practice it emphasizes, and its forms of practice. These differences are compared in detail in article G.G.

2. The names — Southern, Northern, and Esoteric traditions

Vietnamese readers are also familiar with another set of names — "Southern tradition", "Northern tradition", and "Esoteric tradition" — arising from historical perspectives in Chinese and Vietnamese Buddhism. Understanding these names helps avoid confusion when reading Buddhist books:

  • Southern tradition — Buddhism transmitted southward from India: to Sri Lanka and then Southeast Asia; the corresponding traditional name is Theravāda. In the app, the preferred name is Theravāda Buddhism — because "Theravāda" identifies a specific tradition, while "Southern" describes the direction of transmission.
  • Northern tradition — Buddhism transmitted northward through Central Asia into China and then across East Asia; the dominant tradition there is Mahāyāna Buddhism.
  • Esoteric tradition — traditions emphasizing esoteric practices transmitted through teacher–student relationships; the largest surviving tradition today is Vajrayāna Buddhism in Tibet and neighboring regions.

Two notes help avoid misunderstanding. First, "Southern tradition" in Vietnam has two meanings: broadly, Theravāda Buddhism in general; more narrowly, two specific communities in Vietnam — Kinh Theravāda (Vietnamese practitioners using the Pali scriptures) and Khmer Theravāda (the Khmer community in southwestern Vietnam) — which will be distinguished in the G.D4 group. Second, Vajrayāna is not simply a "school" within Mahāyāna: it inherits the Mahāyāna philosophical foundation and adds esoteric methods, so it is commonly treated as a distinct tradition with its own characteristic path of practice. The D, E, and F groups (the G.D group, E, and F) present each tradition in turn.

3. The rise of Mahāyāna

3.1. Prajñāpāramitā scriptures and the bodhisattva ideal

From around the beginning of the Common Era, texts known as the Prajñāpāramitā scriptures (Sanskrit: prajñāpāramitā) appeared in India — scriptures presenting insight into emptiness (Sanskrit: śūnyatā). At the same time, the bodhisattva ideal became increasingly prominent — one who vows to awaken in order to help all beings out of suffering, rather than seeking liberation only for oneself. These two ideas — Prajñāpāramitā scriptures and the bodhisattva ideal — became the "two wings" of the movement called Mahāyāna Buddhism: the "Great Vehicle" that carries all beings.

Over the following centuries, Mahāyāna developed sophisticated philosophical systems: Nāgārjuna (Long Thọ, around the 2nd century CE) systematized emptiness into the Madhyamaka school (Sanskrit: Madhyamaka); Vasubandhu (Thế Thân, around the 4th–5th centuries), together with his elder brother Asaṅga (Vô Trước), systematized the Yogācāra school (Sanskrit: Yogācāra). Major Buddhist universities such as Nalanda (Nālandā) became centers of these philosophical debates.

3.2. How Mahāyāna formed — what older books often get wrong

A common account in older books — "Mahāyāna split from the Mahāsāṃghika in a particular year" — does not reflect modern scholarship accurately. A more accurate picture is:

  • Early Mahāyāna was initially a scriptural and doctrinal movement within the monastic institutions of the existing schools: people collected, copied, and recited new scriptures while the older monastic schools continued alongside them.
  • They coexisted for centuries: archaeological and textual evidence indicates that Mahāyāna and non-Mahāyāna monks could live in the same monastery for centuries while following the monastic discipline of that institution.
  • It was not a single split: there was no particular date on which "Mahāyāna was founded"; it developed gradually through new scriptures, ideals, and networks of transmission.

This helps explain why East Asian Buddhist monasteries today — including in Vietnam — can preserve the Dharmaguptaka Vinaya (previous article) while practicing according to Mahāyāna scriptures: these two layers were not inherently mutually exclusive. For the same reason, when "Theravāda" and "Mahāyāna" are presented as two opposing "schools", remember that historical boundaries were more like a continuous spectrum with many points of interaction than a wall. The rise of Mahāyāna is explored in greater detail in the articles on this tradition (article G.E1).

4. The formation of Tantric Buddhism and Vajrayāna

Around the 6th–7th centuries, a new layer of practices known as Tantric Buddhism appeared in India — methods transmitted through direct teacher–student relationships, because they were regarded as too advanced to be learned from books alone. Its characteristic elements include:

  • Mantra (Sanskrit: mantra) — short phrases recited repeatedly to collect the mind and support practice.
  • Maṇḍala (Sanskrit: maṇḍala) — sacred circular diagrams of Buddhas and bodhisattvas used in visualization practices.
  • Iṣṭadevatā (Sanskrit: iṣṭadevatā) — a chosen Buddha or bodhisattva form used as the focus of visualization and practice.
  • Abhiṣeka (Sanskrit: abhiṣeka) — an initiation ritual through which an esoteric practice is transmitted from teacher to student.

The key point is that Tantric Buddhism did not replace the Mahāyāna foundation; it developed on Mahāyāna philosophy (emptiness, the bodhisattva path). Esoteric practices were presented as a "swift path" for practitioners with sufficient preparation. From India, Tantric Buddhism spread in two directions: eastward into East Asian esoteric traditions (Shingon in Japan and esoteric practices in China — article G.E4), and northward into the Himalayas, where it combined with local Buddhist traditions and developed into Vajrayāna Buddhism — the tradition covered in the G.F group (article G.F1).

5. Buddhism in India — a thousand years of rise and decline

5.1. The high point

From around the 3rd century BCE through the 7th–8th centuries, Buddhism was one of the major cultural forces in India: supported by powerful kingdoms (beginning with Magadha under Aśoka), home to great universities such as Nalanda (the Buddhist university introduced in section 3) with thousands of students, and supported by intellectual centers where Buddhist thinkers studied and debated other schools; Buddhist art, architecture, and literature also flourished. It was from this Indian foundation that Buddhist traditions spread to neighboring countries (next article).

5.2. Decline

In the centuries that followed, Buddhism in India gradually declined for several overlapping reasons:

  • Loss of patronage: later dynasties shifted support toward other traditions; Buddhism — which depended heavily on large monasteries — lost resources needed to sustain them.
  • Philosophical competition: revitalized Brahmanical schools (especially Advaita Vedānta) developed influential philosophical systems; major Buddhist thinkers faced strong criticism from Brahmanical scholars.
  • Absorption: Buddhism — centered largely on monastic communities — did not maintain as strong a popular ritual layer as Hindu traditions later did, and gradually lost presence among the wider population.
  • Warfare: Turkic invasions in the 12th–13th centuries destroyed major universities (Nalanda was destroyed around 1193); central monasteries collapsed, breaking important lines of transmission.

Because monasteries were the "heart" of Indian Buddhism, when major monastic institutions collapsed, Buddhism nearly disappeared from its own homeland for many centuries. This is why Buddhism in India today is the religion of only a small minority — an apparent paradox that often surprises beginners.

5.3. A limited revival in the 19th–20th centuries

In the 19th–20th centuries, Buddhism in India experienced a partial revival through several channels: archaeologists and scholars restored Buddhist sacred sites; traditions from outside India returned to build temples at pilgrimage centers; and especially the movement of B. R. Ambedkar — a social reformer who led millions of people from communities historically treated as "untouchable" to take refuge in Buddhism in 1956, viewing Buddhism as a way out of caste-based injustice (discussed in article G.B1). Buddhism in India today — small but growing — consists mainly of these newer communities and centers of international Buddhist traditions.

6. Summary and next steps

  • The three traditions developed over time: the Pali scriptural tradition took shape (1st century BCE) → Mahāyāna emerged (beginning of the Common Era) → Tantric Buddhism and Vajrayāna developed (6th–7th centuries).
  • Names: Southern tradition = Theravāda; Northern tradition = Mahāyāna; Esoteric tradition = Vajrayāna — each name has a different scope and should be used carefully.
  • Mahāyāna did not simply "split off" from one school: it began as a scriptural and doctrinal movement within existing monasteries and coexisted with the early schools for centuries.
  • Tantric Buddhism developed on a Mahāyāna foundation and was transmitted through teacher–student relationships (mantra, maṇḍala, iṣṭadevatā, abhiṣeka).
  • Indian Buddhism reached a high point over roughly a thousand years, then declined because of lost patronage, philosophical competition, absorption, and warfare — nearly disappearing after the 12th–13th centuries and experiencing a limited revival in the 19th–20th centuries.
  • The three traditions are not three separate religious institutions — they share common roots, continuously interacted, and share a foundational body of teachings.

Glossary:

  • Theravāda Buddhism: the tradition using the Pali Canon, widespread in Sri Lanka, Thailand, Myanmar, Laos, and Cambodia; often called the Southern tradition.
  • Mahāyāna Buddhism: the "Great Vehicle" tradition emphasizing the bodhisattva ideal, widespread in East Asia; often called the Northern tradition.
  • Vajrayāna Buddhism: the tradition combining the Mahāyāna foundation with esoteric methods, widespread in Tibet, the Himalayas, and Mongolia; often called the Esoteric tradition.
  • Prajñāpāramitā scriptures (Sanskrit: prajñāpāramitā): a group of scriptures presenting insight into emptiness, forming a major Mahāyāna foundation.
  • Emptiness (Sanskrit: śūnyatā): the absence of a fixed, independent essence in phenomena; things arise in dependence on conditions.
  • Bodhisattva (Sanskrit: bodhisattva): one who seeks awakening with the vow to lead all beings out of suffering.
  • Madhyamaka (Sanskrit: Madhyamaka): the philosophical school associated with Nāgārjuna that systematized the teaching of emptiness.
  • Yogācāra (Sanskrit: Yogācāra): a philosophical tradition analyzing experience through the "three natures" of mind and phenomena.
  • Nalanda (Nālandā): one of ancient India's greatest Buddhist universities, active from around the 5th to the 12th centuries and a major center of Mahāyāna philosophy; destroyed around 1193.
  • Tantric Buddhism: a body of practices transmitted through teacher–student relationships, including mantra, maṇḍala, and deity visualization.
  • Mantra (Sanskrit: mantra): a short phrase recited repeatedly to collect the mind and support practice.
  • Maṇḍala (Sanskrit: maṇḍala): a sacred diagram, often circular, depicting Buddhas and bodhisattvas for visualization practice.
  • Iṣṭadevatā (Sanskrit: iṣṭadevatā): a chosen Buddha or bodhisattva form used as the focus of visualization and practice.
  • Abhiṣeka (Sanskrit: abhiṣeka): an initiation ritual for transmitting an esoteric practice from teacher to student.
  • Advaita Vedānta: a revitalized Brahmanical philosophical school that competed with Buddhist philosophical traditions during the period of decline.
  • Ambedkar (B. R. Ambedkar): an Indian social reformer whose 1956 Buddhist conversion movement involved millions of people.
