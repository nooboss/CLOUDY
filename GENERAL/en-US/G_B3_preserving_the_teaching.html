G.B3. Preserving the Teaching

For 45 years the Buddha taught the Dharma but did not write down a single line. The entire body of teaching — thousands of discourses and hundreds of rules of monastic practice — was preserved by one main method: memorization and oral transmission, from teacher to student, for four centuries after his passing. This article explains that system and the Buddhist councils — gatherings representing the community to establish and review the authoritative wording of the teaching — and why, today, we have multiple collections of scriptures that are similar in their core but differ in details.

1. The problem: preserving the teaching before written texts

The Buddha entered final passing at age 80, leaving behind a rapidly growing community and a vast body of teaching. An immediate question arose: how could the Dharma be kept intact across generations? The scriptures record that on the very day of the funeral procession, an elderly monk said what many others may have been thinking: "Friends, stop grieving — now we are free; from this point on, we can do whatever we want." This statement led the elder Mahākassapa — the leading figure in the first council tradition — to organize a gathering to review the teaching before it could become disordered. [Source: Vinaya Piṭaka, Mahāvagga (account of the First Buddhist Council) - Theravāda Buddhism].

The problem was not simple. In that world, written texts were not yet a dependable medium — the Vedas of Brahmanism (discussed in article G.B1) had already been preserved for centuries through priestly memory alone; writing existed, but it was expensive and not widespread. So the Buddhist community adopted the same basic method: a living storehouse of memory, in which different groups of disciples specialized in particular collections, while regular communal recitations checked the wording line by line.

2. Oral transmission — how could so much be remembered?

It may sound hard to believe, but the oral system of the early Buddhist community was highly sophisticated, and it worked well enough that modern research finds many early features preserved in collections written down centuries later. The main elements were:

  • Division of responsibility for memorizing texts: different groups of disciples specialized in different collections — those who learned the Majjhima Nikāya preserved the Majjhima, those who learned the Aṅguttara Nikāya preserved the Aṅguttara — like specialized "living libraries."
  • Summary verses: each discourse had a short verse summarizing its content and sometimes listing speakers, questioners, or listeners — like a table of contents that helped check order and prevent omissions.
  • Repetitive structure: discourses used rhythmic recurring formulas — lists, question-and-answer patterns, and similar opening scenes — making them easier to memorize and helping communities detect deviations.
  • Collective recitation: group recitations used a back-and-forth method, with one person reciting and another checking, so that every line was heard by more than one person; if someone recited differently, the group corrected it immediately.
  • The rains retreat: during the three rainy months, monastics stayed together in one place, creating an especially suitable period for memorization and study.

Another point is important: teachings were transmitted in Pali (a language close to the early oral tradition) and in other local languages; and, as the previous article noted, the Buddha encouraged people to preserve and teach the Dharma in their own languages. Early Buddhist teaching was therefore not necessarily a fixed text in a single language. This feature — even more than the mechanics of memorization — helps explain why later collections bear the marks of different regions and periods.

3. The First Buddhist Council at Rājagaha

About three months after the Buddha's final passing, at Rājagaha (Rajgir), the capital of Magadha, around 500 monastics were convened by the elder Mahākassapa, under the patronage of King Ajātasattu, to hold the First Buddhist Council: the entire body of teaching was recited to establish its authoritative wording. According to tradition, two figures played central roles:

  • Mahākassapa asked about the rules of monastic practice, and Upāli, the specialist in discipline, answered: the contents of the Vinaya Piṭaka were established through this question-and-answer process.
  • Ānanda, the Buddha's attendant for decades and a man of extraordinary memory, recited the discourses: the contents of the Sutta Piṭaka were established from his recitation. Each discourse begins with the familiar phrase "Thus have I heard" (Pali: evaṃ me sutaṃ) — in the council tradition, because Ānanda was the one presenting the teachings he had heard.

An important point about reading history: the story of the First Council is how Buddhist traditions narrate the establishment of their scriptural collections. Details such as the number of participants and the exact roles assigned to each elder vary, while the surviving sources were written down centuries later; modern researchers therefore treat this as a form of communal memory, not as an administrative meeting record. What is more secure is that the teaching was transmitted orally and that communal recitation and checking appeared very early.

4. The Second Buddhist Council at Vesālī

About 100 years after the Buddha's final passing, a major dispute over monastic discipline arose at Vesālī. Monastics from the Vesālī group were practicing ten points that much of the community regarded as violations — such as keeping money for personal use, eating after noon, drinking processed fermented beverages, and repeatedly seeking offerings in the same day. An assembly of elders from several regions was convened; after reviewing the Vinaya, the ten points were declared to be violations. [Source: Vinaya Piṭaka, Cullavagga (account of the Second Buddhist Council) - Theravāda Buddhism].

The historical significance of the Second Council does not lie simply in the ruling on the ten points — but in what followed: the Vesālī group did not accept the decision; according to some traditions, they held a separate recitation of their own scriptures — the first sign that the Buddhist community had begun to divide over authoritative texts. This marked the beginning of the period of schisms, the subject of the next article.

5. The Third Buddhist Council under King Aśoka

Around the 3rd century BCE, under King Aśoka (Pali: Asoka, Sanskrit: Aśoka) — the Buddhist ruler of the Magadha empire — another council is recorded: at Pāṭaliputra, the elder Moggaliputta Tissa presided, and the Kathāvatthu (Points of Controversy) was added to the Abhidhamma. This council is presented as having an important role in clarifying orthodox teaching within the lineage that later became Theravāda Buddhism — and it is also associated with a major event: sending missionary delegations abroad, including a mission to Sri Lanka (the next article).

The degree of certainty also needs to be noted: the Third Council is described in greatest detail by Theravāda traditions, while East Asian traditions do not tell the story in the same way; modern researchers therefore treat it as communal memory preserved within the Theravāda lineage. For this article, the more important point is the overall picture: during the first roughly 200 years, the community held repeated collective recitations and reviews, each in a different historical setting.

6. Writing the scriptures down in Sri Lanka

The final milestone — and one with decisive consequences — came in 1st-century BCE Sri Lanka: during a period of famine and warfare, monastics feared that the scriptures might be lost as the people who had memorized them died. The community at Mahāvihāra in Anurādhapura therefore decided to write the entire Pali Canon (Pali: tipiṭaka) down — on palm leaves — for the first time in its history. [Source: Mahāvaṃsa, chapter 33 - Theravāda Buddhism].

From this point onward, the written Pali collection — Vinaya, Sutta, and Abhidhamma — had a more stable physical form that could survive war, epidemics, and generations. Meanwhile, the scriptural collections of other lineages — in Sanskrit and various Prakrit languages — were often not preserved in written form or were later lost through subsequent upheavals; today we know them through Chinese and Tibetan translations and manuscripts found in Central Asia. This difference in "storage conditions" — rather than a difference in "accuracy" — helps explain why the Pali Canon is the most completely preserved early Buddhist collection available to us today (see article G.D2).

7. Why are there parallel collections of scriptures?

Putting the milestones together answers a major question: why do Buddhist traditions have scriptural collections that share a common core but differ in details? The answer lies in the very mechanism of oral transmission:

  • A shared origin: Buddhist lineages all descend from oral transmissions rooted in the regions of central India; core teachings — the Four Noble Truths (suffering – origin – cessation – path), the Middle Way, and major discourses — were stabilized through repeated communal recitation.
  • Different details: each oral lineage operated in a different region, language, and setting; openings, lists of listeners, and wording gradually diverged — like different retellings of the same story.
  • Independent development: after the lineages divided (next article), each developed and added discourses of its own; today's collections therefore contain both common and distinctive layers.

Understanding this mechanism can make beginners calmer when reading scriptures: encountering two versions that are not identical does not automatically mean that "the scripture was altered"; it can be the natural trace of centuries of oral transmission. When major differences appear — especially in doctrine — the sources should be layered and compared (article G.A2).

8. Summary and next steps

  • The early Buddhist teachings were preserved through oral transmission — specialized memorization, summary verses, repetitive structures, and collective recitation and checking.
  • First Buddhist Council (Rājagaha, soon after the Buddha's final passing): Upāli presented the Vinaya, Ānanda the discourses; the phrase "Thus have I heard" is associated with this tradition.
  • Second Buddhist Council (Vesālī, about 100 years later): ten disciplinary points were rejected; this became an early sign of the community's division.
  • Third Buddhist Council (under Aśoka, around the 3rd century BCE): associated in Theravāda tradition with clarifying orthodox teaching, adding the Kathāvatthu, and sending missionary delegations abroad.
  • 1st century BCE: the Pali Canon was written down in Sri Lanka — the most completely preserved early Buddhist collection available to us today.
  • Scriptures that share a core but differ in detail are a natural trace of oral transmission, not automatically evidence that a text was "tampered with."

Suggested next reading: the next article, Early Schisms, continues the story from the disagreement at Vesālī: over the following 2–3 centuries, the Buddhist community developed into dozens of schools — and one of those lineages, under Aśoka's patronage, helped carry Buddhism beyond India. To see how the Pali Canon preserved through this process is structured, read The Pali Canon — a Scriptural Map (article G.D2); to learn how to read scriptures and distinguish levels of evidence, return to How to Read Buddhist Scriptures and Texts (article G.A2).

Glossary:

  • Buddhist council: a communal gathering convened to establish or review the authoritative wording of Buddhist teachings; commonly called a "council" in English-language books.
  • Oral transmission: preserving and passing on texts through memory and recitation rather than relying on written manuscripts.
  • Vinaya Piṭaka: the section of the canon containing monastic rules and regulations governing the ordained community.
  • Sutta Piṭaka: the section of the canon containing the Buddha's discourses and dialogues with disciples and others.
  • Abhidhamma: analytical teachings concerning mind, phenomena, and causal relations, systematized later than the Vinaya and Sutta collections.
  • "Thus have I heard" (Pali: evaṃ me sutaṃ): the conventional opening of Buddhist discourses, associated with the tradition of Ānanda reciting the teachings at the First Council.
  • Rains retreat (Pali: vassa): the three-month rainy-season period when monastics remain in one place for practice and study.
  • The ten points of Vesālī: ten practices declared violations by the Second Council, including keeping money and eating after noon.
  • Kathāvatthu (Pali: Kathāvatthu): the Points of Controversy, a work addressing disputed doctrinal positions and traditionally associated with the Third Council and the Abhidhamma.
  • Three Baskets (Pali: tipiṭaka): the Buddhist canon — Vinaya Piṭaka, Sutta Piṭaka, and Abhidhamma Piṭaka.
  • Mahāvaṃsa (Pali: Mahāvaṃsa): the Great Chronicle of Sri Lanka, a major historical chronicle preserving an account of Buddhist history on the island.
