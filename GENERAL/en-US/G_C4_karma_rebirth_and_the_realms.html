G.C4. Karma, Rebirth, and the Realms

The previous article ended with the "formations" link of Dependent Origination — activities that leave imprints. This opens the door to one of the best-known — and most misunderstood — subjects in Buddhism: karma (Pali: kamma, Sanskrit: karma). This article explains what karma means, how rebirth works without an "unchanging soul," how the map of the realms can be read on two levels, and what practices such as making merit and dedicating merit mean.

1. What is karma — getting it right

The classical definition is surprisingly concise: "It is intention that I call karma." [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 6.63 (Nibbedhika Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]. Intention (Pali: cetanā) is the core: karma is not destiny, not fate, not wealth or social status — it is intentional action together with its natural results. Karma operates through three doors: body (action), speech (words), and mind (thoughts and intentions).

Because intention is central, the same outward act can have different karmic significance depending on the mind: accidentally causing someone to fall and deliberately tripping someone may look similar from the outside, but the karma is very different. For this reason, the texts call living beings "heirs of their karma" — each person owns the actions they themselves have done; no one can carry them for another, and no one can receive their results on another's behalf. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 135 (Cūḷakammavibhaṅga Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism].

Karma works as a natural law, not as a reward-and-punishment system: wholesome actions leave wholesome tendencies and bear pleasant results; unwholesome actions leave unwholesome tendencies and bear painful results — like seeds sprouting according to what was planted, not according to someone's personal decision. Karmic results may mature in this life, in the next life, or in a later life — like different seeds having different harvest times. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 3.58 - Theravāda Buddhism].

2. Common misunderstandings and practical questions

Before going further, a few misunderstandings need to be cleared away — because they can cause people to abandon both Buddhist teaching and ethical life:

  • "Karma = a predetermined fate." The decisive mistake is the assumption that past karma fixes the future. Present intention is each person's "share of freedom" — someone who has done harmful things can still change direction now; the texts tell of the murderer Aṅgulimāla, who had killed many people but changed his life within that same lifetime and attained liberation as a noble disciple (mentioned in article G.B2). [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 86 (Aṅgulimāla Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]. Conversely, someone with much past merit can still undermine their future through unwholesome conduct.
  • "Karmic results are punishment from someone." The mistake is imagining a judge behind the process. No one hands out the result and no one cancels it — results arise naturally from actions, like water overflowing from a full vessel.
  • "Then why do good people experience bad circumstances?" The texts answer this in two ways. First, karma is only one stream of causes among many: illness, weather, accidents, care, and so on (article G.A1 cited this discourse). [Source: Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 36.21 (Sīvaka Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]. Second, the results of old and new karma mature at their own times — a difficulty experienced today may be the result of an older seed, while a wholesome seed just planted may take longer to mature. In short: hardship is not evidence that life is meaningless, and wholesome action is never pointless.

One more note about the quality of actions: the texts distinguish wholesome, unwholesome, and indeterminate actions. The standard for "wholesome" is practical: an action that, when remembered later, leaves one at ease and free from remorse, and does not bring suffering to oneself or others.

3. Rebirth — without an unchanging soul

Karma can produce results across time — including beyond death. This cycle of birth and death is called saṃsāra (Pali: saṃsāra, Sanskrit: saṃsāra). But who "goes through" those lives if Buddhism does not accept an unchanging soul? This is the key question — and the texts offer a famous image: one candle lighting another. The second flame burns because of the first — it is neither exactly the same flame nor completely unrelated to it; likewise, a later life arises because of this life — a continuing stream, without a fixed entity traveling from one life to another. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 38 (Mahātaṇhāsaṅkhaya Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism].

Seen through Dependent Origination (the previous article), what "passes through" lives is not a self, but a stream of consciousness and karmic imprints continuing through conditions — like ocean waves: one wave depends on the previous wave, but no "lump of water" travels from one crest to the next. Thus rebirth and non-self are not contradictory — non-self denies a fixed entity, not continuity.

One point is answered differently by different traditions: is there an intermediate state (Sanskrit: antarābhava) between death and rebirth? Some early schools and Vajrayāna traditions affirm such a state and describe it in detail; the Theravāda tradition, based on the Pāli Canon, does not incorporate it into its scriptural system. This is a typical example of a principle already encountered in the G.B group: the same question can receive different answers in different schools — and the app presents them comparatively.

4. The six realms and the Buddhist cosmology

To describe the full range of modes of existence within saṃsāra, Buddhist traditions use a map of six realms:

  • Hell realm: an existence dominated by intense suffering.
  • Hungry ghost realm (Pali: peta): an existence of endless lack — always wanting, never having enough.
  • Animal realm: an existence dominated by instinct and limited freedom.
  • Asura realm (Sanskrit: asura): an existence of rivalry and conflict without end.
  • Human realm: a balanced existence — pleasant enough that one is not completely overwhelmed by pain, yet difficult enough to reveal suffering — so the human realm offers especially broad conditions for practice.
  • Heavenly realms: the long-lasting happiness of devas (Pali: deva) — beings with great merit but still within saṃsāra (article G.A1 discussed devas).

The six realms are grouped into three realms: the realm of sensuality (still attached to sensual pleasures — from the hells through part of the heavenly realms), the form realm (more refined — associated with the meditative absorptions), and the formless realm (where existence is associated with formless meditative attainments). Traditional Theravāda commentaries elaborate this into a detailed 31-realm model.

The map should be read carefully: alongside the cosmological level (realms described by the scriptures — a matter of belief received differently across traditions), explanatory texts also use a psychological and lived-condition level: when anger burns inside, the mind is in its own "hell"; when craving is never satisfied, that resembles the "hungry ghost" mode; a comfortable life supported by merit resembles a present "heavenly realm." The second level does not replace the first — but it makes the map close to everyday life rather than a distant cosmic diagram.

Beyond the six realms, Mahāyāna traditions also speak of Pure Lands — purified realms associated with the vows and virtues of Buddhas. The most famous is the Pure Land of Amitābha Buddha, where practitioners aspire to be reborn so that continuing the path becomes more favorable (presented separately in article G.E4).

5. Merit, fields of merit, and dedication of merit

Karma is not merely a theory of warning — it is also the reason for wholesome practices. The tradition summarizes three bases of merit (Pali: puññakiriyavatthu): generosity (Pali: dāna), keeping precepts, and mental cultivation (Pali: bhāvanā). [Source: Dīgha Nikāya, DN 33 (Saṅgīti Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism].

Several related ideas go with them:

  • Field of merit (Pali: puññakkhetta): the Saṅgha — a community living according to the Dharma — is compared to a field; offerings planted in this "field" bear merit, like seeds planted in good soil. This is why generosity and offerings are closely connected with monastic life (group G.D3).
  • The giver's mind affects the result of the merit: the texts analyze how the same offering, given with a pure and trusting mind at the right time, differs in karmic result from an offering made casually or for display. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 142 (Dakkhiṇāvibhaṅga Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]. This is why it is important to distinguish genuine merit-making from "buying merit" — giving money as a transaction to obtain whatever one wants; genuine merit lies in the mind of giving, not in the receipt.
  • Dedication of merit (Pali: pattidāna): after making merit, one dedicates the merit to others — loved ones, the deceased, or all beings. This practice appears in all three major traditions: Theravāda has texts instructing practitioners to share merit with deceased relatives; Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna traditions commonly include dedication at the end of chanting and ritual practices. [Source: Khuddakapāṭha, Sutta 7 (Tirokuḍḍa Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism].

The tradition summarizes: merit and wisdom are like two wings — one wing is wholesome action that creates merit, the other is wisdom that sees clearly; without one wing, the path cannot rise. (This wording is common across traditions; a related saying about "two things that lead to happiness" — merit and wisdom — appears in the Aṅguttara Nikāya.)

6. Summary and next steps

  • Karma = intentional action (body — speech — mind) and its natural results; intention is the core; it is a natural law, not a reward-and-punishment system.
  • Past karma does not fix the future — present intention is one's "share of freedom"; good people can experience difficult circumstances because karma is only one stream of causes, and results mature at different times.
  • Rebirth does not require an unchanging soul — like one flame lighting another: continuity connects the lives; the existence of an intermediate state is answered differently by different schools.
  • Six realms — three realms — 31 realms: a map of modes of existence that can be read both cosmologically and as a psychological/lived-condition image; Pure Lands belong to the Mahāyāna stream.
  • Three bases of merit: generosity — keeping precepts — mental cultivation; the giver's mind affects the result; dedication of merit is found across all three traditions; merit and wisdom are like two wings.

Suggested next reading: if karma is action, then who is the "actor"? If there is no unchanging self, what is the "I" we experience each day made of? Those questions lead to the next article — the five aggregates and non-self: analyzing experience into five groups and seeing that the "self" is a composite process rather than a separate entity. This is one of the deepest — and easiest to misunderstand — teachings in Buddhism, so read it slowly.

Glossary:

  • Karma (Pali: kamma, Sanskrit: karma): intentional action of body, speech, and mind together with its natural results; not fate and not punishment imposed by a being.
  • Intention (Pali: cetanā): the intention to act — the core of karma; the texts identify intention as the essential element of karma.
  • Body — speech — mind: the three doors through which karma operates.
  • Wholesome / unwholesome / indeterminate: the three qualities of actions; the practical standard for "wholesome" is that remembering the action leaves one at ease and does not cause suffering.
  • Saṃsāra (Pali and Sanskrit: saṃsāra): the continuing cycle of birth and death; the Sanskrit term suggests ongoing "wandering on."
  • Rebirth: the arising of a new life continuous with the preceding one, without a fixed entity traveling from one life to another.
  • Intermediate state (Sanskrit: antarābhava): a state between two lives; traditions differ on whether and how it exists.
  • Hungry ghost (Pali: peta): a realm characterized by endless lack; as a psychological image, craving that is never satisfied.
  • Asura (Sanskrit: asura): a realm characterized by rivalry and conflict.
  • Devas (Pali: deva): celestial beings with great merit who are still within saṃsāra.
  • Three realms: the realm of sensuality — form realm — formless realm, the three broad divisions of saṃsāra.
  • Merit (Pali: puñña): the wholesome result of beneficial actions; one of the two "wings" of the path.
  • Generosity (Pali: dāna): giving; the first base of merit.
  • Keeping precepts: observing ethical commitments; the second base of merit.
  • Mental cultivation (Pali: bhāvanā): training the mind; the third base of merit, from a root meaning "to bring into being or cultivate."
  • Field of merit (Pali: puññakkhetta): the image of the Saṅgha as good soil in which merit is planted.
  • Dedication of merit (Pali: pattidāna): directing or sharing the merit of one's wholesome actions with others; practiced across all three major traditions.
  • Monk (Pali: bhikkhu): an ordained male monastic; the female form is bhikkhunī.
