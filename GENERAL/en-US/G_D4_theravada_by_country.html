G.D4. Theravāda by Country

The preceding articles introduced Theravāda as a tradition (article G.D1), its scriptures (article G.D2), and its path of practice and daily life (article G.D3). This article follows that picture into particular countries. Each section uses the same frame for comparison: how Theravāda arrived, how the Saṅgha is organized, characteristic forms of practice, and distinctive contributions. The historical sequence begins with Sri Lanka, then Thailand and Myanmar, Laos and Cambodia, and finally Vietnam, home to both Kinh and Khmer Theravāda communities.

1. Theravāda in Sri Lanka

1.1. The cradle: from Mahinda to Kandy

Sri Lanka is where Theravāda developed as a continuous and fully established tradition. The principal milestones were introduced in G.D1: the mission of Mahinda and Saṅghamittā arrived during the reign of King Devānaṃpiya Tissa, around the third century BCE; the Mahāvihāra at Anurādhapura became a principal monastic center; the Pāli Canon was committed to writing in the first century BCE; and Buddhaghosa systematized the commentarial heritage in the fifth century CE. Later capitals included Polonnaruwa and Kandy. Kandy is associated with the Buddha's tooth relic (Pali: sarīra), whose custody was traditionally linked with legitimate kingship. In 1753, ordination was renewed with help from Siam, giving rise to the Siyam Nikāya.

1.2. The Saṅgha today and its relation to the nation

Sri Lanka's monastic community is principally organized through the Siyam Nikāya, with its Asgiriya and Malwatte chapters in Kandy, and the Amarapura and Rāmañña Nikāyas, which developed in the nineteenth century through Burmese ordination lineages. They share the same Vinaya while differing chiefly in ordination history and customary organization. Buddhism remains deeply bound to the country's public and historical life. After the severe effects of colonial rule, the late-nineteenth-century revival associated with figures such as Anagarika Dharmapāla helped renew both local confidence and international connections.

1.3. Characteristic practice and contribution

Stupa pilgrimage is a central feature of Sri Lankan Buddhist life. The ancient stupas of Anurādhapura remain major pilgrimage sites, while the island's chronicles, the Dīpavaṃsa and Mahāvaṃsa, are indispensable historical sources. On the Poson full moon, usually around June, Buddhists commemorate Mahinda's arrival through pilgrimage to Mihintale. Sri Lanka's most enduring contribution is its preservation, writing down, and transmission of the Pāli Canon: it became a foundational archive for the whole Theravāda world and an important source for modern global Buddhism.

2. Theravāda in Thailand

2.1. From Sukhothai to the present

Theravāda reached the Thai region through early Mon communities and became the principal tradition under Sukhothai in the thirteenth century, continuing through the Ayutthaya period. Across these eras, royal patronage has been especially important: kings supported monasteries, helped maintain ordination lineages, and acted as guardians of the Saṅgha. The ideal of a Buddhist ruler has therefore remained influential in Thai public culture.

2.2. Two orders and a national Saṅgha

Thailand has two principal monastic orders. The Mahānikāya is the larger order, continuing older lineages. The Dhammayuttika Nikāya, a reform order initiated by King Mongkut in the nineteenth century, emphasizes close study and observance of the Vinaya. The Supreme Patriarch (Pali: Sangharāja) heads the national Saṅgha, and Buddhist universities including Mahachulalongkorn and Mahamakut train monastics.

2.3. Practice and global influence

The morning alms-round (Pali: piṇḍapāta) remains a familiar part of village life, with the monastery serving as a communal center. Temporary ordination for several weeks or for a rains retreat is also a widespread custom among Thai men, often understood as a form of filial devotion. Thailand's forest tradition, represented by masters such as Ajahn Mun and Ajahn Chah, stresses simplicity, meditation, and disciplined life. Through Ajahn Chah's students and affiliated monasteries, this tradition has had a major influence beyond Thailand.

3. Theravāda in Myanmar

3.1. Bagan and a tradition of renewal

Theravāda became Myanmar's principal tradition under King Anawrahta of Bagan in the eleventh century, traditionally associated with the monk Shin Arahan. Bagan's many temples and stupas still witness to that period. Myanmar also played a recurrent role in sustaining ordination lineages elsewhere: when Buddhism declined in India and Sri Lanka's ordination lineage weakened, Burmese monastics helped renew it. For this reason, Myanmar is often remembered as a keeper of the tradition's flame.

3.2. Making meditation accessible

Myanmar's distinctive modern contribution has been to make meditation accessible to laypeople. Beginning with Ledi Sayādaw, teachers presented insight meditation (Pali: vipassanā) in Burmese for ordinary practitioners. In the twentieth century, Mahāsi Sayādaw established residential meditation centers; the line associated with U Ba Khin and S.N. Goenka spread ten-day courses internationally; and Pa Auk Sayādaw maintained a strong concentration-meditation tradition (Pali: samatha). Myanmar also has a prominent culture of Abhidhamma study.

3.3. Everyday Buddhist life

Observing the eight precepts (Pali: aṭṭhasīla) on regular observance days is especially common, often as a lifelong lay practice. The shinbyu novice-ordination ceremony gives boys an opportunity to experience monastic life. Myanmar has passed through profound political changes in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries; throughout them, monasteries and meditation centers have continued to sustain Buddhist life.

4. Theravāda in Laos and Cambodia

4.1. Laos

Theravāda reached Laos during the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries and became closely associated with the kingdom of Lan Xang. That Luang in Vientiane, the country's great relic stupa, is a national symbol. The annual rhythm is marked by Boun, Lao merit-making festivals. After periods of restriction in the twentieth century, monasteries and offering customs gradually revived from the 1990s onward.

4.2. Cambodia

Cambodia gradually became predominantly Theravāda during the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. Village monasteries long served as centers of religious life, education, and seasonal festivals. Under the Khmer Rouge from 1975 to 1979, temples were closed and the Saṅgha was nearly destroyed. Its subsequent re-establishment and the reopening of monasteries form one of the most significant chapters of contemporary Cambodian Buddhism.

5. Theravāda in Vietnam

5.1. Kinh Theravāda

Vietnamese Theravāda includes Kinh Theravāda monasteries, followed chiefly by ethnic Vietnamese and formed in the early twentieth century, and Khmer Theravāda monasteries in the southwest. Kinh Theravāda is associated in local historical accounts with Bửu Quang Monastery in Saigon and its successors. Chanting is conducted in Vietnamese and Pāli; alms-rounds and observance days are visible features, as are the dark-yellow wrapped robes characteristic of Theravāda monastics. The precise early history should be checked against primary Vietnamese sources before being stated in greater detail.

5.2. Khmer Theravāda

Khmer Theravāda monasteries serve Khmer communities in the Mekong Delta, including Trà Vinh, Sóc Trăng, and Cần Thơ. The monastery is a center of village life: children may learn Khmer there, monastics go on alms-round, and chanting uses Khmer and Pāli. Chôl Chnăm Thmây (Khmer New Year) and Ok Om Bok (the Moon Festival) are well-known observances, while the rains retreat (Pali: vassa) is a major monastic season.

5.3. A major contribution

Vietnamese Theravāda's most important contribution to the Vietnamese-speaking world is Venerable Thích Minh Châu's Vietnamese translation of the Pāli Canon. It has made primary Theravāda texts accessible to generations of Vietnamese readers and is an important translation source for this section.

6. Summary and next steps

  • Sri Lanka is the cradle of the written Pāli Canon and of a continuing Theravāda heritage.
  • Thailand combines royal patronage, national Saṅgha organization, village monasticism, and an internationally influential forest tradition.
  • Myanmar is known for preserving ordination lineages, Abhidhamma learning, and the worldwide spread of lay meditation.
  • Laos and Cambodia show the central role of village monasteries and the resilience of Buddhist communities through modern upheaval.
  • Vietnam includes Kinh and Khmer Theravāda communities, together with a major Vietnamese translation of the Pāli Canon.

Glossary:

  • Relics (Pali: sarīra): revered remains of the Buddha or awakened disciples; the tooth relic at Kandy is a symbol of Sri Lankan kingship.
  • Stupa (Sanskrit: stūpa): a monument that enshrines relics or commemorates the Buddha.
  • Mahāvihāra (Pali: Mahāvihāra): the Great Monastery at Anurādhapura, a foundational institution of Theravāda.
  • Siyam Nikāya: a major Sri Lankan monastic order renewed in 1753 through ordination from Siam.
  • Mahānikāya and Dhammayuttika Nikāya: Thailand's two principal monastic orders.
  • Supreme Patriarch (Pali: Sangharāja): the head of Thailand's national Saṅgha.
  • Forest tradition: monastic lineages that emphasize seclusion, meditation, and strict discipline.
  • Shinbyu: the Burmese ceremony through which a boy temporarily becomes a novice monk.
  • Boun (Lao): a merit-making festival.
  • Chôl Chnăm Thmây: Khmer New Year.
  • Ok Om Bok: a Khmer Moon Festival, often associated with boat racing.
