G.H2. Index of Schools, Sub-Schools, and Lineages

The previous article — G.H1 — listed the scriptures; this article is an index of the early schools, doctrinal schools, traditions, and lineages of Buddhism — from the early schools (Sarvāstivāda) to the Mahāyāna schools (Sanskrit: Mahāyāna - English: Great Vehicle) of East Asia and Japan (Nichiren), together with the schools of Vajrayāna Buddhism (Sanskrit: Vajrayāna - English: Diamond Vehicle) and the lineages found in Vietnam. Each entry is a single line: name, period, region, and central tenet. It opens with the most important part of any index: distinguishing "early school," "doctrinal school," "tradition," "lineage," and "saṅgha organization" — five terms that are often confused. Details for each group are in the history articles: G.B5 (early schools), G.E4 (East Asian – Japanese schools), G.F2 (Tibetan schools), G.B7 (Vietnam).

1. "Early school," "doctrinal school," "tradition," "lineage," "saṅgha organization" — distinguishing five terms

Five words, five different concepts — confusing them is the source of most misunderstandings about "Buddhist schools":

1.1. Early school

  • Meaning: the early monastic groups that separated mainly over details of discipline and modes of exposition after the communal recitations — differing in texts and customs, not yet "schools" in the later sense
  • Examples: Sarvāstivāda, Mahāsāṃghika (article G.B5)

1.2. Doctrinal school

  • Meaning: the "specializations" formed in East Asia — each school goes deep into one scriptural corpus and practice, yet they share the same discipline, recite together, and do not exclude one another (article G.E4)
  • Examples: Chan school, Pure Land school, Tiantai school

1.3. Tradition

  • Meaning: the great schools of Vajrayāna Buddhism — differing in transmission and practice systems, while sharing the same tantric foundation
  • Examples: Nyingma, Kagyu, Sakya, Gelug (article G.F2)

1.4. Lineage

  • Meaning: a teacher-to-student chain transmitting one specific practice across generations, which may run across traditions
  • Examples: the Mahāmudrā lineage, the Nyingma terma lineages

1.5. Saṅgha organization

  • Meaning: a modern administrative organization (19th–20th centuries onward) — not a "school" in the traditional sense
  • Examples: the Vietnam Buddhist Sangha, national saṅgha bodies (article G.B7)

A short way to remember: early school = monastic communities that separated; doctrinal school = specializations within one community; tradition = a practice stream with its own transmission; lineage = the teacher-to-student thread; saṅgha organization = the modern administration. The question "which school do you belong to?" at a Vietnamese temple usually means in practice "which practice does this temple specialize in?".

2. Early schools (c. 3rd century BCE – 5th century CE)

After the communal recitations, the Saṅgha divided into early schools — the ancient records list 18 to 20 schools depending on the source (article G.B5); the table below selects those that shaped later texts and traditions:

2.1. Sthaviravāda (Sanskrit: Sthaviravāda)

  • Main regions: central India → Sri Lanka
  • In one line: preserving and deepening the early teaching, with emphasis on monastic life
  • What remains: continued as Theravāda Buddhism (Pali: Theravāda - English: Way of the Elders)

2.2. Mahāsāṃghika (Sanskrit: Mahāsāṃghika)

  • Main regions: central – eastern India
  • In one line: a more expansive form of organization; many records regard it as one root of the Mahāyāna current
  • What remains: traces left in texts preserved in the Chinese canon (article G.E3)

2.3. Sarvāstivāda (Sanskrit: Sarvāstivāda - English: the "everything exists" school)

  • Main regions: northern India, Central Asia
  • In one line: rich scholastic philosophy — "all factors exist" in the sense of the three times; a partial foundation for the later Theravāda Abhidhamma borrowings
  • What remains: treatises preserved in the Chinese canon; influence on Abhidhamma (article G.D2)

2.4. Dharmaguptaka (Sanskrit: Dharmaguptaka)

  • Main regions: northern India, Central Asia
  • In one line: this school's discipline — the Four-Part Vinaya — became the ordination Vinaya for East Asian monastics to this day
  • What remains: monastics at Vietnamese – Chinese – Japanese temples still receive ordination under this Vinaya (article G.B4)

2.5. Mahīśāsaka (Sanskrit: Mahīśāsaka)

  • Main regions: central India
  • In one line: a branch of the Sthavira line; this school's Vinaya was also translated into Chinese
  • What remains: its Vinaya text in the Chinese Tripiṭaka

2.6. Vātsīputrīya (Sanskrit: Vātsīputrīya)

  • Main regions: central India
  • In one line: the position of "something that remains" (pudgalavāda — the inexpressible person) — famous in the debates
  • What remains: known only through records of the debates

2.7. Sautrāntika (Sanskrit: Sautrāntika - English: "those who follow the sūtras")

  • Main regions: northern India
  • In one line: placing the sūtras above the treatises — in contrast to the scholasticism of the Sarvāstivāda
  • What remains: a dialogue partner in later Mahāyāna treatises

Note: the boundaries between the early schools were mainly matters of discipline and scholastic philosophy, not of the goal — all aimed at liberation according to the early teaching; the "deviant views" named in ancient records are internal labels, not condemnations. The list of 18–20 schools varies by source (Vasumitra, Bhavya...); the table above gives its most stable part.

3. Chinese schools

The eight great schools formed around the 6th–9th centuries (article G.E4) — sharing one Vinaya foundation and one Saṅgha:

3.1. Tiantai school (Chinese: Tiantai)

  • Period / founder: 6th century, Zhiyi
  • In one line: placing the Lotus Sūtra at the summit; the "five periods – eight teachings" system; calm-and-insight meditation
  • Today: still living; great influence through Japan (Tendai)

3.2. Huayan school (Chinese: Huayan)

  • Period / founder: 7th century, Fazang
  • In one line: the worldview of "one in all, all in one"; the non-obstruction of principle and phenomena
  • Today: its thought lives on through Chan and ritual life

3.3. Sanlun school (Chinese: Sanlun)

  • Period / founder: 6th century, Jizang
  • In one line: the dialectic of "emptiness — and the emptiness of emptiness" after Madhyamaka
  • Today: lives on through Chan and Tibetan thought

3.4. Faxiang school (Chinese: Faxiang; also called the Consciousness-Only school)

  • Period / founder: 7th century, Xuanzang – Kuiji
  • In one line: analysis of the eight consciousnesses; the table of one hundred factors
  • Today: strongly revived through modern Consciousness-Only studies

3.5. Chan school (Chinese: Chan)

  • Period / founder: 6th century, Bodhidharma
  • In one line: "point directly at the human mind, see nature and become a buddha"
  • Today: the most globally influential school

3.6. Pure Land school (Chinese: Jìngtǔ)

  • Period / founder: 4th–5th centuries (the Mount Lu assembly), fully formed in the 6th–7th centuries
  • In one line: reciting the name of Amitābha Buddha, aspiring to rebirth in the Land of Ultimate Bliss
  • Today: the most widespread practice in East Asia — often cultivated together with Chan

3.7. Vinaya school (Chinese: Lǜzōng)

  • Period / founder: 7th century, Daoxuan
  • In one line: specializing in the study and observance of the Vinaya
  • Today: a specialist "school" — with no separate saṅgha organization

3.8. Zhenyan school (Chinese: Zhēnyán — East Asian Esoteric Buddhism)

  • Period / founder: 8th century, developed in Japan by Kūkai
  • In one line: the three mysteries (body – speech – mind) in correspondence, the maṇḍala (article G.F1 — distinct from Tibetan Vajrayāna)
  • Today: strongly alive in Japan (Shingon); faded in China after 845

4. Japanese schools

Japan received these teachings and "popularized" them (article G.E5):

4.1. Tendai

  • Founder: Saichō (9th century), from Tiantai
  • In one line: the "hundred teachings" — a framework holding all practices

4.2. Shingon

  • Founder: Kūkai (9th century), from Zhenyan
  • In one line: attaining buddhahood in this very body through the three mysteries

4.3. Jōdo-shū (Pure Land)

  • Founder: Hōnen (12th–13th centuries)
  • In one line: Buddha-recitation as the main practice — "single recitation" (senju nembutsu)

4.4. Jōdo Shinshū (True Pure Land school)

  • Founder: Shinran (13th century)
  • In one line: entrusting faith — a clergy that may marry — differing from the traditional renunciant Saṅgha model

4.5. Rinzai (Linji Chan / Zen)

  • Founder: Eisai (12th–13th centuries)
  • In one line: investigating kōans — the direct strike

4.6. Sōtō (Caodong Chan / Zen)

  • Founder: Dōgen (13th century)
  • In one line: silent illumination — just sitting (shikantaza)

4.7. Nichiren

  • Founder: Nichiren (13th century)
  • In one line: chanting the title of the Lotus Sūtra (daimoku) as the sole practice

5. Vajrayāna schools

The four schools of Tibetan Buddhism, sharing one tantric foundation (article G.F2):

5.1. Nyingma (Tibetan: nying ma - English: the Ancient Ones)

  • Period / origins: from Padmasambhava, 8th century
  • Core practices: Great Perfection (dzogchen); the terma lineages
  • Recognizable figure: the red hats (original usage, not a "red/white" color code)

5.2. Kagyu (Tibetan: bka' brgyud - English: the Oral Lineage)

  • Period / origins: Marpa – Milarepa, 11th century
  • Core practices: Great Seal (Mahāmudrā); the Six Dharmas of Nāropa
  • Recognizable figure: the Karmapa

5.3. Sakya (Tibetan: sa skya - English: Grey Earth — the color of its main monastery)

  • Period / origins: 1073, the Khön family
  • Core practices: Lamdré ("the path with its result")
  • Recognizable figure: abbots in family succession

5.4. Gelug (Tibetan: dge lugs - English: the Virtuous Way)

  • Period / origins: Tsongkhapa, 14th–15th centuries
  • Core practices: lamrim; strict discipline; monastic colleges
  • Recognizable figure: His Holiness the Dalai Lama; the yellow hats

Four more entries often met: Bön — the indigenous Tibetan tradition predating Buddhism, developing alongside it, not a Buddhist school; Rimé — the 19th-century non-sectarian movement, not founding a new school (article G.F2); Newar Buddhism in Nepal — an Indian Vajrayāna lineage surviving in place (article G.F4); and Shingon already listed in section 4 — sharing the East Asian esoteric source with the Chinese Zhenyan school.

6. Lineages in Vietnam

6.1. Northern-tradition monasteries — the majority

  • Base tradition: East Asian Mahāyāna Buddhism
  • In one line: combined Chan – Pure Land practice; Sino-Vietnamese liturgy (article G.E6)

6.2. Trúc Lâm

  • Base tradition: Mahāyāna Buddhism — a purely Vietnamese Chan lineage
  • In one line: founded by King Trần Nhân Tông (13th century); strongly revived in the 20th century through Most Ven. Thích Thanh Từ (article G.B7)

6.3. Ethnic Vietnamese Theravāda

  • Base tradition: Theravāda Buddhism
  • In one line: Vietnamese monastics following the Pali Tipiṭaka; Theravāda liturgy

6.4. Khmer Theravāda

  • Base tradition: Theravāda Buddhism
  • In one line: the Khmer community of the Mekong Delta — the temple as the center of village life (article G.D4)

6.5. Plum Village

  • Base tradition: Mahāyāna Buddhism — a modern Chan lineage
  • In one line: rooted in Zen Master Thích Nhất Hạnh; an international community (article G.B7)

And a separate note on an easily confused word: "saṅgha organization" — bodies such as the Vietnam Buddhist Sangha are modern administrative structures gathering many lineages; not to be confused with a traditional "school." The map "entering a Vietnamese temple: recognizing the lineage by statues, rites, and robe colors" is in article G.B7.

7. Summary and next steps

  • Five concepts — early school / doctrinal school / tradition / lineage / saṅgha organization — do not use them interchangeably; each table above is one layer.
  • The early schools differed mainly in discipline and scholastic philosophy; the East Asian schools are specializations within one Saṅgha; the Tibetan schools are practice streams with their own transmissions.
  • This index is a reference map — not a ranking: every entry has its own article going deeper (articles G.B5, G.E4, G.E5, G.F2, G.F4, G.B7, G.D4) and all can be compared through the three-traditions table (article G.G1).

Glossary:

  • Early school: the early monastic groups that separated after the communal recitations — mainly over details of discipline and modes of exposition.
  • Sthaviravāda (Sanskrit: Sthaviravāda): the early school continuing as Theravāda Buddhism (Pali: Theravāda - English: Way of the Elders).
  • Mahāyāna Buddhism (Sanskrit: Mahāyāna - English: Great Vehicle): the tradition centered on the bodhisattva ideal, widespread in East Asia.
  • Vajrayāna Buddhism (Sanskrit: Vajrayāna - English: Diamond Vehicle): the tantric tradition of the Himalayan – Mongolian region.
  • Sarvāstivāda (Sanskrit: Sarvāstivāda - English: "everything exists" school): the great scholastic school of northern India — "all factors exist" in the sense of the three times past – present – future.
  • Dharmaguptaka (Sanskrit: Dharmaguptaka): the school whose Vinaya (the Four-Part Vinaya) became the standard ordination Vinaya of East Asian monastics.
  • Vātsīputrīya (Sanskrit: Vātsīputrīya): the school holding the inexpressible person (pudgalavāda) — famous in the early debates.
  • Sautrāntika (Sanskrit: Sautrāntika - English: those who follow the sūtras): the school placing the sūtras above the treatises.
  • Three mysteries (Chinese: sānmì - English: three mysteries): body – speech – mind of the tantric practitioner in correspondence with the body – speech – mind of the deity; a characteristic term of East Asian Esoteric Buddhism.
  • Maṇḍala (Sanskrit: maṇḍala - English: mandala): the sacred cosmic diagram — the central means of tantric practice.
  • Terma (Tibetan: gter ma - English: hidden treasure): texts revealed by tertöns at the right moment — characteristic of the Nyingma lineage.
  • Great Perfection (Tibetan: rdzogs chen / dzogchen - English: Great Perfection): the ultimate practice of the Nyingma — direct recognition of the nature of mind.
  • Great Seal (Sanskrit: Mahāmudrā - English: Great Seal): the ultimate practice of the Kagyu — recognizing mind through direct pointing.
  • Lamrim (Tibetan: lam rim - English: stages of the path): the graduated-path system of the Gelug school.
  • Lamdré (Tibetan: lam 'bras - English: the Path with its Result): the core practice of the Sakya school.
  • Daimoku (Japanese: daimoku - English: the title): chanting "Homage to the Lotus Sūtra" — the practice of the Nichiren school.
  • Shikantaza (Japanese: shikantaza - English: just sitting): the sitting meditation of the Sōtō lineage — sitting is just sitting, aimed at nothing else.
  • Rimé (Tibetan: ris med - English: non-sectarian movement): the non-partisan movement — studying the practices of all schools, founding no new school.
  • Bön: the indigenous Tibetan tradition, developing alongside Buddhism — not a Buddhist school.
