1. The four main schools of Tibetan Buddhism

The schools of Tibetan Buddhism are not competing "churches": they keep the same Vinaya and recognise the same Kangyur–Tengyur canon (article G.F3), differing in the lineage received from India, their central practices, and their founding figures. The four main schools are:

  • Nyingma (Tibetan: nyingma): "the ancient school": stemming from the first dissemination (8th century — article G.F1), honouring Padmasambhava as founder; preserving its own corpus of root tantras (article G.F3). Its highest practice: Great Perfection (Tibetan: dzogchen) — direct recognition of the natural state of mind.
  • Kagyu (Tibetan: kagyu): "oral lineage school": arising from Marpa–Milarepa (11th century — article G.F1), emphasising oral transmission — teachings passed directly from teacher to student through practice. Its highest practice: Great Seal (Sanskrit: Mahāmudrā) — recognising the nature of mind. The Karma Kagyu branch carries the Karmapa line — as section 6 will explain, the oldest tulku lineage.
  • Sakya (Tibetan: sakya): formed around the Khön family with Sakya Monastery (1073 — article G.F1); transmission passed hereditarily from father to son; known for balancing scholarship and practice. Its central teaching: Lamdré (Tibetan: lam 'bras): "path with its fruit" — a path inseparable from its result.
  • Gelug (Tibetan: gelug): "the virtuous school": the latest (14th–15th centuries), founded by Tsongkhapa (1357–1419), inheriting Atiśa's Kadam lineage (article G.F1); known for a rigorous scholastic curriculum — decades of studying Vinaya, Madhyamaka, Yogācāra, and Prajñāpāramitā through debate — before tantric practice; its synthesis: the lamrim (Tibetan: lam rim): "stages of the path" works modelled on Atiśa's "Lamp for the Path to Enlightenment." The Dalai Lama line belongs to this school.

Two accompanying notes: (1) in the 19th century, the Rimé movement (Tibetan: rime): "non-sectarian" arose in Kham — scholars of different schools collecting, studying, and preserving one another's teachings, resisting sectarian exclusion; the Rimé spirit remains alive in many colleges. (2) Bön (Tibetan: bön) — Tibet's indigenous tradition, formed before and alongside Buddhism — is a separate tradition, not a "school" of Buddhism; although it uses many similar forms (mantras, turning wheels, monastic community), it has its own origins and canon. When reading sources, do not list Bön among the Buddhist schools.

2. The spiritual teacher — lama, guru, and lineage

2.1. Why the teacher is central

In Vajrayāna, lineage (lineage, the unbroken teacher-to-student transmission — article G.F1) is the living condition of a practice: a practice is "effective" only when transmitted through a line of real people. The holder of that transmission is the lama (Tibetan: bla ma; Sanskrit: guru) — not necessarily an "elder monk": a lama may be monastic or lay (like Marpa); what matters is having received the lineage and being qualified to pass it on. Because the teaching passes through people, the teacher–student relationship is seen as the "lifeblood" of the tradition: the student aspires to see the teacher as the embodiment of the path followed — a deeper and closer bond than ordinary relationships.

2.2. Healthy boundaries — important for beginners

Precisely because the teacher holds a central place, newcomers need to distinguish a healthy tradition from a distorted system. This part should be read carefully before committing to any teacher, in any tradition (general principles — see article G.M080 on lineage):

  • Signs of trustworthiness: a clear lineage that can be traced; teachings that accord with the scriptures (you can cross-check, and the teacher encourages questions and verification); teachings not tied to expensive fees or demands for wealth; respect for students' privacy, family, and decisions; an open community — visitors welcome, free to leave; a teacher who lives simply and in line with what is taught.
  • Warning signs: demanding isolation of students from family and friends; demanding absolute secrecy; demanding money for "permissions," "charms," or "quick help"; excessive personality cult — the teacher can never be wrong, no one may ask questions; using psychological pressure or threats of "karmic consequences" to retain people; making unverifiable claims of special privilege. If you meet these signs, step away and seek advice from another trustworthy Buddhist community.

What is presented here are general principles — neither praising nor disparaging any particular individual; and note: distorted systems can arise in any tradition, not only Vajrayāna.

3. What is empowerment

Empowerment (Sanskrit: abhiṣeka): a ritual "authorisation" performed by a lineage-holding teacher to permit and activate a student's practice of a tantric method — the familiar image: being handed the "key" to a door. It should be understood correctly: empowerment is not a mystical blessing that can be bought — it is the establishment of a lineage connection among practitioner, practice, and lineage; after the ritual, the practitioner receives practice instructions and undertakes commitments (depending on the practice). There are different levels of empowerment according to levels of teaching: broadly, vase empowerment (authorising entry-level practice — "body") and higher empowerments linked to deeper levels of the teaching — their number and names vary by tantra system (article G.F3 discusses the tantra levels).

Why do many practices require empowerment beforehand? Because tantric methods use powerful imagery and methods (sections 4, 5) — practising without guidance easily leads astray; empowerment is the tradition's way of ensuring the traveller already has a guide. A common question: "if I attend a ceremony, may I receive a public empowerment?" — yes: public empowerments are open for the community to attend; newcomers receive them as a blessing of connection and orientation — but for genuine practice, detailed instructions must be sought from the teacher or an authorised appointee; do not practise on your own from written materials after a single "receiving." 4. Mantra, mandala, and yidam — seeing them with understanding

The three "esoteric means" named in article G.F1 — here understood as symbolic language, not magic:

  • Mantra (Sanskrit: mantra): sacred syllables linked to a practice — like "action through sound": familiar rhythmic sound helps unify the mind, and the mantra's content declares a quality. The world's best-known mantra — OṂ MAṆI PADME HŪṂ — is the six-syllable mantra of Avalokiteśvara (Sanskrit: Avalokiteśvara): "the one who hears the cries of the world" (article G.E2): the six syllables are linked to the six realms (six realms) (article G.C4) — each recitation turns the mind toward compassion for all realms; its literal sense suggests "oṃ — the jewel upon the lotus" — the image of a pure mind amid the world.
  • Mandala (Sanskrit: maṇḍala): a sacred cosmogram: a "map" of a pure world — central figure, surrounding figures, gates, colours — drawn in coloured sand, painted, or constructed by hand in visualisation. Used as a map of the mind: visualising entry into the mandala trains seeing mind and world in a pure order; sand mandalas created painstakingly over many days are then carefully swept away — a lesson in impermanence (article G.C5) taught without words.
  • Yidam (Sanskrit: iṣṭadevatā, Tibetan: yidam): tutelary deity: a buddha or bodhisattva chosen as the focus of personal visualisation — an enlightened image to "identify with": the yidam's forms (many arms, many faces, implements, garments, ornaments...) are symbols of qualities (wisdom, compassion, power to cut through afflictions...), not descriptions of a "being." Visualising the yidam is practising "seeing what one can become" — in the spirit of Buddha-nature (Sanskrit: tathāgatagarbha) already studied in article G.E2.

All three share the same principle: symbolic language for the teaching — sound, image, diagram — not magic; and all require guidance (section 3). Detailed practice belongs to the app's Meditation section (articles G.M074, G.M075).

5. The path of practice in Vajrayāna

A map-level picture of the path — following the shared framework of the schools (practice details belong to the Meditation section — articles G.M143, G.M144, G.M077 → G.M079):

  • Foundational reflections: the four thoughts that turn the mind — precious human life (rare conditions for practice), impermanence, karma and the realms, suffering of saṃsāra (Pali: saṃsāra): "cycle of rebirth" (articles G.C4, G.C5) — giving rise to resolve to enter the path; this framework inherits lamrim (section 1).
  • Preliminary practices (Tibetan: ngöndro): the foundational block — taking refuge (taking refuge) and bodhicitta (Sanskrit: bodhicitta): the mind of enlightenment for self and others (articles G.A3, G.E2), purification practice linked to Vajrasattva (Sanskrit: Vajrasattva), mandala offering, and guru yoga (guru yoga — practice of devotion to the teacher) — often in fixed numbers (for example 100,000 repetitions of each) to go deep enough before moving to higher levels.
  • Yidam practice (section 4): choosing a yidam under a teacher's guidance, practising according to transmission.
  • Two stages of higher tantric practice: generation stage (Sanskrit: utpattikrama): visualising oneself as the yidam and mandala) and completion stage (Sanskrit: saṃpannakrama): penetrating the nature of body and mind through methods involving "channels" and breath); the two stages are two halves of the same practice — appearing and penetrating.
  • Culminating practices: in each lineage a different summit — Great Seal (Mahāmudrā) of Kagyu, Great Perfection (dzogchen) of Nyingma — all pointing to direct recognition of the nature of mind (article G.E2 — Buddha-nature) beyond all concepts.

The common point to see: this path does not replace the shared foundation — refuge, precepts, bodhicitta, meditation — but builds upon it in clear stages, with a guiding teacher (section 2) and empowerment at each gate (section 3).

6. Tulku — the reincarnation system, Dalai Lama and Karmapa

6.1. What is a tulku

Tulku (Tibetan: sprul sku): "reincarnate lama" is a distinctive organisational feature of Tibetan Buddhism: when an influential teacher passes away, the tradition searches for a child born afterward — seen as continuing the teacher's work — to educate and continue the lineage. In the traditions' own understanding: the teacher "reincarnates" to continue aspirational vows; in academic description: a institution for selecting and educating religious leaders formed from specific historical needs — the two views can be placed side by side, as practised in article G.B4.

6.2. History: from Karmapa to Dalai Lama

The tulku system began with the Karmapa — head of the Karma Kagyu branch: the first Karmapa died in the late 12th century, his successor recognised in the early 13th century — the oldest tulku lineage; its emblem is the Black Crown (Tibetan: shwa nag): "the Black Crown" — traditionally "self-arisen" (self-arisen, not crafted by anyone). Later, many lines established their own tulku systems. The Dalai Lama belongs to the Gelug school: the title granted from the 16th century to this school's tulku line; the Fifth Dalai Lama (17th century) joined Tibet's political authority to this office; the Fourteenth Dalai Lama — recognised in 1937 — after 1959 established the exile community in India (article G.F4) and received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1989. Under the Qing (18th century), the Chinese court instituted the "golden urn" procedure (golden urn lottery — drawing name tablets from a golden urn) for recognising major tulkus — a historical detail needed when reading differing sources on the present (article G.F4).

6.3. Modern questions

The tulku system today faces real questions: a child recognised as a tulku — how to educate while both preserving lineage and protecting childhood and the right to choose one's life? Recognition of major tulkus is entangled with history and politics (the next Dalai Lama, the next Karmapa — with differing claims). This page presents a map-level view; modern issues in detail are in article G.F4, written neutrally under editorial principles — not taking a side.

7. Prayer flags, prayer wheels, and butter lamps

The three objects most associated with Tibetan Buddhism — understanding them reveals this culture's spirit of "something always reciting":

  • Prayer flag (prayer flag): strips of cloth printed with mantras and images, hung in strings on passes, temple roofs, and bridges. The colours of the five strips in order — blue, white, red, green, yellow — correspond to the elements (sky – wind – fire – water – earth). The principle: wind passing through the sacred words carries the mantra's "echo" far and wide — the merit of recitation spreads with nature; flags faded by sun and rain are replaced — hanging flags is sowing aspirations, not decoration.
  • Prayer wheel (prayer wheel): a rotating cylinder containing printed mantras (above all OṂ MAṆI PADME HŪṂ — section 4) — from small hand-held wheels to tent-sized pillars. The principle: one turn equals reciting the mantras inside the wheel — one walking around a monastery or turning a hand wheel is "reciting" in that way; etiquette: turn clockwise, not backward to "go faster."
  • Butter lamp (butter lamp): small lamps burning butter as fuel, offered before altars and around monasteries. Meaning: light of wisdom dispelling the darkness of ignorance (Pali: avijjā) (article G.A1) — offering a lamp is the wish "may brightness arise in the mind," and a form of light offering; in Tibetan temples, hundreds of butter lamps burning together is the most familiar scene.

And a few dos and don'ts for visitors to monasteries: walk around sacred structures clockwise; ask permission before photographing people and rituals; keep quiet in shrine halls; do not climb on, touch, or turn sacred structures and objects backward; do not take sand from mandalas or objects in shrines as souvenirs.

8. Summary and next steps

  • Four schools — Nyingma (ancient, Padmasambhava, dzogchen), Kagyu (oral lineage, Mahāmudrā, Karmapa), Sakya (Khön family, Lamdré), Gelug (Tsongkhapa, lamrim, Dalai Lama); the 19th-century Rimé movement; Bön is a separate tradition, not counted within Buddhism.
  • Lama and lineage — the lifeblood of practice; a deep teacher–student bond; know healthy boundaries (clear lineage, accord with scriptures, no fees, respect for privacy) and warning signs.
  • Empowerment — a ritual "authorisation to practise," establishing a lineage connection; not a blessing that can be bought; public attendance is possible, but practice requires instruction.
  • Mantra – mandala – yidam — three symbolic means (sound – diagram – image), not magic; OṂ MAṆI PADME HŪṂ — the six syllables of Avalokiteśvara.
  • Path of practice — foundational reflections → ngöndro → yidam → generation/completion two stages → culminating practices (Mahāmudrā / dzogchen); built on refuge – precepts – bodhicitta.
  • Tulku — the "reincarnate teacher" institution (Karmapa — 13th century; Dalai Lama — Gelug school); two views: traditional and academic; modern questions placed side by side neutrally.
  • Prayer flags – prayer wheels – butter lamps — a culture of "recitation in motion": wind carries mantras, turning is reciting, light of wisdom; visitors: go clockwise, ask permission before photographing.

Glossary:

  • Vajrayāna Buddhism (Sanskrit: Vajrayāna): the esoteric Buddhist tradition of the Himalayan region and Mongolia; an esoteric current within Mahāyāna Buddhism (Sanskrit: Mahāyāna).
  • Tantra (Sanskrit: tantra): the name for esoteric scriptures and esoteric practices (article G.F1).
  • Lineage: the unbroken teacher-to-student transmission — the living condition of tantric practice.
  • Lama (Tibetan: bla ma; Sanskrit: guru): spiritual teacher — one who has received the lineage and is qualified to pass it on; may be monastic or lay.
  • Empowerment (Sanskrit: abhiṣeka): a ritual transmission "authorising practice" of a method — establishing a connection among practitioner, practice, and lineage.
  • Vase empowerment: entry-level empowerment — authorising practice of the initial stages; higher empowerments belong to deeper levels of the tantra system.
  • Mantra (Sanskrit: mantra): sacred syllables — "action through sound"; OṂ MAṆI PADME HŪṂ — the six-syllable mantra of Avalokiteśvara, linked to the six realms.
  • Mandala (Sanskrit: maṇḍala): a sacred cosmogram — a "map of mind and of a pure world"; sand mandalas are created and then swept away — a lesson in impermanence.
  • Yidam (Sanskrit: iṣṭadevatā, Tibetan: yidam): a buddha or bodhisattva chosen as the focus of personal visualisation — an enlightened image to "identify with."
  • Vajrasattva (Sanskrit: Vajrasattva): the yidam linked to purification practice — within the preliminary practices.
  • Ngöndro (Tibetan: ngöndro): the block of preliminary practices — refuge, bodhicitta, purification, mandala offering, guru yoga (guru yoga), often in fixed numbers.
  • Generation stage (Sanskrit: utpattikrama): visualising oneself as the yidam and mandala; completion stage (Sanskrit: saṃpannakrama): penetrating the nature of body and mind.
  • Great Seal (Sanskrit: Mahāmudrā): the summit practice of the Kagyu lineage — direct recognition of the nature of mind.
  • Great Perfection (Tibetan: dzogchen): the summit practice of the Nyingma lineage — direct recognition of the natural state of mind.
  • Nyingma (Tibetan): "the ancient school" — from the first dissemination; Kagyu (Tibetan): "oral lineage school" — from Marpa–Milarepa; Sakya: from the Khön family (1073); Gelug (Tibetan): founded by Tsongkhapa (14th–15th centuries).
  • Lamdré (Tibetan: lam 'bras): the central teaching of Sakya — "path with its fruit."
  • Lamrim (Tibetan: lam rim): "stages of the path" works modelled on Atiśa's "Lamp for the Path to Enlightenment" — the synthesis teaching of Gelug.
  • Rimé (Tibetan: rime): the 19th-century "non-sectarian" movement — studying and preserving the teachings of all schools, opposing exclusion.
  • Bön (Tibetan: bön): Tibet's indigenous tradition formed before and alongside Buddhism — a separate tradition, not a Buddhist school.
  • Tulku (Tibetan: sprul sku): a "reincarnate" teacher recognised to continue a lineage; in traditional terms — continuing aspirational vows; in academic terms — an institution for selecting and educating religious leaders.
  • Karmapa: head of the Karma Kagyu branch — the oldest tulku lineage (beginning in the 13th century); emblem of the Black Crown.
  • Dalai Lama: the tulku line of the Gelug school, titled from the 16th century; the 14th Dalai Lama — exile in 1959, Nobel Peace Prize 1989.

  • Prayer flag (prayer flag): cloth strips printed with mantras — wind carries the mantra's "echo" far and wide; five colours blue – white – red – green – yellow correspond to the elements.
  • Prayer wheel (prayer wheel): a rotating cylinder containing mantras — one turn equals reciting; turn clockwise.
  • Butter lamp (butter lamp): a lamp offered before altars — the light of wisdom dispelling ignorance.
