G.B7. Buddhism in Vietnam

Buddhism reached Vietnam very early — from the first centuries CE, by both sea and land — and over two thousand years it became part of Vietnamese cultural, ethical, and linguistic life. This article tells that story in four stages: the early period (the two routes of transmission), the Lý–Trần period (its high point and the Trúc Lâm Zen tradition), the early modern and modern periods (from decline to revival movements and today's Vietnam Buddhist Sangha), and a map of the Buddhist traditions present in Vietnam today — with a few "recognition tips" for when you enter a Vietnamese temple.

1. Buddhism arrives in Vietnam — from the first centuries CE

1.1. Two routes of transmission

Unlike most countries, Buddhism reached Vietnam through two routes at the same time:

  • Overland, from the north: Indian and Central Asian Buddhist traditions traveled along the Silk Road into China, then moved south into Jiaozhi — northern Vietnam today — through overland trade routes and coastal routes.
  • By sea, from the south: Indian merchants and monks traveled the maritime routes between India and China, stopping at ports along the Vietnamese coast; Luy Lâu (now in Bắc Ninh) — a major trading hub — became the earliest major center of Buddhism.

Because of these two origins, Vietnamese Buddhism very early took on both strands: one connected with Indian Buddhist schools (and later with Theravāda through Southeast Asia), and another connected with Chinese Mahāyāna. This coexistence is a distinctive feature of Vietnamese Buddhism — unlike Sri Lanka (predominantly Theravāda) or Japan (predominantly Mahāyāna).

1.2. Early traces and figures

Early evidence includes both historical records and traditional stories, which need to be read with different levels of certainty:

  • Luy Lâu (2nd–3rd centuries): among the earliest surviving temple and stupa remains; associated with Indian monks such as Moubo (Mouzi in Chinese sources) — traditionally credited with composing Lihuolun — an early Chinese-language Buddhist work associated with Vietnam, using argument to defend Buddhism against Confucianism and Daoism.
  • Vinitaruci and Pháp Hiền (late 6th century): the Indian meditation master Vinitaruci arrived in Jiaozhi, and his Vietnamese disciple Pháp Hiền became the first Vietnamese disciple associated with the lineage — beginning the oldest recorded meditation lineage in Vietnam.
  • Vô Ngôn Thông (8th century): an Indian monk who came to Jiaozhi and gave his name to another meditation lineage — one of the major traditions before the Lý dynasty.
  • Local traditions — such as the stories surrounding Dâu Pagoda (Luy Lâu) and the Luy Lâu water legends — show that Buddhism had entered popular culture very early.

A note on certainty: archaeological remains (Luy Lâu, Dâu Pagoda) and names recorded in historical sources provide relatively firm evidence; legendary details (the contents of stories and miraculous episodes) should be read according to the layered-source approach discussed in article G.B2.

2. The Lý–Trần period: royal patronage and the Trúc Lâm Zen tradition

2.1. Buddhism under the Lý

By the Lý dynasty (1010–1225), Buddhism had become a de facto state religion: Lý kings supported monks at court, used them as advisers, built temples throughout the country (including some famous temples that still survive), and brought Buddhism into court ritual life. Emperor Lý Nhân Tông established examinations for monks — examinations selecting educated practitioners — a distinctive Vietnamese development. Intellectually, this was also the period when organized meditation lineages took shape: the Vinitaruci and Vô Ngôn Thông traditions, both maintaining continuous lineages for centuries. Buddhist literature also became an important stream: the poetry and writings of monks and Buddhist rulers of the Lý–Trần period laid the foundation for Vietnamese Buddhist literature (most prominently in the works of Trần Nhân Tông and the Trúc Lâm tradition; see 2.2).

2.2. Trúc Lâm — a Zen tradition founded by a king

Under the Trần dynasty (1225–1400), the most significant development was the Trúc Lâm Zen tradition, founded by Trần Nhân Tông (1258–1308) — the king who had twice defeated Mongol forces — after he abdicated and entered monastic life. Trúc Lâm was not simply imported from abroad; it was a synthesis of earlier meditation traditions, Vietnamese cultural life, and Trần Nhân Tông's own spiritual journey. Trần Nhân Tông expressed a distinctive ideal — "Cư trần lạc đạo phùng thời thích chí" — practicing in the midst of ordinary life: not by fleeing into the mountains, but by living and working in the world without clinging.

Trúc Lâm developed an institutional system: monasteries at Yên Tử — where Trần Nhân Tông practiced and passed away — together with temples and a lineage transmitted through disciples such as Pháp Loa and Huyền Quang. After the Trần period, Trúc Lâm declined as an independent Zen lineage, but the ideal of "Cư trần lạc đạo" and the name "Trúc Lâm" continued in later revival movements (section 4).

3. The early modern and modern periods: from decline to revival

3.1. Decline and preservation

From the Lê–Nguyễn period onward, Vietnamese Buddhism lost its central position at court: dynasties prioritized Confucianism; many temples were damaged during wars; monks increasingly became village temple caretakers and ritual specialists. Buddhism nevertheless remained alive — through village temples, charitable societies, printed and orally transmitted scriptures — but it lacked the large scholarly centers of the Lý–Trần period. This was a transition "from a state religion to a religion of village society" — a shift that should be understood correctly so that one does not assume Vietnamese Buddhism simply "disappeared" during these centuries.

3.2. The early 20th-century Buddhist revival movement

In the early 20th century, alongside wider cultural revival movements, Vietnamese Buddhism entered a period of Buddhist revival:

  • Buddhist associations: the Lạc Không Buddhist Association (Huế, 1931), the Southern Vietnam Buddhist Studies Association (Saigon, 1932), and the Northern Vietnam Buddhist Studies Association (1934) translated scriptures, published books, opened schools, and issued journals such as Đuốc Tuệ.
  • Saṅgha administration reforms: local and regional monastic organizations were established — paving the way for later national organizations.
  • Charity organizations (Huế): Buddhist social groups operated clinics and relief work, showing an early Vietnamese form of engaged Buddhism.
  • Kinh Theravāda formed in the early 20th century around Bửu Quang Temple (Saigon) — Vietnamese practitioners studied and practiced according to the Pali scriptures of Theravāda; successor monasteries developed later (domestic historical sources should be consulted for more detailed research).

3.3. Buddhist organizations up to the present

Across periods of national division, war, and reunification, Vietnamese Buddhist organizations changed many times. Today, the Vietnam Buddhist Sangha (established in 1981) is a unified Buddhist organization bringing together a range of traditions (including Chan–Pure Land practice, Theravāda, Khmer Buddhism, and others). Other organizations and lineages also exist inside and outside the country. This is a historically and politically sensitive topic; this article keeps to a practical description: the app does not enter into disputes between organizations, and the following articles present traditions according to their teachings and forms of practice.

4. A map of Vietnamese Buddhism today

4.1. Traditions currently present

  • Northern tradition (Mahāyāna) — the dominant stream: most Vietnamese temples belong to this tradition, with forms such as Chan (seated meditation, kōan introspection), Pure Land (Buddha-recitation), and combined Chan–Pure Land practice (both kōan introspection and Buddha-recitation — the most common form).
  • Kinh Theravāda: Vietnamese monasteries using the Pali scriptures and following Theravāda observances — alms round, observance days (Pali: uposatha), and the rains retreat.
  • Khmer Theravāda: temples of the Khmer community in southwestern Vietnam (Sóc Trăng, Trà Vinh, etc.), closely connected with Khmer language and festivals (Chôl Chnăm Thmây, Ok Om Bok).
  • Revived Trúc Lâm tradition: centers reviving the Trúc Lâm Zen tradition — seated meditation and study of Trần Nhân Tông's thought — which expanded strongly from the late 20th century.
  • Plum Village tradition: a tradition of mindfulness and right speech founded by Zen master Thích Nhất Hạnh, present in centers inside and outside Vietnam.
  • Modern meditation centers: meditation and mindfulness groups and centers outside the traditional sectarian framework, often connected with modern methods.

4.2. Recognition tips when entering a Vietnamese temple

A few simple signs may help indicate which tradition a temple is associated with — with the understanding that different traditions are not ranked higher or lower; they simply follow different paths of practice:

  • Main Buddha image: a lone image of Śākyamuni Buddha is common in Theravāda temples; an image of Amitābha or the Western Pure Land triad suggests Pure Land practice; images of bodhisattvas (Kṣitigarbha, Avalokiteśvara, etc.) suggest Mahāyāna.
  • Ritual language: chanting in Pali (refuge formulas, offering verses) → Theravāda; chanting in Sino-Vietnamese (Lotus Sūtra, Amitābha texts, etc.) → Northern-tradition Mahāyāna.
  • Robe colors: bright yellow or saffron robes are characteristic of Theravāda; brown, dark yellow, gray, and other robe colors are common in Northern-tradition Mahāyāna; Khmer monks also often wear dark yellow robes, but the way they are wrapped differs.
  • Daily practice: Theravāda temples maintain alms rounds and clearly observed uposatha days; Northern-tradition Mahāyāna temples often have morning and evening chanting services; Trúc Lâm and Plum Village centers may hold longer periods of seated meditation.

These tips are only guides: in practice, many temples combine elements (for example, a Mahāyāna temple may offer meditation classes in a modern style). The important point is not to "classify" temples, but to see the diversity of Buddhist practice within one country — a richness that few countries share in quite the same way.

5. Summary and next steps

  • Buddhism reached Vietnam from the first centuries CE through two routes (overland and maritime); Luy Lâu was the earliest major center.
  • Under the Lý, Buddhism became a de facto state religion; under the Trần, the Trúc Lâm Zen tradition was founded by Trần Nhân Tông, expressing the ideal of "Cư trần lạc đạo".
  • Under the Lê–Nguyễn, Buddhism declined at court but remained strong in village society; in the early 20th century, a Buddhist revival movement promoted scripture translation, monastic reforms, and the formation of Kinh Theravāda.
  • Today: the Vietnam Buddhist Sangha brings together multiple traditions; alongside it are Khmer Theravāda, the revived Trúc Lâm tradition, Plum Village, and modern meditation centers.
  • This diversity is a cultural heritage — not a hierarchy, but a range of different paths of practice.

Glossary:

  • Jiaozhi: the ancient name for northern Vietnam in the period of Chinese rule and influence.
  • Luy Lâu: an early trading hub and Buddhist center in Vietnam, now in Bắc Ninh; Dâu Pagoda there is among the country's oldest surviving temples.
  • Mouzi / Moubo: an Indian monk traditionally associated with Luy Lâu around the 2nd century and with the work Lihuolun.
  • Lihuolun: an early Chinese-language Buddhist text associated with Vietnam, arguing in defense of Buddhism against Confucianism and Daoism.
  • Vinitaruci tradition: a meditation lineage begun in Vietnam by the Indian master Vinitaruci in the 6th century — the oldest meditation lineage recorded in Vietnam.
  • De facto state religion: a religion supported and favored by the ruling court without an official legal declaration making it the state religion.
  • Trúc Lâm: the Zen tradition founded by Trần Nhân Tông during the Trần dynasty; uniquely among major Vietnamese Zen lineages, it was founded within Vietnam by a Vietnamese ruler.
  • "Cư trần lạc đạo": a concise expression of the Trúc Lâm spirit — practicing in the midst of ordinary life rather than leaving the world.
  • Alms round (Pali: piṇḍapāta): the Theravāda monastic practice of receiving food from households; preserved by Kinh and Khmer Theravāda communities in Vietnam.
  • Observance day (Pali: uposatha): days for keeping precepts and listening to the Dhamma according to the lunar calendar — characteristic of Theravāda practice.
  • Chôl Chnăm Thmây, Ok Om Bok: two major Khmer festivals in southwestern Vietnam associated with Khmer Theravāda temples.
  • Combined Chan–Pure Land practice: practice combining kōan introspection and Buddha-recitation — common in Vietnamese Northern-tradition Mahāyāna temples.
  • Monastic robe (Pali: kāsāya): the robe worn by monastics; colors and ways of wearing it vary among traditions.
  • Kōan introspection: a Chan method using questions that cannot be resolved through ordinary reasoning (for example, "Who is reciting the Buddha's name?") to turn the mind inward.
  • Bửu Quang: a temple in Saigon associated with the formation of Kinh Theravāda in the early 20th century.
