G.D2. The Canon

After the overview in the previous article — G.D1 — this article turns to the canon: the Pali Canon, the body of scriptures preserved by Theravāda as a record of the Buddha's original teaching. Three questions will be answered: what is in the canon and how is it organized (the Tipiṭaka — three baskets: Vinaya, Sutta, and Abhidhamma); what does the third basket — Abhidhamma — discuss and why is it usually studied later; and where do the commentaries — books explaining the scriptures — fit into the picture, and how can we avoid confusing the different layers? After reading, you will see that the Pali Canon offers a different entry point for different kinds of readers — and know which doorway to begin with.

1. The Pali Canon — a map of the scriptures

1.1. The "three baskets" and the origin of the division

The complete Theravāda canon is called the Tipiṭaka (Pali: tipiṭaka, Sanskrit: tripiṭaka) — literally the "Three Baskets." The word piṭaka means "basket": three large baskets, each containing texts with a shared function — the Vinaya Piṭaka (Pali: Vinaya Piṭaka) preserves the rules governing monastic life; the Sutta Piṭaka (Pali: Sutta Piṭaka) preserves discourses; and Abhidhamma (Pali: abhidhamma, Sanskrit: abhidharma) contains a systematic analytical treatment of the teachings — the subject of section 2.

The three-basket division has its roots in the First Buddhist Council, as article G.B3 described: after the Buddha's passing, at Rājagaha, Elder Mahākassapa presided; the monk Upāli recited the Vinaya, and the monk Ānanda — who served the Buddha and heard many of his teachings — recited the Suttas. [Source: Vinaya Piṭaka, Cullavagga, chapter XI - Theravāda Buddhism]. From there, Vinaya and Sutta became two distinct baskets; the third basket took shape and was organized later. The Pali Canon was written down in the 1st century BCE in Sri Lanka (previous article — G.D1) and represents the most complete surviving form of these three baskets (article G.B4).

1.2. The Vinaya Piṭaka — the "life manual" of the monastic community

The Vinaya answers the question: how does the monastic community function? — from major matters (not killing, not stealing...) to small daily matters (how food is received, how people live together, how disagreements are settled). Its main section is the Suttavibhaṅga (Pali: Suttavibhaṅga) — an analysis of each rule: the circumstances in which the Buddha established it, its content, and cases of violation — including 227 rules for bhikkhus (Pali: bhikkhu) and 311 rules for bhikkhunīs (Pali: bhikkhunī). The Mahāvagga and Cullavagga record major rituals and events of the Saṅgha — including the story of the First Council just mentioned; the Parivāra is a summary section. Every two weeks, on the uposatha day (Pali: uposatha), monks and nuns recite the rules to renew their commitment — a practice that continues today (and returns in article G.D3).

In terms of difficulty, the Vinaya is not where beginners should start — it is dense and full of detailed regulations; for a newcomer, reading enough to understand monastic life is sufficient.

1.3. The Sutta Piṭaka — the five Nikāyas

The largest and most approachable basket is the Sutta Piṭaka: the suttas (Pali: sutta, Sanskrit: sūtra) — teachings delivered by the Buddha or major disciples, preserved together with their setting: where, to whom, and in response to what event. The discourses are grouped into five collections, collectively called the Nikāyas (Pali: Nikāya):

  • Dīgha Nikāya — the long discourses; 34 long suttas that provide broad pictures of Buddhism and contemporary thought, including the discourse describing the Buddha's final days (article G.B2).
  • Majjhima Nikāya — the middle-length discourses; 152 substantial discourses, each built around a complete subject; often the most approachable for first-time readers.
  • Saṃyutta Nikāya — the connected discourses; 56 thematic groupings covering dependent origination, the noble truths, the senses, and other subjects, with thousands of shorter discourses — like a topical reference collection.
  • Aṅguttara Nikāya — the numerical discourses; thousands of discourses organized by increasing numbers: teachings with one factor, two factors, and so on up to eleven; an easy structure to remember and search.
  • Khuddaka Nikāya — the collection of shorter texts; traditionally 15 books in the Sri Lankan recension (the Burmese listing may include several additional works); best known are the Dhammapada (423 verses), the Jātaka stories (the Buddha's previous lives — encountered in the previous article, G.D1), and the Milindapañha — a dialogue between the Greek king Menander (Milinda) and the monk Nāgasena.

1.4. Where should you start reading?

A familiar suggestion for beginners is the Majjhima Nikāya and Aṅguttara Nikāya — each discourse has a clear subject and relatively little specialized terminology. For an even lighter starting point, the Dhammapada in the Khuddaka Nikāya is a popular choice: short, memorable verses. The Dīgha Nikāya is useful when you want to read historical context in greater detail, especially the Buddha's final days; the Saṃyutta Nikāya is helpful once you are comfortable with the terminology and want to follow themes. A reference list of commonly cited discourses, together with Vietnamese translations, appears in article G.H1; the citation codes used in these articles (such as "MN 27" or "AN 3.61") are also explained there.

2. What is Abhidhamma?

2.1. The Suttas tell stories; Abhidhamma classifies

In the Suttas, the word "dhamma" often means the teaching; in Abhidhamma, dhamma (Pali: dhamma) has a broader meaning: every constituent of experience — a mental state, a feeling, a sense faculty, or a physical quality. The task of Abhidhamma is to list, classify, and define relationships among these phenomena, down to factors treated as irreducible in the system (Pali: paramattha).

An easy image to remember is this: if the Sutta Piṭaka is like a collection of stories — the Buddha teaches different people in different settings — Abhidhamma is like a scientific classification system: the same materials are arranged in tables, named, categorized, and linked according to rules. A verse such as "Mind precedes all phenomena, mind leads them, they are made by mind" [Source: Dhammapada, verse 1 - Theravāda Buddhism] — can be unpacked in Abhidhamma into its components: what is mind, what mental factors accompany it, what leads and what follows. The two presentations do not conflict — one teaches according to the listener and situation, the other analyzes for deeper study.

2.2. The seven books of the Abhidhamma

According to the Pali tradition, the Abhidhamma consists of seven treatises. Three are especially common in study: the Dhammasaṅgaṇī (Pali: Dhammasaṅgaṇī) — the opening work, which enumerates and classifies phenomena; the Kathāvatthu (Pali: Kathāvatthu) — a work on points of doctrinal controversy, traditionally associated with the Third Council (previous article — G.D1); and the large Paṭṭhāna — an analysis of the conditional relations among phenomena, regarded as the most extensive book of the entire Tipiṭaka. The seven works form a specialized system of analysis rather than a second set of narrative discourses (the early schools had their own analytical systems — article G.B4 — but the Pali system is the lineage that survives and continues today).

2.3. Why Abhidhamma is usually studied later

There are three reasons. First — it has its own technical vocabulary: reading Abhidhamma is like entering a new system of principles, so the terminology needs to be familiar first. Second — the classification is dense: one experience can be divided into many dhammas with detailed conditional relations — useful for advanced study, heavy for beginners. Third — the path already presented in the Suttas comes first: hearing the Dhamma, keeping the precepts, practicing meditation; fine-grained analysis supports this path but does not replace it. Traditional curricula therefore often place Abhidhamma after the Sutta Piṭaka, and many lifelong students work through the books gradually. This article therefore stays at the level of a map; the detailed "lists of phenomena" are advanced study, not required for the general learning path.

3. The commentarial tradition and the Visuddhimagga

3.1. The canon and the layers of interpretation

When reading Theravāda sources, the material often comes to you in several layers. The base layer is the canonical text — the Pali Canon, written down as article G.D1 described. Above it are the commentaries (Pali: aṭṭhakathā) — books that explain individual words, contexts, and difficult concepts. These commentaries were originally written in Sinhala and were systematized into a shared Pali form by the scholar Buddhaghosa in the 5th century. Above them again are sub-commentaries (Pali: ṭīkā) — explanations of the commentaries — followed by modern teaching literature.

Why are commentaries needed? The discourses were spoken more than two thousand years ago for audiences of their own time: words can change meaning, and contexts — titles, customs, place names — may need explanation. Commentaries function as "interpreters between eras." But one caution from article G.A2 remains important: not everything described as "what the suttas teach" is actually in the canonical text — many detailed interpretations belong to the commentarial layer; the two layers carry different levels of authority when comparing sources.

3.2. Visuddhimagga — a map of the path

Among the commentarial works, one is especially prominent and distinctive: the Visuddhimagga (Pali: Visuddhimagga) by Buddhaghosa at the Mahāvihāra. Comprising 23 chapters, it systematizes the entire path according to the framework of the seven purifications (Pali: satta visuddhiyo) — from ethical conduct through concentration to wisdom — a framework on which later Theravāda curricula have often drawn, corresponding to the three trainings of ethical conduct — concentration — wisdom presented in article G.C6. Detailed practice steps belong to article G.D3 and the Meditation section of the app.

3.3. Reading Theravāda sources today — ask "which layer?"

A practical reading habit: when a sentence is presented as "the suttas teach," ask which layer does it belong to — canonical text, commentary, or modern teaching literature? The three layers carry different kinds of authority: the canonical text is the layer preserved by the tradition as scripture; commentaries explain it and sometimes add interpretations shaped by the period in which they were written; modern books summarize — sometimes accurately, sometimes by simplifying. Good sources usually identify their textual layer clearly; readers who develop this habit become steadier when different sources speak differently about the same topic. At every layer, one principle remains: the scriptures are a map of the path, not the destination — the final test is practice.

4. Summary and next steps

  • Tipiṭaka — the Three Baskets: Vinaya Piṭaka (monastic life), Sutta Piṭaka (the five Nikāyas), and Abhidhamma (systematic analysis of phenomena).
  • The Sutta Piṭaka has five Nikāyas: Dīgha (34 long discourses), Majjhima (152 middle-length discourses), Saṃyutta (organized by theme), Aṅguttara (organized numerically), and Khuddaka (shorter texts — Dhammapada, Jātaka, Milindapañha).
  • Where to start reading: Majjhima and Aṅguttara; the Dhammapada is the lightest entry point; the commonly cited suttas and citation codes are listed in article G.H1.
  • Abhidhamma analyzes experience into dhammas through seven treatises; its technical vocabulary and dense classifications are why it is often studied after the Sutta Piṭaka.
  • Theravāda sources have several layers — canon, commentaries, sub-commentaries, and modern books; when reading, ask "which layer?" — and remember that the Visuddhimagga is the standard work that systematizes the path of ethical conduct — concentration — wisdom.

Glossary:

  • Tipiṭaka (Pali: tipiṭaka, Sanskrit: tripiṭaka): the complete canon — Vinaya Piṭaka, Sutta Piṭaka, and Abhidhamma; "piṭaka" means "basket."
  • Vinaya Piṭaka (Pali: Vinaya Piṭaka): the rules governing monastic life.
  • Sutta Piṭaka (Pali: Sutta Piṭaka): the discourses of the Buddha and major disciples.
  • Abhidhamma (Pali: abhidhamma, Sanskrit: abhidharma): the third basket — a systematic analysis of the teachings into dhammas; also called the Abhidhamma Piṭaka.
  • Sutta (Pali: sutta, Sanskrit: sūtra): a recorded teaching together with its context.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the main Sutta Piṭaka collections in the Theravāda canon.
  • Bhikkhu / bhikkhunī (Pali: bhikkhu / bhikkhunī): an ordained monk / nun.
  • Uposatha (Pali: uposatha): a special observance day, traditionally on the full and new moons; monastics recite the precepts every two weeks.
  • Suttavibhaṅga (Pali: Suttavibhaṅga): the main disciplinary analysis in the Vinaya — each rule and the circumstances in which it was established.
  • Mahāvagga / Cullavagga / Parivāra (Pali: Mahāvagga / Cullavagga / Parivāra): the remaining major divisions of the Vinaya — rituals, major Saṅgha events, and summary material.
  • Dīgha / Majjhima / Saṃyutta / Aṅguttara / Khuddaka Nikāya: the five collections of the Pali Sutta Piṭaka.
  • Dhammapada (Pali: Dhammapada): a collection of 423 verses expressing foundational teachings, part of the Khuddaka Nikāya.
  • Jātaka (Pali: Jātaka): stories of the Buddha's previous lives, part of the Khuddaka Nikāya.
  • Milindapañha (Pali: Milindapañha): a dialogue between King Menander (Milinda) and the monk Nāgasena, addressing practical questions of Buddhist teaching.
  • Dhamma (Pali: dhamma): in Abhidhamma, a constituent of experience; in other contexts, it commonly means the Dhamma or teaching.
  • Paramattha (Pali: paramattha): the "ultimate realities" used in the Abhidhamma analytical framework.
  • Dhammasaṅgaṇī (Pali: Dhammasaṅgaṇī): the opening Abhidhamma treatise, enumerating and classifying dhammas.
  • Kathāvatthu (Pali: Kathāvatthu): the treatise dealing with points of doctrinal controversy, traditionally associated with the Third Council.
  • Paṭṭhāna (Pali: Paṭṭhāna): the largest Abhidhamma work, analyzing conditional relations among dhammas.
  • Commentary (Pali: aṭṭhakathā): a textual layer explaining the canonical scriptures — words, contexts, and difficult concepts.
  • Sub-commentary (Pali: ṭīkā): a later layer explaining the commentaries.
  • Sinhala: the language of Sri Lanka in which many early commentaries were composed before being systematized into Pali.
  • Buddhaghosa: the 5th-century scholar associated with the Mahāvihāra who systematized the Pali commentarial tradition and composed the Visuddhimagga.
  • Visuddhimagga (Pali: Visuddhimagga): the 23-chapter work systematizing the path through the seven purifications — a standard map of the Theravāda path.
  • The seven purifications (Pali: satta visuddhiyo): seven levels of purification, from purification of conduct to purification by knowledge and vision — the organizing framework of the Visuddhimagga.
  • The three trainings (Pali: tisso sikkhā): ethical conduct — concentration — wisdom — the three-step training program of the Buddhist path (presented in article G.C6).
