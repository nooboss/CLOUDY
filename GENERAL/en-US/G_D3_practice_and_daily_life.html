G.D3. The Path of Practice and Daily Life

The canon examined in the previous article — G.D2 — is the map; this article turns to the journey: the Theravāda path of practice as a whole, meditation as a central method, and the lives of the two sides of the community — ordained monastics and lay Buddhists. 1. The path of practice in Theravāda

1.1. The gradual training — moving step by step

At the center of the Theravāda path is the concept introduced in the previous article — G.D1: the gradual training (Pali: anupubbasikkhā). The texts describe a natural sequence, like climbing a staircase:

  • Taking refuge (Pali: saraṇa) — taking refuge in the Three Jewels (Buddha — Dhamma — Saṅgha): laying the foundation, trusting the path, and beginning to learn.
  • Keeping the precepts (Pali: sīla) — living without causing harm: five precepts for lay practitioners (section 4), hundreds of rules for monastics (section 3).
  • Giving and making merit (Pali: puñña) — opening the heart, giving, and planting wholesome seeds.
  • Hearing the Dhamma — going to the temple, reading the scriptures, and asking teachers: loading the map before setting out.
  • Meditation — training the mind: tranquillity meditation and insight meditation (section 2).
  • Realization — the four stages of awakening, from stream-entry to arahantship (Pali: arahant, Sanskrit: arhat) (article G.C7).

The sequence appears repeatedly in the discourses, through a memorable comparison: like someone tracking an elephant by its footprints — following small tracks to larger ones until finally seeing the elephant itself; confidence in the teaching comes first, direct knowing through practice later. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 27 (Cūḷahatthipadopama Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism].

1.2. No skipping steps — and the role of the Saṅgha

The key point of the gradual path is that the stages are not skipped — like building a house: foundation first, walls next, roof last; meditation rests on ethical conduct, and wisdom rests on concentration. The three trainings of ethical conduct — concentration — wisdom (article G.C6) and the seven purifications of the Visuddhimagga (previous article — G.D2) are two ways of drawing this same path. The picture also always includes the role of the Saṅgha as custodian of the teaching: the transmission of instruction through generations of monastics — without people to preserve and teach it, the map would be lost. That is why this article looks at life from both sides: monastic life (section 3) and lay life (section 4).

2. Meditation in Theravāda — an introduction

2.1. Two complementary streams: tranquillity and insight

Theravāda meditation has two major streams. Tranquillity meditation (Pali: samatha) steadies and concentrates the mind — like muddy water settling clear in a jar. Insight meditation (Pali: vipassanā) observes body and mind as they occur in order to see their nature clearly: impermanence, suffering, and non-self (article G.C5). Many meditation teachers teach the two in sequence — tranquillity gives the mind enough stability, insight provides clear seeing; other lineages move directly into observation through the breath and sensations — each tradition has its own approach, all directed toward liberation. The three factors of right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration in the Noble Eightfold Path (article G.C2) are the mind-training part of this path. Detailed practice — posture, breathing, handling distraction — belongs to the Meditation section of the app, which has its own step-by-step path.

2.2. Common meditation subjects

Theravāda meditation classifies meditation subjects (Pali: kammaṭṭhāna) — what the mind takes as an object during training; the traditional system lists forty meditation subjects. Four especially familiar groups are:

  • Mindfulness of breathing (Pali: ānāpānasati) — the most widely known subject; the Buddha practiced with the breath in the years before his awakening (article G.B2), and one discourse is devoted specifically to this subject. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 118 (Ānāpānasati Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism].
  • The four divine abodes (Pali: brahmavihāra) — cultivating loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity into established states of mind (article G.C6); the discourse presents this teaching in response to the question of the path to the Brahmā world. [Source: Dīgha Nikāya, DN 13 (Tevijja Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism].
  • The four foundations of mindfulness (Pali: Satipaṭṭhāna) — observing four "grounds": body, feelings, mind, and phenomena; a foundational discourse for modern insight-meditation lineages. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 10 (Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism].
  • Kasiṇas (Pali: kasiṇa) — concentrating on earth, water, fire, air, and colors; this group also includes recollections such as recollection of the Buddha (Pali: buddhānussati) — all within the traditional system of forty subjects.

A necessary warning here: the four groups above are a map, not instructions for self-training. Do not simply choose a subject and go deeply into books on your own; the safest approach is to follow a guided path — the Meditation section of the app has separate material on preparation and meditation safety.

3. Theravāda monastic life

3.1. A monk's day

The Saṅgha (Pali: saṅgha) lives according to the rhythm established by the Vinaya (previous article — G.D2). A typical day for a bhikkhu (Pali: bhikkhu) may look like this: rising early for meditation; at daybreak going on alms-round (Pali: piṇḍapāta) — moving from house to house, stopping where people are waiting, receiving offerings without choosing or rejecting food; returning to the monastery for the main meal before noon; listening in the afternoon to instruction from an elder, studying and reciting scriptures; and spending the evening in meditation. This rhythm turns "keep the precepts — study the Dhamma — meditate" from three doctrinal terms into a concrete daily schedule.

3.2. Reciting the monastic code and the rains retreat

Every two weeks, as promised in the previous article — G.D2 — monks and nuns recite the Pātimokkha (Pali: pātimokkha) on the uposatha day (Pali: uposatha): the whole community gathers, an elder recites the rules one by one, and anyone who has committed an offense acknowledges it — a mechanism of communal purification. [Source: Vinaya Piṭaka, Mahāvagga - Theravāda Buddhism].

During the three-month rainy season, monastics remain in one place for intensive practice — the rains retreat (Pali: vassa): avoiding long alms journeys during the storm and flood season and creating more time for study and intensive meditation. Lay communities often treat the rains retreat as a major period of giving; when it ends, people offer kaṭhina robes (Pali: kaṭhina) — one of the year's major merit-making occasions (article G.D5). [Source: Vinaya rules on the rains retreat and kaṭhina offering, Mahāvagga - Theravāda Buddhism].

3.3. Village monasteries, forest monasteries, and teacher-student relationships

Theravāda has several different "living lineages." Village monasteries are embedded in local communities: the temple is a center for ritual life, once also a place where children learned to read and write, and the abbot serves as a respected elder. Forest monasteries (forest tradition) choose quiet places — forests and mountains — and emphasize meditation and stricter discipline; the Thai forest tradition through meditation masters Ajahn Mun and Ajahn Chah has had global influence (article G.D4). The two are not opposites — monastics often train at multiple monasteries over the course of their lives.

The teacher-student relationship is the backbone of transmission: a newly ordained monastic depends on a teacher to learn the precepts, discipline, and way of life — a relationship called nissaya (Pali: nissaya) — for at least the first five years of monastic life. The texts describe this as a two-way responsibility: the teacher teaches fully, and the student follows fully. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 2.31 (Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 2.31) - Theravāda Buddhism].

4. Theravāda lay life

4.1. The foundation: refuge, the five precepts, and giving

The foundation of lay life has three parts. Taking refuge — the ceremony of taking refuge in the Three Jewels, the common doorway into all traditions (article G.A3). The five precepts (Pali: pañcasīla, Sanskrit: pañcaśīla): not killing, not stealing, avoiding sexual misconduct, not lying, and avoiding intoxicants — five trainings for lay practitioners, not merely five prohibitions (article G.A3 has a dedicated section). Giving (Pali: dāna) — supporting the Saṅgha and helping people in need: the first major merit-making practice of lay life; the Saṅgha is regarded as a field of merit (Pali: puññakkhetta) — a field in which wholesome actions are planted (article G.C4). [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 8.31 (Dakkhiṇāvibhaṅga Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism].

4.2. Observance days, hearing the Dhamma, and sharing merit

Beyond that foundation are monthly practices. On an uposatha day (new moon and full moon), many people go to the temple to observe the eight precepts (Pali: aṭṭhasīla): living for one day more like a monastic — observing additional precepts and living more simply: no food after noon, no entertainment or adornment, and no high or luxurious beds — a kind of "experience day" for the monastic path. The day is also used for hearing the Dhamma and meditating at the temple — the main monthly gathering for lay practitioners. Giving is often connected with sharing merit (Pali: pattidāna): after making merit, practitioners dedicate or share its benefit with deceased relatives — a practice supported in scriptural traditions and found across all three major traditions (article G.C4).

4.3. Regional customs

The framework above is shared; each country adds its own color. In Thailand, the morning alms-round is a familiar village scene, and the beginning and end of the rains retreat are major community merit-making periods. In Myanmar, observing the eight precepts is very common — many people keep regular uposatha observances throughout their lives. In Laos, merit-making festivals called Boun (Lao - merit-making festival) mark the rhythm of the year. In Cambodia, lay life revolves around village temples and festivals tied to the agricultural seasons. These customs are detailed in article G.D4; festivals are covered in article G.D5.

5. Summary and next steps

  • The gradual training: taking refuge → keeping the precepts → giving and making merit → hearing the Dhamma → meditation → realization; the stages are not skipped — meditation rests on ethical conduct, wisdom on concentration.
  • Meditation has two complementary streams — tranquillity and insight; familiar subjects include mindfulness of breathing, the four divine abodes, and the four foundations of mindfulness; practice should follow a guided path rather than be improvised from books.
  • The Saṅgha lives according to the Vinaya: alms-round — Dhamma study — meditation; the Pātimokkha is recited every two weeks; the rains retreat ends with the kaṭhina robe offering; village and forest traditions coexist; the teacher-student relationship (nissaya) is the backbone of transmission.
  • Lay practitioners have their own foundation: refuge, the five precepts, and giving; deeper practice includes observing the eight precepts on uposatha days, hearing the Dhamma, and sharing merit with relatives — with regional customs adding different forms.

Glossary:

  • Gradual training (Pali: anupubbasikkhā): the path arranged step by step — refuge, precepts, giving, hearing the Dhamma, meditation, realization.
  • Taking refuge (Pali: saraṇa): taking refuge in the Three Jewels — Buddha, Dhamma, Saṅgha.
  • Ethical conduct (Pali: sīla): training in non-harmful living; a foundation for concentration and wisdom.
  • Merit (Pali: puñña): a wholesome action understood to bear beneficial results — like a seed that has been planted.
  • Arahant (Pali: arahant, Sanskrit: arhat): one who has completely ended the mind's defilements — the culmination of the four stages of awakening.
  • Tranquillity meditation (Pali: samatha): a practice for calming, stabilizing, and concentrating the mind.
  • Insight meditation (Pali: vipassanā): observing body and mind to see impermanence, suffering, and non-self clearly.
  • Meditation subject (Pali: kammaṭṭhāna): "a work of the mind" — what the mind takes as an object during meditation; the traditional system lists 40 subjects.
  • Mindfulness of breathing (Pali: ānāpānasati): mindfulness of the breath — one of the most widely known Theravāda meditation subjects.
  • The four divine abodes (Pali: brahmavihāra): loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity.
  • The four foundations of mindfulness (Pali: Satipaṭṭhāna): body, feelings, mind, and phenomena.
  • Kasiṇa (Pali: kasiṇa): a group of meditation subjects involving earth, water, fire, air, and colors.
  • Recollection of the Buddha (Pali: buddhānussati): one of the recollections in the traditional system of forty subjects — directing the mind toward the qualities of the awakened one.
  • Saṅgha (Pali: saṅgha): the community of Buddhist practitioners — a central institution in Theravāda life.
  • Bhikkhu (Pali: bhikkhu): an ordained monk; the ordained female form is bhikkhunī (Pali: bhikkhunī).
  • Alms-round (Pali: piṇḍapāta): receiving food in a bowl each morning without choosing among offerings — traditionally the main meal of the day for monastics.
  • Instruction: teaching and guidance given by an elder to monastics within the monastery.
  • Uposatha (Pali: uposatha): a special observance day each month — the new moon and full moon; monastics recite the rules, and lay practitioners may observe the eight precepts.
  • Pātimokkha (Pali: pātimokkha): the collection of monastic rules, recited every two weeks on an uposatha day.
  • Rains retreat (Pali: vassa): the three-month rainy-season period when monastics remain in one place for intensive practice.
  • Kaṭhina robe offering (Pali: kaṭhina): the robe-offering ceremony after the rains retreat — one of the year's major merit-making occasions.
  • Forest tradition: monasteries in quiet settings that emphasize meditation and strict discipline, in contrast with village monasteries.
  • Nissaya (Pali: nissaya): the dependence of a student on a teacher for training in precepts, discipline, and way of life — traditionally for at least the first five years.
  • The five precepts (Pali: pañcasīla, Sanskrit: pañcaśīla): five trainings for lay practitioners — refraining from killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, false speech, and intoxicants.
  • Giving (Pali: dāna): giving to others, including supporting the Saṅgha and helping people in need — a foundational merit-making practice.
  • Field of merit (Pali: puññakkhetta): a "field" in which wholesome actions are planted and understood to bear merit; the Saṅgha is traditionally regarded as a primary field of merit.
  • The eight precepts (Pali: aṭṭhasīla): eight training rules observed for one day — living temporarily in a more monastic way, usually on an uposatha day.
  • Sharing merit (Pali: pattidāna): dedicating or sharing the merit of one's wholesome actions with others, including the deceased.
  • Boun (Lao): a general name for merit-making festivals in Laos.
