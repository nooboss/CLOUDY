G.E5. East Asian Buddhism — by Country

East Asian Buddhism developed through translation, travel, local institutions, and cultural adaptation. China, Japan, Korea, and Vietnam share major scriptures and schools, yet each formed distinctive lineages and everyday practices.

  • 2. Buddhism in Japan

  • 4. Summary and next steps

1. Buddhism in China

Buddhism reached China through Central Asian and maritime routes, then took deep root through translation and interpretation. Chinese translators such as Kumārajīva and Xuanzang made Indian texts available in a literary language that shaped East Asian Buddhism for centuries. Tiantai, Huayan, Chan, Pure Land, Faxiang, Sanlun, and Chinese Esoteric forms emerged or flourished there. Chinese Buddhism also learned to express Buddhist teaching in conversation with Confucian and Daoist cultures.

2. Buddhism in Japan

Buddhism reached Japan chiefly through Korea and China and became intertwined with court, monastic, and popular life. Major traditions include Tendai, Shingon, Zen, Pure Land, Nichiren, and later lay movements. Japanese Buddhist history is marked by periods of state support, reform, persecution, and modern change; it should not be reduced to Zen alone.

3. Buddhism in Korea

Korean Buddhism received Chinese scriptures and schools, developing traditions such as Seon and Hwaeom. It interacted closely with state institutions and Confucian culture, and has endured periods of suppression and renewal. Korean Seon is a major living meditation tradition, while Korean Buddhism also maintains broad ritual, scholastic, and devotional life.

4. Summary and next steps

  • China was the main translation and school-forming centre of East Asian Mahāyāna.
  • Japan and Korea received shared Buddhist resources but created distinctive institutions and lineages.
  • Vietnam is treated in G.B7; its common Mahāyāna form combines Chan and Pure Land practice.

Glossary:

  • Seon: the Korean form of Chan / Zen.
  • Tendai: the Japanese form of Tiantai.
  • Hwaeom: the Korean form of Huayan.
  • Nichiren: a Japanese tradition centred on the Lotus Sutra.

Source notes

  • National Buddhist histories contain multiple lineages and changing relations with political and social institutions.
  • Country-level summaries are orientation maps; they do not replace the histories of individual schools or communities.
