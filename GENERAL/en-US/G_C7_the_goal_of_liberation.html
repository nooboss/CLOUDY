G.C7. The Goal of Liberation

The final article in the shared foundational teachings group answers the question toward which all five previous articles have been pointing: what exactly is the destination of the path? This article explains what Nibbāna is (and what it is not), the process of reaching it through the four stages of awakening, and the picture of Buddhas — past, present, and future — a belief shared across all three traditions.

1. What is Nibbāna?

Article G.C1 introduced Nibbāna (Pali: nibbāna, Sanskrit: nirvāṇa) through the metaphor of the truth of cessation: a fire goes out because its fuel is exhausted. Now we need a more precise definition. The texts define it in terms of the three fires burning in the mind: Nibbāna is the extinguishing of the fires of greed, aversion, and delusion — the three roots of defilement that continuously produce suffering. [Source: Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 38.1 (Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 38.1) - Theravāda Buddhism]. The Pali word nibbāna carries precisely this sense: the fire has gone out, the burning has ended — and when the fire of suffering has gone out, suffering has no fuel left to burn.

The tradition analyzes this in terms of two occasions:

  • Nibbāna with residue remaining (Pali: sa-upādisesa): an awakened person has completely extinguished the three fires of greed — aversion — delusion, but the body is still alive — the fire is out, but the embers remain warm; the person continues to live, teach, and act — without producing further suffering. The Buddha after his awakening is the classic example of "Nibbāna with residue remaining": he lived and worked for decades, yet his mind was completely free. The texts precisely describe this as Nibbāna realized in this very life — the extinguishing of greed — aversion — delusion visible here and now. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 44 (Cūḷavedalla Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism].
  • Nibbāna without residue remaining (Pali: anupādisesa): when that body comes to an end — there are no longer any "warm embers" to sustain the process; the stream of existence is no longer being fueled again. Traditions differ in how they explain this stage in detail; those different approaches are summarized in the comparative article (article G.G1). Here we record only what is shared: Nibbāna is not "somewhere" but the complete ending of suffering.

One important detail of the definition is that Nibbāna belongs to the category of the unconditioned (Pali: asaṅkhata) — the one thing not produced by conditions, and therefore not subject to change or ending. Thus Nibbāna is not "the best of all states" — it is the ending of every process that produces suffering.

2. What Nibbāna is not

Because Nibbāna lies outside ordinary experience, misunderstandings easily arise — they are worth stating clearly:

  • "Nibbāna = destruction, disappearing." No. What goes out is the fire of suffering, not a person: as article G.C5 showed, a "person" is a stream of processes rather than a permanent core — what ends are the fires of greed — aversion — delusion, and with them the whole mechanism that produces suffering. What could be "lost" when there is no permanent self to lose?
  • "Nibbāna = a heavenly realm." No. The heavenly realms are still forms of existence within saṃsāra — still conditioned and still changing (article G.C4); Nibbāna is the ending of saṃsāra, not its highest level.
  • "Nibbāna = an ecstatic, floating feeling." No. The pleasant experiences of meditative absorption (article G.C2) are still conditioned — they arise and pass away; Nibbāna is unconditioned, not a feeling that can be grasped.
  • "What is Nibbāna after death?" The texts answer: the question does not apply. A text tells of the Buddha asking where a fire that has just gone out has gone: the question is meaningless, because a fire that has gone out has not "gone" anywhere — its fuel is exhausted and its conditions have ceased. The same applies to the liberated person: the categories "exists — does not exist — both — neither" do not capture what has happened. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 72 (Aggivacchagotta Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]. Another famous image describes Nibbāna as "water in a snail's claw" — not something nonexistent, but not ordinary water that can be collected and measured. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 7.48 - Theravāda Buddhism].

Why do the texts not describe Nibbāna directly? Because language works through concepts — and concepts are constructed from experience of the conditioned world; Nibbāna is the ending of conditioning — so conceptual descriptions can only point the way, not constitute the destination. The texts describe it from one direction only: a reality that is "visible here and now" (Pali: sandiṭṭhika), "inviting inspection" (ehipassika), and "to be experienced individually by the wise". [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 12 (Mahāsīhanāda Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]. In other words: Nibbāna is not something to believe in — it is something known through the path; each step in ending defilement is a direct taste of that "water."

3. The four stages of awakening — the process of liberation

"Going to Nibbāna" is not an all-or-nothing leap — the Pāli Canon describes a step-by-step process through four stages of awakening, according to the number of fetters (Pali: saṃyojana) that have been abandoned:

  • Stream-enterer (Pali: sotāpanna, Sanskrit: srotāpanna): abandons the first three fetters — identity view (the belief in a self within the five aggregates — article G.C5), doubt (about ethics and the path), and attachment to rites and rituals (clinging to rituals and customs as though they could purify one by themselves). Such a person is no longer destined for the suffering realms and has at most seven more lives.
  • Once-returner (Pali: sakadāgāmi): greed — aversion — delusion have become markedly weaker; the person returns to the human realm only once more.
  • Non-returner (Pali: anāgāmi): additionally abandons sensual desire and ill will — no longer reborn in the sensual realm.
  • Arahant (Pali: arahant, Sanskrit: arhat): completely abandons the ten fetters — including the five higher fetters: desire for form, desire for the formless (attachment to refined meditative realms), conceit (the subtle trace of "I am something"), restlessness (subtle unease), and ignorance. The mind is completely free; the task is finished — "what had to be done has been done." [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 70 (Kīṭāgiri Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism].

These four stages reveal the shape of the process: liberation is cutting the fetters one by one — insight first, stability growing afterward; it is not something that depends on a miracle, nor a privilege reserved for any particular group. The texts record awakened people among both men and women, monastics and lay practitioners.

4. Two maps of the path in two traditions

The four stages of awakening are presented most explicitly in the Pāli Canon — and Mahāyāna traditions continue to recognize them as a description of one stream of practice. But Mahāyāna also has its own map of the bodhisattva path: the ten bodhisattva grounds (Sanskrit: daśabhūmi) — from the "ground of joy" through ten levels to the "Dharma Cloud ground" — ending in buddhahood. [Source: Daśabhūmika Sūtra - Mahāyāna Buddhism].

The two maps do not conflict in their function: both map a gradual process of increasing purification; they differ in ideal — the four stages of the arahant path (liberation for oneself, revered across traditions), and the ten grounds of the bodhisattva path (a longer journey together with all beings — article G.E2 presents the bodhisattva ideal). Theravāda also has its own bodhisattva path — the path followed by Buddhas — so both maps are present within the larger Buddhist landscape. How the traditions understand one another's ideals will be brought together in the comparative article (article G.G1).

5. Buddhas — past, present, and future

First, one foundational distinction: "Buddha" is a title (Pali: buddha) — not a person's proper name. The person we usually call "the Buddha" was named Siddhattha, of the Śākya clan — hence the full title "Śākyamuni," meaning "sage of the Śākyas." Whenever a person directly realizes the Four Noble Truths completely and teaches them to the world, that person is also a "Buddha."

The texts tell of past Buddhas — awakened beings who appeared before Śākyamuni: the best known is Dīpaṅkara (Pali: Dīpaṅkara) — the Buddha who, according to tradition, gave a prophecy of future buddhahood (Pali: vivaraṇa, Sanskrit: vyākaraṇa) to the bodhisattva who would later become Śākyamuni. Lists of past Buddhas appear in the Dīgha Nikāya. [Source: Dīgha Nikāya, DN 14 (Mahāpadāna Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism].

And there is the future Buddha: Metteyya (Pali: Metteyya, Sanskrit: Maitreya) — the next Buddha who will appear when the teachings of the Śākyamuni era have faded. Faith in Metteyya is shared across all three traditions: Theravāda venerates Metteyya as the future Buddha in temples; Mahāyāna develops the tradition extensively — Maitreya is a bodhisattva residing in Tuṣita who will descend to teach major treatises; Vajrayāna incorporates Maitreya into visualization systems. [Source: Dīgha Nikāya, DN 26 (Cakkavatti Sīhanāda Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism].

The significance of this picture for beginners is that Buddhism is not "the religion of one unique person" — "Buddha" is the function of an awakened teacher who rediscovers and teaches the path, appearing again across ages; and that path remains before us in our own time — there is no need to wait for a savior. In Mahāyāna thought, as traditions explore more deeply what "Buddha" means, the picture expands into the three bodies of a Buddha — a subject to be presented in article G.E2.

6. Summary and next steps

  • Nibbāna = the complete extinguishing of greed — aversion — delusion; two occasions: with residue remaining (the body is still alive — Nibbāna realized in this life) and without residue remaining (the body comes to an end); it is unconditioned — not produced by conditions and not subject to change.
  • Nibbāna is not annihilation, not heaven, and not an ecstatic feeling; the question "what happens after death?" does not apply (MN 72); it cannot be captured by concepts and is known through the path — "visible here and now, inviting inspection."
  • The four stages of awakening: stream-enterer → once-returner → non-returner → arahant — the ten fetters are abandoned step by step; liberation is a process, not a miracle.
  • Two maps of the process: the four stages (Pāli Canon) and the ten bodhisattva grounds (Mahāyāna) — not conflicting in function; different in ideal.
  • "Buddha" is a title — an awakened one; past Buddha (Dīpaṅkara), present Buddha (Śākyamuni), future Buddha (Metteyya) — a belief shared across the three traditions; opening into the three bodies of a Buddha.

Glossary:

  • Nibbāna (Pali: nibbāna, Sanskrit: nirvāṇa): the extinguishing of greed — aversion — delusion; the complete ending of suffering; the destination of the Buddhist path.
  • Greed — aversion — delusion (Pali: lobha, dosa, moha): the three root fires of defilement; the main causes that continually produce suffering.
  • Nibbāna with / without residue (Pali: sa-upādisesa / anupādisesa): the two occasions of Nibbāna — while the body remains alive and when the body comes to an end.
  • The unconditioned (Pali: asaṅkhata): that which is not produced by conditions and is not subject to arising and passing away; Nibbāna belongs to this category.
  • Fetters (Pali: saṃyojana): the ten links binding beings to saṃsāra, abandoned progressively by the stages of awakening.
  • Identity view (Pali: sakkāyadiṭṭhi): the belief in a permanent self within the five aggregates — the first fetter to be abandoned.
  • Attachment to rites and rituals (Pali: sīlabbataparāmāsa): clinging to rituals and customs as though they could purify one by themselves — the third fetter.
  • Sensual desire and ill will (Pali: kāmarāga, byāpāda): attachment to pleasures of the sensual realm and hostility over matters of the sensual realm — two of the lower fetters.
  • Conceit and restlessness (Pali: māna, uddhacca): the subtle trace of "I am something" and subtle unease — two of the five higher fetters.
  • Stream-enterer (Pali: sotāpanna, Sanskrit: srotāpanna): the first stage of awakening — abandoning three fetters and "entering the stream" of the Noble Eightfold Path.
  • Once-returner (Pali: sakadāgāmi): the second stage of awakening — greed, aversion, and delusion have weakened; one more rebirth in the human realm.
  • Non-returner (Pali: anāgāmi): the third stage of awakening — abandoning sensual desire and ill will; no rebirth in the sensual realm.
  • Arahant (Pali: arahant, Sanskrit: arhat): the fourth stage of awakening — completely abandoning the ten fetters; fully liberated.
  • The ten bodhisattva grounds (Sanskrit: daśabhūmi): the ten levels of the bodhisattva path in Mahāyāna, from the "ground of joy" to the "Dharma Cloud ground."
  • Prophecy of future buddhahood (Pali: vivaraṇa, Sanskrit: vyākaraṇa): a Buddha's declaration that someone will become a Buddha in the future.
  • Dīpaṅkara: the famous past Buddha who gave the prophecy of buddhahood to the bodhisattva who would become Śākyamuni.
  • Metteyya (Pali: Metteyya, Sanskrit: Maitreya): the next Buddha of the future — a belief shared across all three traditions.
  • Tuṣita (Sanskrit): the realm where Maitreya is described in Mahāyāna sources as residing as a bodhisattva.
  • Buddha (Pali: buddha): the title of one who awakens fully by oneself and teaches the path to others — not a proper name.
  • Undeclared questions (Pali: avyākata): questions that the texts decline to answer because their conceptual categories do not apply — the four categories "exists — does not exist..." are examples.
