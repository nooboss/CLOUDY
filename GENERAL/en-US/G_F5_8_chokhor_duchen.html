G.F5.8. (Phugpa) Chökhor Düchen — Festival of Turning the Wheel of Dharma
Fourth day of the sixth Tibetan month

Chökhor Düchen (Tibetan: Chökhor Düchen — "chökhor" is the wheel of Dharma, "düchen" is a great festival occasion — the Festival of Turning the Wheel of Dharma) commemorates the Buddha's first sermon after his enlightenment — the First Turning of the Wheel of Dharma (Sanskrit: dharmacakrapravartana) — on the fourth day of the sixth Tibetan month. It is one of the four Düchen days (article G.F5.1, item 3).

1. The event commemorated

After his enlightenment at Bodh Gaya, the Buddha went to the Deer Park at Sarnath (Pali: Isipatana, Sanskrit: Ṛṣipattana) near Varanasi and gave his first sermon to his five former ascetic companions — the sermon on the Four Noble Truths (Pali: cattāri ariya-saccāni). The detailed story is presented in article G.B2 (after the enlightenment) and article G.C1 (the content of the Four Noble Truths); the equivalent observances are: G.D5.4 (Theravāda) and G.E7.6 (Vietnam — Mahāyāna), G.E7.11 (China — Asalha). In the Tibetan tradition, this event is especially important because the entire Tibetan canon is seen as successive "wheels of Dharma" turned through different teaching periods (the Tibetan tradition's division of the three turnings of the wheel — presented in the article group G.F2 and G.F3).

2. How it is observed

  • At monasteries: sutra chanting — especially chanting the Sutra of the Four Noble Truths or the foundational sutras of the tradition; Dharma teachings for devotees; many monasteries hold public Dharma talks on this occasion.
  • Devotees: offerings, sutra chanting, mantra recitation (Sanskrit: mantra); turning the prayer wheel (Tibetan: mani khorlo) — turning the prayer wheel is explained as "chanting sutras by hand" (article G.F2 on the prayer wheel); doing good deeds because merit is multiplied many times on this day.
  • Community: this occasion falls at the start of the high-plateau summer; many people perform the pilgrimage circuit (kora) (Tibetan, from the Sanskrit "cakra" — circle — pilgrimage circuit — as explained in article G.F5.5) around the local monastery.

3. Three layers for reading Chökhor Düchen

  • Foundational teaching layer: commemorating the first teaching of the Dharma — the core in articles G.B2, G.C1, G.D5.
  • Shared practice layer: sutra chanting, Dharma teaching, offerings, turning the prayer wheel.
  • Custom layer: kora around monasteries; summer festivals; customs tied to the prayer wheel in everyday life.

4. Summary and next steps

  • Chökhor Düchen = the fourth day of the sixth Tibetan month; it commemorates the First Turning of the Wheel of Dharma — one of the four Düchens.
  • The same event as G.D5.4 (Theravāda) and G.E7.6 / G.E7.11 (Mahāyāna) — read those articles for the full story.

Glossary:

  • Chökhor Düchen (Tibetan, "chökhor" is the wheel of Dharma, "düchen" is a great festival occasion — the Festival of Turning the Wheel of Dharma): the Tibetan name of the great festival of turning the wheel of Dharma.
  • First Turning of the Wheel of Dharma (Sanskrit: dharmacakrapravartana): the Sanskrit name of the event of the Buddha's first sermon.
  • Deer Park at Sarnath (Pali: Isipatana, Sanskrit: Ṛṣipattana): where the Buddha gave his first sermon, near Varanasi.
  • Four Noble Truths (Pali: cattāri ariya-saccāni): the four fundamental truths taught in the first sermon — see article G.C1.
  • prayer wheel (Tibetan: mani khorlo): a rotating cylinder inscribed with or containing mantras — turning it is explained as chanting sutras by hand (article G.F2).
  • three turnings of the wheel (Tibetan — according to the Tibetan tradition): the division of the Buddha's teaching phases according to the Tibetan tradition — presented in groups G.F2 — G.F3.

Source notes

  • The core event is presented in full in article G.D5.4 (Theravāda) and article G.B2 (the life of the Buddha); this article keeps the part specific to the Tibetan tradition.
  • The concept of the "three turnings of the wheel" is the Tibetan-tradition division — Vajrayāna Buddhism.
  • The festival date of the fourth day of the sixth Tibetan month follows the standard festival calendar of this subsection (the Phugpa system); it is not converted into a fixed Gregorian date for every year.
