G.E4. Mahāyāna Schools

Mahāyāna is a family of traditions, not a single school. The schools below arose chiefly in China and travelled, in different forms, to Korea, Japan, and Vietnam. Their classifications overlap: one temple or practitioner may draw on more than one tradition.

1. A map of Mahāyāna schools

Schools can be grouped by their principal practice, scripture, or philosophical system. Chan and Pure Land are major living practice traditions. Tiantai and Huayan organise the scriptures into comprehensive visions. Faxiang and Sanlun develop Yogācāra and Madhyamaka thought. Shingon preserves East Asian esoteric practices. This map is a guide, not a hierarchy.

2. Chan / Zen

Chan, called Seon in Korea and Zen in Japan, centres meditation and direct insight into mind and reality. Its classical rhetoric emphasises direct pointing rather than dependence on words alone, yet Chan communities also study sutras, maintain precepts, and conduct liturgy. Chan is not simply “sitting quietly”; it is a disciplined lineage practice.

3. Pure Land

Pure Land practice centres Amitābha Buddha, aspiration for rebirth in Sukhāvatī, and Buddha-recitation. It relies on faith, vows, and practice, not on passive hope. In Vietnam, combined Chan–Pure Land practice is widespread: meditation, recitation, ethics, and devotion support one another.

4. Tiantai

Tiantai, associated with Zhiyi, takes the Lotus Sutra as its central text. It is known for its systematic classification of teachings and for zhǐguān, “calming and insight,” which unites meditation with doctrinal understanding. Its Japanese form is Tendai.

5. Huayan

Huayan, based on the Flower Garland Sutra, presents the mutual interpenetration of all phenomena. Its image of Indra's net conveys that every thing reflects every other without erasing particularity. Its Korean form is Hwaeom and its Japanese form Kegon.

6. Faxiang (Yogācāra)

Faxiang is the East Asian Yogācāra school, shaped especially by Xuanzang and Kuiji. It analyses consciousness, karmic seeds, and the transformation of cognition. Its purpose is practical liberation from distorted experience, not speculative idealism.

7. Sanlun

Sanlun, the School of Three Treatises, is East Asia's Madhyamaka tradition. It draws on Nāgārjuna and related masters to show that fixed views of existence and non-existence alike are grasping. Its “middle” is not a compromise position but freedom from reified extremes.

8. Shingon and East Asian Esoteric Buddhism

East Asian Esoteric Buddhism uses mantra, mudrā, mandala, ritual, and initiation. Shingon is its major Japanese form, associated with Kūkai. These practices require appropriate transmission and guidance; they should not be reduced to decorative symbols or generic techniques.

9. Summary and next steps

  • Mahāyāna schools overlap and often coexist in one community.
  • Chan, Pure Land, Tiantai, Huayan, Faxiang, Sanlun, and Shingon each foreground a distinctive practice, text, or analysis.
  • Vietnam commonly combines Chan and Pure Land practice.

Glossary:

  • Chan / Zen: East Asian meditation traditions.
  • Pure Land: practice centred on Amitābha and rebirth in Sukhāvatī.
  • Tiantai / Tendai: Lotus-Sutra-centred Chinese and Japanese traditions.
  • Huayan / Hwaeom / Kegon: Flower-Garland traditions.
  • Faxiang: East Asian Yogācāra.
  • Sanlun: East Asian Madhyamaka.
  • Shingon: Japanese East Asian Esoteric Buddhism.

Source notes

  • School labels represent historical lineages and teaching systems; actual temple practice often combines them.
  • Mantric and initiatory practices require qualified instruction within their traditions.
