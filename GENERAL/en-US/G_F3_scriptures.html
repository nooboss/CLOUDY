G.F3. Scriptures

The previous article — G.F2 — introduced the foundational concepts of Vajrayāna Buddhism — from the four schools to the tulku system. This article introduces the textual source of all that: the Tibetan-language scriptural system, comprising the Kangyur (the collection of the Buddha's words — around 100 volumes) and the Tengyur (the collection of commentaries — over 200 volumes), the place of tantra within the canon, the historical woodblock editions, the Nyingma school's separate tantra collection, and reading the Tibetan canon today — including the "84000" English translation project. This is a map-level (L3) article: enough to understand what this system is and to set it beside the other two canons, without going into volume-by-volume detail.

1. The Kangyur — the collection of the Buddha's words

Kangyur (Tibetan: bka' 'gyur, "Translation of the Word") is the body of texts regarded in Tibetan Buddhism as the words of the Buddha, translated from Sanskrit (with a few from other languages) into Tibetan — around 1,100 texts in around 100 volumes (figures vary by edition). Its main divisions are:

  • Vinaya (Sanskrit: Vinaya): monastic discipline — the longest section in the Kangyur, translated from Indian Vinaya lineages.
  • Perfection of Wisdom (Sanskrit: Prajñāpāramitā): versions ranging from a hundred thousand verses down to eight thousand verses — the same textual family as the Chinese translations in G.E3.
  • Flower Garland Sutra (Sanskrit: Avataṃsaka Sūtra): the Tibetan translation of this sutra.
  • Heap of Jewels (Sanskrit: Ratnakūṭa): a collection of independent sutras.
  • Other sutras — including sutras shared across all traditions (such as the discourse on Turning the Wheel of Dharma — G.B2).
  • Tantra — the esoteric scriptures, detailed in section 3.

One point to note: unlike the Pali Canon (G.D2) — fixed once and preserved as a single standard textual system — the Kangyur and Tengyur are collections: different canonical editions (Narthang, Derge...) share most of the same texts but differ slightly in arrangement and selection; there is no single "official" edition. And unlike the Chinese canon (G.E3) — formed through translation waves spread over many centuries in China — the Tibetan system was translated within a concentrated period (from the 8th century, then a revision phase in the 11th–13th centuries — G.F1), with standardised terminology: the Tibetan court issued translation rules and a standard lexicon, the Mahāvyutpatti (Sanskrit: Mahāvyutpatti, "Great Lexicon", early 9th century) — so that one Sanskrit term was rendered by the same Tibetan word throughout the canon — something the other canonical systems do not have.

2. The Tengyur — the collection of commentaries

Tengyur (Tibetan: bstan 'gyur, "Translation of the Treatises") gathers texts composed by Indian scholars — sutra commentaries, treatises, and ancillary disciplines (philosophy and logic, grammar, medicine, poetics) — around 4,000 texts in over 200 volumes. This is the commentarial channel through which Indian thought has been preserved nearly intact: the treatises of Nāgārjuna (Madhyamaka — G.E2), Asaṅga – Vasubandhu (Yogācāra), Candrakīrti (7th-century Madhyamaka commentator), and commentaries by Śāntarakṣita and Atiśa (G.F1) are all found here. For the reader, the boundary to remember is: Kangyur = the Buddha's words; Tengyur = explanations by commentarial masters — corresponding to the "root text + commentary" pair of the Pali system (G.D2), but on a far larger scale.

3. The place of tantra in the canon

In the Kangyur, the tantra section comes after the common sutra sections and occupies a large number of volumes — but this order must be understood correctly: it indicates the sequence of study, not relative importance. The tantras are arranged in four classes (the arrangement common among the later schools — Sarma):

  • Action tantra (Sanskrit: Kriyā tantra): centred on ritual, purification, and mantra recitation — the entry level of tantra.
  • Performance tantra (Sanskrit: Charyā tantra): balancing ritual and meditative visualisation.
  • Yoga tantra (Sanskrit: Yoga tantra): focused on mandala and deity visualisation (G.F2).
  • Highest Yoga tantra (Sanskrit: Anuttarayoga tantra): the highest class — including the two-stage generation-and-completion practices (G.F2) — placed last by the later schools and requiring empowerment and a teacher's guidance before practice.

In other words, the position of tantra in the canon reflects the path already drawn in G.F2: the common sutra sections are the shared foundation (together with Mahāyāna Buddhism — group G.E1 → G.E7); tantra is the upper layer, entered later and under conditions. Tibetan materials therefore devote a large volume to the higher tantras — but teachers advise beginners to study the foundation first: foundational thinking and lamrim (G.F1, G.F2), before approaching tantra.

4. Historical woodblock editions and the Nyingma collection

The two collections above do not exist as a single "book": the canons were copied by hand and then carved on wooden blocks — known as woodblock editions — at scriptural printing monasteries, each producing an edition of different scope and quality. The best-known editions are Narthang (14th century — the early systematising model edition), Tshalpa, and Derge (Tibetan: sde dge, 1733) — regarded as the highest-quality edition, the standard for much research and the main source of modern print editions. How to read a Tibetan canonical citation (simply): text title → edition → volume – page, for example "Sukhāvatī Sutra, Derge edition, volume ka".

The Nyingma school preserves an additional repository: the Nyingma Gyübum (Tibetan: rnying ma rgyud 'bum, "Collected Tantras of the Ancients") — tantras of the first dissemination not included in the Kangyur, including the foundational tantras of the dzogchen summit (G.F2). Connected with this repository is the terma tradition (Tibetan: gter ma, "treasure texts") — according to tradition, Padmasambhava concealed teachings as texts and objects in earth, rock, and mind, to be revealed at the right time by tertön (Tibetan: gter ston, "treasure revealers"). The terma tradition is Nyingma's distinctive textual feature: new texts still "arise" within the old lineage; the tradition's own understanding and the scholarly description of this phenomenon are set side by side — as practised in G.B4 and in G.F2 on tulkus.

5. Reading the Tibetan canon today

The situation for Vietnamese readers is: (1) there is no complete Vietnamese translation — most Kangyur–Tengyur texts exist only in Tibetan with partial English, Chinese, and Japanese translations; (2) many important sutras already have Chinese translations — the Perfection of Wisdom texts, Lotus, Flower Garland, and Pure Land sutras — and the Chinese versions often correspond to the Tibetan ones (two independent translations from Sanskrit — comparable); (3) the 84000 project (84000: Translating the Words of the Buddha) — launched in 2010 — translates the Kangyur and Tengyur into English, published free online, and has already translated many important sutras and treatises.

The most sensible entry point for beginners — following the general advice of teachers — is: do not start with tantra; start with (1) the familiar shared foundation sutras (group E), (2) lamrim texts — "stages of the path" works written to be read — most famously the Lamrim Chenmo (Tibetan: lam rim chen mo, The Great Treatise on the Stages of the Path) by Tsongkhapa (G.F2), available in English translation with commentarial books in many languages; (3) tantra only after guidance — following the empowerment principle (G.F2). The overall catalogue and reading order — including the Tibetan canon — is in G.H1.

6. Summary and next steps

  • The Tibetan scriptural system comprises the Kangyur (around 1,100 texts, around 100 volumes — "the Buddha's words") and the Tengyur (over 4,000 texts, over 200 volumes — "commentaries") — the largest surviving Buddhist textual corpus today.
  • Distinctive features: concentrated translation with standardised terminology (Mahāvyutpatti); a collection of many canonical editions, with no single "official" version; notable woodblock editions: Narthang (14th century), Derge (1733) — the highest-quality standard edition.
  • The tantra section follows the common sutras in study sequence: four classes (Kriyā → Charyā → Yoga → Anuttarayoga) — the highest class requires empowerment; this is a sequence of study, not a ranking of importance.
  • Nyingma preserves the additional Nyingma Gyübum and the terma tradition — "treasure" texts revealed by tertön — a distinctive textual feature.
  • Reading today: no complete Vietnamese translation; much overlaps with Chinese translations; the 84000 project offers free English translations; beginners are advised to study the foundation sutras and lamrim first, not to practise tantra on their own.

Glossary:

  • Vajrayāna Buddhism (Sanskrit: Vajrayāna): the esoteric Buddhist tradition of the Himalayan and Mongolian regions — the esoteric strand within Mahāyāna Buddhism (Sanskrit: Mahāyāna).
  • Tantra (Sanskrit: tantra): esoteric scriptures and practices (G.F1).
  • Kangyur (Tibetan: bka' 'gyur, "Translation of the Word"): the collection of the Buddha's words translated into Tibetan — around 1,100 texts, around 100 volumes; comprising Vinaya, Perfection of Wisdom, Flower Garland, Heap of Jewels, other sutras, and tantras.
  • Tengyur (Tibetan: bstan 'gyur, "Translation of the Treatises"): the collection of commentaries — around 4,000 texts by Indian scholars, over 200 volumes; comprising treatises, commentaries, logic, medicine, and grammar.
  • Pali Canon (Pali: tipiṭaka, "Three Baskets"): the scriptural system of Southern Buddhism — fixed once, with a single standard textual system (G.D2).
  • Mahāvyutpatti (Sanskrit: Mahāvyutpatti): the standard lexicon issued in the early 9th century — unifying the Tibetan rendering of Sanskrit terms throughout the canon.
  • Action / Performance / Yoga / Highest Yoga tantra (Sanskrit: Kriyā / Charyā / Yoga / Anuttarayoga tantra): the four tantra classes — from purificatory ritual to the highest two-stage practices, requiring empowerment.
  • Empowerment (Sanskrit: abhiṣeka): the rite conferring authorisation to practise an esoteric teaching (G.F2).
  • Woodblock edition: a canonical edition carved on wooden blocks at printing monasteries — Narthang (14th century, early model), Tshalpa, Derge (sde dge, 1733 — the highest-quality edition).
  • Nyingma Gyübum (Tibetan: rnying ma rgyud 'bum, "Collected Tantras of the Ancients"): the Nyingma school's own tantra collection — first-dissemination tantras not found in the Kangyur.
  • Terma (Tibetan: gter ma, "treasure texts"): concealed teachings hidden by Padmasambhava to be revealed later by tertön (gter ston, "treasure revealers"); Nyingma's distinctive textual feature.
  • Lamrim Chenmo (Tibetan: lam rim chen mo, The Great Treatise on the Stages of the Path): Tsongkhapa's great "stages of the path" work — the most widely read entry text to the path in the Gelug approach.
  • Candrakīrti: 7th-century Madhyamaka commentator — his treatises hold a central place in the Tibetan curriculum.
  • 84000 (84000: Translating the Words of the Buddha): the global project launched in 2010 — translating the Kangyur and Tengyur into English, published free online.
