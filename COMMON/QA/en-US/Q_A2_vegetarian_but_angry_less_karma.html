Q.A2. If a person eats a vegetarian diet but is still prone to anger, do they really create less karma?

The previous article — Q.A1 — clarified that eating meat does not mechanically equal the karma of killing. The reverse question naturally arises at once: if a person eats a vegetarian diet — yet is frequently hot-tempered, hateful, and wishes suffering on others — do they really create less karma? Place two people side by side: Person A keeps a strict vegetarian diet but often hurls abuse, holds grudges, and rejoices when others fail; Person B still eats meat, does not kill, does not order killing, but behaves gently and tries to reduce greed — hatred — delusion (Pali: lobha, dosa, moha - greed, hatred, delusion). Who creates less karma? This question touches the very core of the teaching on karma — and the answer is not about declaring one of them the "winner."

1. Ask the question again: less of which karma?

Karma (Pali: kamma - intentional action) — by the canonical definition — lies in intention (Pali: cetanā - intention): "Intention, I declare, is karma." After intention, karma is performed through the three doors: bodily deeds, speech, and the deliberate thoughts of the mind itself. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 6.63 (Nibbedhika Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]. Article G.C4 has presented this theme in full.

Hence to ask "who has less karma" while looking at only one fragment of the bodily door (food) is not enough — hatred spreads across all three doors: in the mental door as resentment and wishing suffering on others, in the verbal door as cruel words, in the bodily door as harmful actions. The more precise question is not "does this person eat vegetarian food?", but: "which states of mind is this person nourishing, and which behaviors arise from them?".

2. Hatred is also karma — not only in hands and feet

One misunderstanding must be cleared away at once: "hatred that stays only in the head is not karma." According to the canonical analysis, karma is tied to a deliberate mental current — and hatred (Pali: byāpāda - ill will) is one of the five hindrances (Pali: nīvaraṇa - hindrance), the five states that most directly obstruct concentration and wisdom (the full list and their antidotes are in article M.C1.3).

But "hatred is karma" does not mean "every fleeting angry thought is a heavy karma." Levels must be distinguished:

  • A passing thought: a flash of anger that appears and passes — if it is merely noticed and released, its karmic weight is very thin.
  • Nourished hatred: "I want that person to suffer" — hatred has become deliberate.
  • Reinforced hatred: repeatedly rehearsing revenge, retelling the story to make the anger stronger.
  • Hatred turned into speech and action: scolding, slander, retaliation, sabotage.

Deliberateness, repetition, nourishment, and turning into action make the karmic stream progressively more significant. The Dhammapada (Pali: Dhammapada - verses of the Dhamma) opens with two verses placing mind at the center: mind precedes all things — an unwholesome mind brings suffering in its wake, a purified mind brings well-being — as a shadow follows its form. [Source: Dhammapada, verses 1–2 - Theravāda Buddhism].

3. What is vegetarianism in terms of karma — a wholesome deed or a form?

Is vegetarianism wholesome karma? The answer: it depends on the mind behind the choice. When a person deliberately eats vegetarian food because they do not want animals to be killed, want to reduce their own participation in killing, and want to nourish compassion — then genuinely wholesome states stand behind it: loving-kindness (Pali: mettā - loving-kindness), compassion (Pali: karuṇā - compassion), the wish not to cause harm, and awareness of the consequences of one's behavior. Article M.D2.1 presents the cultivation of loving-kindness as a meditation object.

But compare two motives:

  • Case A: "I eat vegetarian food out of care for living beings; I do not want to contribute to killing" — a wholesome motive.
  • Case B: "I eat vegetarian food to prove I am nobler than meat-eaters" — the outward behavior is the same, but behind it stand conceit (the mind that places oneself above others), contempt for others, and clinging (Pali: upādāna - clinging).

The same action, different minds, different karmic value. This is why the teaching on karma cannot be reduced to a table of "dish X = good, dish Y = bad."

4. Two streams of karma cannot be measured with a single ruler

Return to Person A and Person B at the opening. Can we conclude "B definitely has less karma than A"? No — because two different karmic streams are being compared:

  • Person A has one considerable wholesome deed (reduced connection with killing), but at the same time many states of hatred and many hateful behaviors through words, thoughts, and deeds.
  • Person B has one remaining ethical problem (consuming meat; the previous article — Q.A1 — analyzed the levels of involvement), but may have no intent to kill and many other wholesome states of mind.

A complete assessment must look at the whole chain: intention — object — degree of greed, hatred, and delusion — deliberateness — whether the action was carried out — consequences — and the mental habits being reinforced. A person's entire karma cannot be graded with a single criterion.

One important principle must be stated clearly: one wholesome karma does not automatically erase another unwholesome karma. "I eat vegetarian food" gives no one a license for hatred; and "one good deed" does not automatically balance every bad deed. Buddhism looks at karma by the nature and conditions of each action, not by adding and subtracting scores.

5. Do not reduce everything to a single cause

In the opposite direction, another misunderstanding must also be guarded against: attributing all of life's outcomes to past karma. The discourses record the Buddha rejecting a view called the view that everything is caused by past deeds (Pali: pubbekata-vāda - the view that everything is caused by past deeds): the pleasure and pain people experience may come from many sources — illnesses, bodily humors, changes of weather, harm done by others, and one's own karma — not from a single cause alone. [Source: Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 36.21 (Sīvaka Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism].

Nor can others conclude "what happened to you is your old karma" as an absolute verdict — the teaching rejects both condemnation and exemption. Karma is one of the strongest causal streams, but not the only one; article G.C4 has already given the details.

6. The cycle of resentment — why hatred reinforces itself

The teaching places hatred in an especially dangerous group because hatred has one characteristic: it easily feeds on itself. You think about what angered you → remember it → retell the story → gather evidence that the other person is bad → feel justified → grow angrier → want revenge. The person who first caused the hurt created only one event; your own mind recreates that event dozens, hundreds of times.

The Dhammapada states this cycle very compactly: one who keeps holding the thought "they insulted me, they struck me, they defeated me" — in such a person hatred never ceases; hatred cannot cease by hatred; hatred ceases by non-hatred. [Source: Dhammapada, verses 3–5 - Theravāda Buddhism].

This shows: hatred is not a "secondary moral issue" — it is a mechanism continuously producing suffering. A vegetarian diet may reduce one form of outward harm; but if hatred keeps operating, another source of suffering remains fully intact — and that source is using the practitioner's own life as its birthplace.

7. When a beautiful practice is used by a mistaken mind

Consider a paradox worth reflecting on. A person says: "I eat vegetarian food out of compassion." Then gradually thinks: "All meat-eaters are contemptible." Then they despise others, hurl abuse, wish retribution on them, and rejoice in others' misfortune. What happened?

  • Starting point: an action that may point in the right direction — not eating meat in order to reduce harm.
  • Then clinging appears: "I am better than you."
  • Conceit and hatred seize that practice as their instrument.

The result: a rightly-directed practice used by a wrongly-directed mind. Compassion for animals walks side by side with a lack of compassion for human beings — at that point the path has veered from its goal. This is exactly why one must be wary of turning a moral practice into an identity: when "vegetarian" becomes something to prove "I am better than others," it nourishes precisely what the path of practice seeks to reduce — greed, hatred, and delusion. Vegetarianism is a very good means; but the means is not the goal of liberation.

8. Keeping precepts and training the mind must go together

The doctrinal frame that puts these pieces in their places is the three trainings (Pali: sikkhā - three trainings): virtue restrains harmful behavior; concentration steadies the mind; wisdom sees clearly into the roots of greed, hatred, and delusion. If only outward form is kept without transforming the mind, virtue can turn into identity — and identity breeds self-clinging (clinging to a fixed self): "I am a vegetarian" → "I am better than meat-eaters" → "you do not eat vegetarian food, so you are lower than me." Article G.C6 presents the three trainings and the four qualities of loving-kindness — compassion — sympathetic joy — equanimity in daily life.

Instead of only asking "did I eat meat today?", one can widen this into daily questions of self-examination:

  • Today, did my mind hurt anyone? Am I holding a grudge?
  • Did I deliberately say something that wounded another person?
  • Did I rejoice when someone else failed?
  • Did I look down on others because I thought myself more moral?
  • Can I reduce the harm I cause by a little more?
  • Can I look even at those who do what I dislike with a less hateful mind?

Facing anger is also a trainable skill — observing anger instead of following it or suppressing it is presented in article M.C2.2. And most importantly: practice is not "never being allowed to get angry" — a practitioner may still feel irritation, recognize the anger, and then not nourish it. What decides is what you do with the anger after it appears.

9. Summary and next steps

  • "Less karma" must be asked again: karma through which door — body, speech, or mind; a vegetarian diet touches only one fragment of the bodily door.
  • Intention is the core of karma; nourished hatred turned into harmful words and deeds is fully-formed unwholesome karma — even in one who eats vegetarian food.
  • A vegetarian diet out of compassion is a wholesome deed; out of conceit it becomes a place where unwholesome states are nourished.
  • One wholesome karma does not automatically erase another unwholesome karma; there is no "discount on hatred-karma for being vegetarian."
  • A meat-eater is likewise not automatically equated with direct killing (previous article — Q.A1); and do not reduce everything to a single cause (SN 36.21).
  • The balanced formula: reduce outward killing while also reducing inward hatred — both are parts of compassion, supporting rather than negating each other.
  • A memorable line: a vegetarian diet makes the dining table less violent; only transforming hatred makes one's own mind less violent.

Glossary:

  • Karma (Pali: kamma): intentional action of body - speech - mind together with its natural results; neither fate nor plus-minus points in a judgment system.
  • Intention (Pali: cetanā): the core of karma — "Intention, I declare, is karma" (AN 6.63); with the same outward result, different intentions mean different karma.
  • Hatred (Pali: byāpāda): the mind wishing harm on others; one of the five hindrances; together with greed and delusion it forms the three unwholesome roots — the three poisons of the mind.
  • Hindrance (Pali: nīvaraṇa): the five states obstructing concentration and wisdom — sensual desire, ill will, dullness, restlessness-and-remorse, doubt.
  • Three doors of action: karma is performed through deeds, words, and deliberate thoughts.
  • Loving-kindness (Pali: mettā) and Compassion (Pali: karuṇā): two of the four immeasurables (four qualities of mind cultivated as meditation objects); article M.D2.1 in the Meditation section presents the cultivation of loving-kindness.
  • Conceit: the mind comparing oneself with others — better, equal, or worse — and swelling up; a fetter to be abandoned on the path to liberation.
  • Clinging (Pali: upādāna): the stage after craving — craving "wants," clinging "grasps and will not let go."
  • Self-clinging: taking "I" and "mine" as the center — it can turn even a beautiful moral practice into its instrument.
  • Three trainings (Pali: sikkhā): the practice framework of Buddhism — morality — mental training — wisdom; virtue is the foundation of concentration, concentration the foundation of wisdom.
  • The view that everything is caused by past deeds (Pali: pubbekata-vāda): one of the extreme views the Buddha rejected in SN 36.21 — the pleasure and pain people experience have many causes, karma being only one.
  • Dhammapada (Pali: Dhammapada): 423 short verses in the Minor Collection (the Pāli Canon of Theravāda Buddhism) — renowned for their compact teachings on mind, virtue, and wisdom.
  • Vegetarian diet: a diet without meat or fish; in East Asian Buddhism it has become a practice of compassion; its value depends on the mind behind the choice and does not automatically decide "more or less karma."
