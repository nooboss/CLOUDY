Q.D1. Does karma really determine a person's fate?

Opening the article group on karma and cause and effect is a very everyday question: does karma (Pali: kamma - intentional action) really determine a person's fate? Why is one person born into many advantages, while another faces hardship from the very start? The kind-hearted still meeting misfortune, wrongdoers still succeeding — is karma still "working"? And if everything is already written by karma, can people still change their lives? The Buddhist answer lies between two extremes: karma truly influences life, but karma is not a pre-written verdict — and precisely the space between "what has been sown" and "what is being sown" is where human freedom exists.

1. The short answer

  • "Does karma affect life?" → Yes. Deliberate bodily - verbal - mental action creates tendencies and consequences: they affect how we think, how we act, relationships, and — in the rebirth worldview — even conditions met in later lives (article Q.B1).
  • "Does karma absolutely decide fate, leaving people unable to change?" → It should not be understood so. Karma is one part of the conditions making up life, not a closed script of an entire life.
  • And one oft-forgotten point: not everything happening to us comes from karma — life is the interplay of very many kinds of causes and conditions (section 3). Past karma influences, but the present is precisely where new causes are being created — there, people still have choice.

2. Karma is not "fate"

In everyday speech, karma is sometimes used as a synonym for fate: "that is my karma," "with such karma there is no escape." Repeated so often, karma gradually becomes an invisible hand that has pre-written life — a vague fatalism. But in the teaching, karma is first of all deliberate action. A discourse sums it up: "Intention, I declare, is karma" — intention (Pali: cetanā - intention) is the core of karma. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 6.63 (Nibbedhika Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

The crux lies in the word "deliberate." For karma is not only about "what has happened to me" — karma is also about what I am thinking, what I am nourishing, what I am saying, what I am doing, and what kind of person I am becoming through those choices. An action driven by greed, hatred, delusion creates different tendencies from one driven by kindness and wakefulness. In other words: karma does not belong only to the past; karma is being created right in the present moment — in very small choices we usually overlook: each time choosing to answer with hatred or calm, to lie or be truthful, to take revenge or let go.

3. Not everything in life comes from karma

This is the most misunderstood point, and the discourses speak of it very clearly. The wanderer Sīvaka once argued: "whatever feeling one feels, all comes from old karma" — and the Buddha rejected it: some feelings arise from karma, but others arise from bile disorders, phlegm disorders, wind disorders, imbalance of the three, seasonal change, wrong care, assault — apart from karmic cause. [Source: Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 36.21 (Sīvaka Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism] In the same spirit, the Devadaha Sutta criticizes the view of some paths of the time that "all pleasure and pain come from old karma," for then practice would be meaningless. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 101 (Devadaha Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

The later Theravāda commentarial system further gathers this into five orders (Pali: pañca-niyāma - fivefold cosmic order): physical-weather order, seed-germ order, karma order, mind order, phenomena order — karma is only one of those five. [Source: the Pāli commentarial system - Theravāda Buddhism] That is: bodily illness, crop failure, sudden accidents, how others behave... may come from very many causes belonging to other orders — one cannot look at an event and conclude "this is karma."

4. Karma needs conditions and time to ripen

Even the karmic portion does not ripen like a vending machine: put good in, instantly get luck; put evil in, instantly get calamity. The Canon uses the farmer-sowing-seed image: whether a seed sprouts, fast or slow, still depends on soil, water, season — karma's ripening likewise needs conditions (Pali: paccaya - supporting condition) and time. A discourse further describes the difference between two kinds of sowing-ground: the same seed, sown on good and bad soil, yields different results; the same karma, in one who regularly practices and one who does not, ripens very differently (article Q.D3 will go deep). [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 3.76 (Nidāna Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism; the image "karma is the field, consciousness the seed, craving the moisture"]

Hence, looking at one moment of a person — rich or poor, healthy or ill, success or failure — and concluding their whole karma is a methodological error: we see one fruit, not the whole field and sowing seasons. The classic question "why do good people still meet misfortune, bad people live in comfort?" is precisely looking at such a moment — it is discussed separately in article Q.I4.

5. A dangerous mistake: using karma to blame

One understanding of karma can cause great harm: "people suffer because of their karma." Said unwisely, this turns empathy into judgment: the poor told they are poor from karma; the ill hinted illness is retribution; the abused suggested to be "repaying karma." Such words do not lighten the sufferer's pain — they make people feel they deserve suffering.

But as section 3 saw: karma is only one of many causal layers, and no one can look at a circumstance and declare "I know exactly why this person bears this." The causal spirit does not aim to make us judges of others' karma. Instead of asking "what did this person do to deserve suffering?", a kind mind asks: "what can I do now to help this person suffer less?" — that is the constructive application of causality (the next article - Q.D2 discusses more deeply "is all suffering one's own karma?").

6. The past explains, but does not decide

Picture life as a boat: the past is like current, wind, and the boat's position — we cannot turn back and erase waves already passed. But that does not mean the rudder cannot be adjusted. Today we may stand in difficulty from previously formed conditions; yet how we respond to present circumstances keeps creating new causes. One once hot-tempered may keep nourishing hot temper — or begin observing anger. One once selfish may continue selfish — or begin learning to share. Old mistakes may become excuses for further mistakes — or causes for growth.

A very practical understanding: karma is like habits repeated. One outburst is only a reaction; but each time insulted, flaring up, speaking hurtfully, regretting, then repeating — we are creating a tendency. Thoughts nourished many times grow familiar; words repeated many times become communication style; deeds repeated many times become habit; habits form character; and character affects further choices. The past explains part of the present person, but does not necessarily decide the person we will become.

7. "Transforming karma" transforms what?

"Transforming karma" is sometimes understood as something mystical: performing some rite to wipe old karma clean. But rightly seen, transforming karma is first of all transforming one's own way of living and acting mind. If before reacting with hatred, today practice pausing. If before speaking hurtfully, today practice speaking truthfully, with kindness. If before thinking only of self-benefit, today practice seeing others' benefit. If before blaming circumstances, today begin owning the part one can change. Each change is small — but karma too is made of small things repeated. Transformation usually happens quietly: one thought, one word, one restraint, one forgiveness, one non-retaliation — then one more.

It must also be said plainly: because karma lies in action being created, transforming karma lies not in substitute prayers, but in real change of mind and living (offering-rites "erasing karma" for others are discussed in article Q.H3). And a deeper question: "who" is creating karma? As the previous group presented, the "I" is no unchanging entity — karma is a continuing stream of cause and effect, not a prisoner jailed by a verdict (article Q.B2 on non-self). That view opens something very beautiful: we need not deny the past to change the future — only begin creating a new causal stream.

8. Three traps: fear, despair, resignation

  • The fear trap: misunderstanding karma, people live in fear — fearing karma-making, retribution, every mistake returning. But understanding causality aims not to frighten, but to help us take more responsibility for our minds — careful with what we sow, not from fear of a punishing power, but from understanding we ourselves help make our future. Like a seed-sower: no need to fear seeds, only to know which seeds one sows.
  • The despair trap: looking back at too many mistakes and thinking "my life can no longer change." Were people utterly unchangeable, then repentance, doing good, meditating, developing wisdom would all be meaningless — yet it is precisely changeability that makes the practice path meaningful (article Q.D3 discusses deeply: can past-created karma be changed?). The past needs acknowledging — but need not be worshipped.
  • The resignation trap: "with such karma, endure," "fate has come," "whatever I do is the same." That attitude inadvertently turns causality into an excuse for inaction. If ill — still need health care. If in difficulty — still need solutions. If a relationship is toxic — still need boundaries. If a mistake was made — still need repair. Rightly understanding karma does not make us resign; it makes us more wakeful in each choice (article Q.D4 specifically discusses: if karma decides everything, need people still strive?).

9. Summary and next steps

  • Karma is deliberate action — with intention as core (AN 6.63); it is being created right now, not only past.
  • Not everything comes from karma (SN 36.21; MN 101): karma is only one of many causal layers operating life.
  • Karma needs conditions and time to ripen — like seeds needing soil, water, season; one moment cannot reveal the whole karmic field.
  • Do not use karma to blame the suffering — change "what do they deserve?" into "what can I help?".
  • The past explains but does not decide: transforming karma means transforming living; freedom lies in how the present is answered.
  • Avoid the three traps: fear, despair, resignation.

Glossary:

  • Karma (Pali: kamma): deliberate bodily - verbal - mental action together with the tendencies and results it creates; neither fate nor anyone's judgment.
  • Intention (Pali: cetanā): the core of karma — "Intention, I declare, is karma" (AN 6.63).
  • Fatalism: denied by Buddhism; karma differs from fate in that karma is still being newly created each moment.
  • Feeling (Pali: vedanā): per SN 36.21, feeling may come from karma and also from many other causes (bodily disorders, weather, wrong care, assault...).
  • Fivefold cosmic order (Pali: pañca-niyāma): physical-weather (utu), seed-germ (bīja), karma (kamma), mind (citta), phenomena (dhamma) orders; a Theravāda commentarial synthesis, not a single discourse's verbatim.
  • Supporting condition (Pali: paccaya): karma like seeds, conditions like soil - water - season; lacking conditions, fruit does not ripen or ripens differently.
  • Dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda): the general frame for all causes and conditions, of which karma is one part.
  • Cycle of rebirth (Pali: saṃsāra).
  • Non-self (Pali: anattā): karma is a continuing cause-and-effect stream, not a verdict jailing a fixed "self."
  • Loving-kindness (Pali: mettā).
  • Repentance (recognizing faults and resolving not to repeat them): unlike self-reproach; the start of a new causal stream.
