Q.H3. Can making offerings for the dead change their karma?

This is the group's central question on offerings: the two previous articles discussed votive paper and donating — now the hardest: can offerings "fix" the dead's karma? A father in life did many wrongs; descendants offer generously, hold grand rites — is that karma erased? The answer demands a very important distinction in the karma teaching: changing created karma differs from receiving benefit from new wholesome karma others make.

1. The short answer

Per early Buddhist discourses, offerings should not be understood as "erasing" or "fixing" the dead's old karma — created karma does not vanish merely because the living hold rites. However, the discourses also clearly state that the living can give, offer, and dedicate to the departed; under suitable rebirth circumstances, the departed may benefit from those wholesome deeds. So: if "changing karma" means erasing karma they created and turning it into never-happened — no basis to say so; if "changing" means creating one more wholesome condition that may benefit the dead under suitable conditions — scripturally grounded.

2. What is "the dead's karma" — who inherits karma?

Karma is no verdict a god records in books awaiting trial — karma attaches to intention (Pali: cetanā - intention) and beings' own actions (article Q.D1). One discourse teaches very clearly and directly: beings are owners of karma, heirs of karma, born of karma, bound to karma, supported by karma — karma divides beings by lowness and excellence. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 135 (Cūḷakammavibhaṅga Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism] The important idea: each person inherits karmic causes they themselves created.

Hence one cannot picture the chain: person A does evil → creates bad karma → dies → family holds rites → bad karma erased. That is not the discourses' karma model. Relatives cannot say "my father did wrong, but I hold a grand rite to erase it all."

3. What the discourses say directly: "suitable realm"

Yet saying "offerings help the dead not at all" is also inaccurate. One discourse begins with life's very question: a brahmin asks the Buddha — "offerings we make for the dead, hoping departed relatives benefit; do they truly receive?" The Buddha answers: "Where there is a suitable realm there is benefit; where unsuitable, none" — then explains: those creating much unwholesome karma reborn into hell or unsuitable realms do not receive the living's giving that way; those reborn in the hungry-ghost realm "dependent on others" do receive; those reborn human or heavenly are nourished by their own lives. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 10.177 (Jāṇussoṇi Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

Suitable realm means: offerings may benefit if the departed are in a rebirth realm where that benefit can reach them. The Tirokuḍḍa Sutta (already presented in article Q.H1) details the typical case: relatives in the hungry-ghost realm gathering when the living give and dedicate; the discourse also stresses weeping, wailing, and grief do not benefit the dead — rightly placed offerings and doing good do benefit. [Source: Tirokuḍḍa Sutta (Khuddakapāṭha 7; Petavatthu 1.5) - Theravāda Buddhism] This guidance differs greatly from "the more weeping, the more the dead are helped."

4. So what do offerings really do? Two causal streams

The most careful phrasing: the living create new wholesome karma, then direct mind/vows toward the departed; under suitable conditions, the departed may benefit — not the living taking part of their "merit-points" then erasing part of the dead's bad karma. Two separate streams can be pictured:

  • The departed's stream: their own old karma → karmic fruits per their conditions.
  • The living's stream: doing good → creating new wholesome karma → dedication/goodwill → the departed may benefit if conditions suit.

These two streams need not become one "addition-subtraction." A concrete example: person A in life often hot-tempered, deceitful, harmful; after passing, A's children offer, help the poor, keep precepts, dedicate to A. One should not say "children have fully repaid father's karma" — closer to the discourses: "children have created wholesome karma; if father is in a realm suitable for receiving benefit from those wholesome deeds, father may benefit." While karma A themselves created still belongs to A's karmic stream. Also note: if relatives are reborn human, they are living new lives — do not picture "they still wait somewhere for family offerings."

5. Not erasing — yet affecting: avoiding two extremes

  • Extreme 1 — "created karma admits no further influence": too rigid. Karma is conditional; present life is many conditions meeting; newly nourished wholesome karma may change the overall causal stream (article Q.D3).
  • Extreme 2 — "grand enough rites erase any karma": also wrong. The discourses give no "small rite → small benefit; grand rite → wholly changed fate" formula; they stress rebirth-conditions, giving-qualities, and keep a very clear limit of "where possible" and "where impossible" (AN 10.177).

Hence one should not affirm "the grander the offering, the surely hell-escaped dead" — the discourses directly list hell as an "unsuitable place" for the living's offerings. Nor turn funerals into offering-races: "the other house offered 100 million, ours must offer 200 million" turns remembrance into merit competition — value lies not in "who spends more," but in whether mind truly shifts from grief to wholesome action. A most beautiful memorial is not only grand offerings, but the living growing kinder after remembering the departed.

6. How does "dedication" differ from "karma-transfer"?

"Karma-transfer" easily misleads: if meaning taking A's karma onto B — that is not the discourses' presentation. The living create their own karma; the dead inherit their own. The more fitting phrase is dedication of merit: the living do truly wholesome deeds — giving, offering, precept-keeping, practice — then vow "may this wholesome karma benefit departed relatives." This understanding keeps two principles at once: not self-claiming "I surely transfer all karma to them," nor denying "the dead can benefit from nothing the living do" — AN 10.177 stands precisely in the middle: benefit in suitable cases; none in unsuitable.

7. The living also change — and honest cognitive limits

One sometimes-forgotten benefit: when descendants remember the departed by charity, offering, precept-keeping, practice — the living are creating new karmic streams for themselves. The Tirokuḍḍa Sutta ends precisely on this point: rightly done offerings may both benefit the departed in suitable circumstances and merit the living. Memorial rites may thus become an opportunity turning grief into wholesome karma — turning memory into wholesome karma, instead of only "sending property."

And honesty with limits is needed: we cannot surely know "which realm relatives presently inhabit" by ordinary methods. Hence do not say "surely they are receiving the rites" — the careful phrasing: per the discourses, there are cases where the departed may benefit from the living's wholesome deeds; but that depends on rebirth-conditions (article Q.H4 continues on chanting).

8. Summary and next steps

  • Erasing the dead's old karma: no. Each person is heir to their own karma (MN 135).
  • Creating further wholesome conditions that may benefit: yes — under suitable rebirth-conditions ("suitable realm," AN 10.177).
  • The case the discourses state clearest: relatives in the hungry-ghost realm receiving benefit from dedicated giving (Tirokuḍḍa); hell and other realms do not receive by the same mechanism.
  • Weeping benefits less than doing good — turning pain into good action.
  • Dedication ≠ karma-transfer: merit does not move like money between two accounts.
  • Avoid both extremes: "inviolable karma" and "grand rites erase all karma."
  • The living create their own karma in memorializing — the most beautiful rite is the living becoming better.

Glossary:

  • Heir of one's own actions (Pali: kammadāyāda): how MN 135 describes each being — karma cannot be "repaid for" or "erased for."
  • Suitable realm: the rebirth-realm condition deciding whether the departed receive benefit from dedicated giving (AN 10.177).
  • Dedication of merit: vowing wholesome deeds toward others' benefit — unlike "karma-transfer" (the matter-moving image).
  • Intention (Pali: cetanā): karma's core — "Intention, I declare, is karma" (AN 6.63).
  • Two extremes: "karma uninfluenceable" and "grand rites erase all karma" — the discourses walk between these two.
  • Hungry ghost (Pali: peta): the realm described as "suitable" for receiving benefit from relatives' giving.
  • Hell realm (Pali: niraya): listed by the discourses as an "unsuitable place" for the living's offerings (AN 10.177).
