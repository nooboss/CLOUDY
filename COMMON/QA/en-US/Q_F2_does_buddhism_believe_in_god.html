Q.F2. Does Buddhism believe in a God?

After the demon question (previous article - Q.F1), the God question is asked most when comparing Buddhism with other religions. The question seems to need only "yes" or "no," but the Buddhist teaching poses the issue differently: Buddhism does not take an omnipotent, eternal Creator as the teaching's foundation — yet the discourses still speak of many kinds of beings in heavenly realms. The full answer lies in distinguishing a "creator God" from "devas," and in seeing Buddhism's true focus rightly.

1. The short answer

Place two meanings side by side, and the answer appears clearly:

  • If "God" means an entity supreme, omnipotent, omniscient, eternal, creating the universe and the ultimate source of all existence — then Buddhism does not build its teaching on belief in such an entity. This is the most important point.
  • Yet Buddhist discourses do speak of many kinds of beings in heavenly realms — devas (Pali: deva - celestial being) and even Brahmā realms (Pali: Brahmā - Brahmā) — living far longer and more blissfully than humans. "There are deities" is not synonymous with "there is an omnipotent Creator": these are two entirely different concepts.

Hence avoid both extreme sayings: "Buddhism denies all deities" — untrue, the discourses speak much of devas; and "Buddhism believes in many Gods" — also untrue, for these beings still lie within rebirth (Pali: saṃsāra - cycle of rebirth) and are not the final refuge. More precisely: Buddhism takes no Creator as foundation, yet acknowledges heavenly realms and superhuman beings in its cosmology (article G.C4 presents this realm-map).

2. What does "God" mean — and what do the discourses say of deities

In religious language, "God" usually denotes an entity: supreme, omnipotent, universe-creating, the first cause of all things, and sometimes the absolute ground of morality. Theistic religions' (theism) hallmark is teaching revolving around that being.

Buddhism has a different structure. The discourses describe many being-kinds — humans, animals, peta, yakkha, devas, Brahmās (previous article - Q.F1 already listed) — but no "eternal creator God" kind standing outside conditional law. A deva in the Buddhist cosmology may have extremely long life, very great powers, very high rank — yet still:

  • subject to impermanence (Pali: anicca - impermanence) — existing then ceasing;
  • subject to karma (Pali: kamma - intentional action);
  • and unescaped from birth-and-death without liberation.

Extremely long life is not synonymous with immortality; extremely great powers not synonymous with omnipotence; high rank not synonymous with ultimate reality. This is a very characteristic Buddhist point. [Source: Dīgha Nikāya, DN 1 (Brahmajāla Sutta) — listing and analyzing contemporary creator-views - Theravāda Buddhism]

3. Is Brahmā God?

They should not be equated. In ancient India's context, Brahmā held a very high position — living immensely long, immensely authoritative — so that some such beings deemed themselves eternal creators. Buddhist discourses record a striking narrative: after one world-cycle dissolved then re-formed, one being was born first in the Brahmā realm. Born before all other beings, that one thought: "I am the creator, father of all, eternal and unchanging"; while later-born beings — reborn there — also believed that one was the creator. The discourse analyzes: that is only confusion — that one is merely the first-born being in a world-cycle, not the true cause of other beings. [Source: Dīgha Nikāya, DN 1 (Brahmajāla Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

This narrative's lesson is very deep: a being may mistake long life and high rank for absolute creative power. In Buddhism's presentation, Brahmā remains a being in the conditioned world — still within arising and ceasing, not an eternal Creator standing outside dependent origination. Comparing these views among traditions is presented in a non-sectarian spirit (article G.G2).

4. Without God, who made the universe?

This is a very natural question. Buddhism answers not "another god made the universe," but changes how the question is put. Dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda - dependent origination) views phenomena by causes and conditions: a phenomenon appears because suitable conditions exist; those conditions in turn depend on other conditions (article G.C3 presents in full). The focus is not finding a "first person" standing outside the whole process.

And be clear: dependent origination does not mean "everything appears naturally without causes." A tree needs seed, soil, water, light; an anger needs contact, recognition, memory, many psychological conditions. Reality is a causal network, not causeless chaos. "Who made the first cause?" leads to a loop: answering "God needs no cause" already accepts a reality needing no outer cause — Buddhism asks back: why must causeless reality be a willing, personal subject? The world can be seen through conditional relations — precisely the dependent-origination spirit.

5. So is Buddhism "atheist"?

It should not be settled with one word. If "atheist" (atheism) means "not accepting an omnipotent creator God," then Buddhism is very close to that label. But if "atheist" means "denying all deities, heavens, supernatural entities," then the label does not fit the discourses. The careful phrasing: Buddhism takes no absolute creator God as foundation, yet acknowledges heavenly realms and superhuman beings in its cosmology.

Also distinguish Dhamma teaching from community religious culture: in many Buddhist cultures there are rites, offerings, deva ceremonies, guardian deities, folk beliefs. A cultural rite does not necessarily reflect the teaching's philosophical ground — at the teaching level, devas are not the practice path's final refuge. The supreme-being question is continued in article Q.F3: why the Buddha did not make that question the teaching's center.

6. The Buddha is not God

This is a very common misunderstanding among beginners: "the Buddha is a god Buddhists worship." In the discourses, "Buddha" means "the awake one," "the awakened one" — a title describing wakefulness, not a creator's name (article G.A1 presents in full). The historical Buddha is regarded as an awakened human who pointed out the path — the lost respect one who found the exit, not because that one made the forest, but because that one knows the way out.

The "salvation" vs "path-pointing" difference is huge: Buddhism does not picture begging an almighty being to practice for us. He cannot awaken for you, cannot erase clinging for you. Practitioners must still observe, practice, transform themselves — presented clearly in article Q.F4 (why the Buddha does not return to save people) and article Q.F5 (who the historical Buddha is).

7. Where does morality rest without God?

This is a very strong question, and Buddhism has its own answer: morality rests on karma and causality. Not "do not do evil because a god will punish," but unwholesome action itself creates suffering and nourishes unwholesome tendencies:

  • Do not lie — for lying distorts mind, breaks trust, and next lying grows easier: one's own moral barrier erodes.
  • Do not kill — for that act injures and reinforces violence in mind.
  • Be compassionate — for compassion creates good conditions for self and others, while transforming the practitioner's own mind.

Karma needs no "cosmic accountant" bookkeeping. It is the causal network of deliberate action — consequences operating conditionally (article group Q.D discusses karma and causality deeply). This is why Buddhist morality stands on understanding suffering and causality, needing no outer command.

8. What about prayer and merit-seeking?

In many Buddhist traditions, "prayer" means very differently from begging an omnipotent god to change universal laws for us: vowing, contemplation, Buddha-recitation, repentance, mind-directing, dedication, nourishing loving-kindness. Here, vows act first on the practitioner's own mind-direction and action — vowing for less hatred, to live kindly, to share merit with others.

Doctrinally, the Buddha should not be turned into "a reward-granting god": if only begging money, luck, avoiding all illness sufficed, the practice path would shrink into a begging – granting system, with mind still ruled by greed and fear. Vowing and practice can create motivation, mind-direction, wholesome action, mindfulness — i.e. create new causes and conditions — but one prayer does not automatically erase all causality (article Q.D3 discusses changing karma). Article group Q.H specifically discusses offerings and merit-seeking.

9. Summary and next steps

  • Buddhism takes no omnipotent, eternal Creator as foundation of teaching and condition for liberation.
  • Yet the discourses speak of devas, Brahmās and superhuman realms — these beings remain in rebirth, subject to impermanence and karma, not the final refuge.
  • Brahmā "deeming himself creator" is a narrative criticizing confusion between long life – powers and omnipotence – eternity.
  • The universe's origin is seen through dependent origination — a conditional network — instead of a personal first cause.
  • Morality rests on karma and causality; prayer means vowing, mind-directing, dedication — not miracle-begging.
  • The Buddha is an awakened one and path-pointer, not a creator.

Glossary:

  • God (Creator God / Supreme Being): a supreme, omnipotent, omniscient, eternal entity creating the universe — per monotheistic religions' common definition; this article uses that definition for comparison.
  • Devas (Pali: deva): heavenly-realm beings, living longer and more blissfully than humans yet still in rebirth, still subject to impermanence and karma.
  • Brahmā (Pali: Brahmā): high-meditation-realm beings in the triple world; the discourses record a Great Brahmā deeming himself creator — analyzed by the Buddha as misunderstanding.
  • Theism; Atheism: two religious-studies concepts; Buddhism fits neatly into neither word, both taking no creator as ground and denying no devas.
  • Cycle of rebirth (Pali: saṃsāra): birth-and-death continuing per karma and conditions.
  • Karma (Pali: kamma): deliberate bodily - verbal - mental action with its natural results; "Intention, I declare, is karma" (AN 6.63).
  • Impermanence (Pali: anicca): everything formed by conditions changes and ceases — including long-lived devas.
  • Conditioned phenomena: opposed to unconditioned phenomena like nirvana; Brahmās belong to the conditioned.
  • Dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda): the conditional law — this existing, that exists; helping answer "who made the universe" differently.
  • Dedication of merit: offering wholesome deeds' merit to someone or all beings.
  • Dhamma teaching — distinguished from religious culture: community rites and customs may be richer than the ground teaching.
