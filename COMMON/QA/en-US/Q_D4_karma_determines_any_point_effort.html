Q.D4. If karma determines everything, is there still any point in making an effort?

This question is the logical challenge of the whole group: if karma (Pali: kamma - intentional action) already decided everything — born rich or poor, meeting what, becoming what — what meaning remains in striving? Studying, doing good, repenting, keeping precepts, meditating... are they not all meaningless? The Buddhist answer is clear: yes, striving is needed — and moreover, right present striving is itself part of the causes creating the future. But the premise "karma decides everything" is wrong from the start — it is not how the Buddha presented karma. This article shows why.

1. The short answer

  • Striving is needed — certainly. The reason lies in karma's very definition: "Intention, I declare, is karma" — karma is made through present intention (Pali: cetanā - intention), not only past legacy. What we nourish today, that tendency strengthens tomorrow.
  • The premise "karma decides everything" is false: were it true, then studying, doing good, repentance, meditation, precepts, the Noble Eightfold Path — all meaningless; yet the very discourse on karma speaks of the ending of karma and the path to its ending (section 3).
  • Striving does not stand outside karmic law — striving is part of the karmic process: resolve to change is a mental activity, a new intention, creating new conditions.

2. The false assumption: karma is not fate

Many picture karma by the model: past deeds → karma → programmed future → unchangeable. Were that true, studying would be meaningless, doing good meaningless, repentance meaningless, meditation meaningless, precept-keeping meaningless, the Noble Eightfold Path meaningless — but that is not Buddhism's structure. Buddhism does not say "the past has decided everything, just let be"; closer to the discourses is: the past is one part of present conditions; present behavior and intention are in turn becoming conditions for the future. People are both inheritors of causality and continuing makers of causality.

3. The very discourse on karma speaks of "the ending of karma"

This is the strongest argument. The discourse defining karma ("Intention, I declare, is karma") does not stop there: it presents six points in full — karma; karma's origin; kinds of karma; karma's fruit; the ending of karma; and the path to karma's ending (precisely the Noble Eightfold Path). [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 6.63 (Nibbedhika Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism] Were karma absolute fate, the concept of "ending karma" would contradict itself — no one "ends" what is wholly pre-decided. The existence of a way out of karma is itself proof karma does not lock the future.

4. Fatalism ranked worst by the discourses

Early Buddhism directly confronted fatalism. In the Long Discourses, listing teachers of the time, the Buddha called Makkhali Gosāla's doctrine — holding all beings arranged by fate, with no cause, no condition, no one able to change — the worst of all. [Source: Dīgha Nikāya, DN 2 (Sāmaññaphala Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism] The deep reason for that criticism: fatalism denies the very capacity of deliberate action — i.e. denies the foundation of all practice and of karma itself. In other words, "karma decides everything, no need to strive" is not Buddhism speaking of karma, but the doctrine Buddhism opposes most strongly, merely wearing karma's clothes.

5. Striving is itself karma

This is the answer's deepest point. Because karma attaches to intention, understanding karma requires looking at: what the mind wants, the motive behind action, how intention turns into action. Two people giving money alike: person A thinks "I want to help this person suffer less"; person B thinks "I want everyone to praise me as good" — outward behavior alike, but karma (in Buddhist analysis) differs, for intention is the core.

Apply to striving itself: saying "I resolve to change" — that resolve is already a mental activity, a new intention. Striving not to speak hurtfully is one intention, different from "I will retaliate." Striving to keep precepts creates different conditions from "ignoring consequences." Striving does not stand outside karmic law — striving is part of the karmic process: it is the causal stream flowing in a new direction, right in this moment. Hence the question is not "need we strive" but "what are we striving for, with which motive."

6. An example: the hot-temper habit

Suppose you have a hot-temper habit. Part of its causes comes from the past: family, temperament, experience, environment — existing conditions, no need to deny. But today, in one concrete moment: recognizing anger → not answering at once → breathing → observing → speaking slower → not insulting. You have just created a new conditional chain. Next time: flaring but less explosively. After many times: the gap between "anger" and "action" lengthens. After years: temperament may change considerably. Were karma absolute fate, this process could not occur — yet it occurs daily in the lives of those truly practicing.

That chain is precisely dependent origination operating in the wholesome direction: the past makes part of the present, but the present also makes the future (the dependent-origination frame was presented in article Q.B4). Not escaping the past does not mean the past decides the whole future.

7. "I have already made karma, so what now?"

One may think: "I did many bad things, so the future is set." That is not how karma should be understood. As the previous article - Q.D3 presented by the discourses themselves: the past unerased, but how fruit ripens depends on the present person's "water" (salt-lump - river, AN 3.99); the Aṅgulimāla story shows one who once made enormously grave karma still completely transformed — the past is not the whole present person. A Buddhist's right response to one's own faults is not "karma made, just endure," but: "from now, which conditions can be transformed?" — stopping old behavior, repairing what can be repaired, building new habits, developing loving-kindness, keeping precepts, practicing mindfulness.

One point worth adding: the discourses encourage recalling five daily contemplations, including: I am the owner of my karma, heir to my karma, born of my karma, bound to my karma, supported by my karma — whatever I sow, wholesome or unwholesome, I myself inherit. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 5.57 (Upajjhatthana Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism] Saying "I am the owner of my karma" is not for despair — but to responsibilize: what I sow today, my own lifestream reaps; hence striving is how to cherish one's own lifestream.

8. Right effort — not self-coercion

A final misunderstanding needs removing: hearing "diligence," many think of forcing oneself very hard, tensely, self-tormenting. But right effort (Pali: sammā-vāyāma - right effort) is a branch of the Noble Eightfold Path, taught as balanced effort: nourishing unarisen wholesome states, increasing arisen wholesome states, preventing unarisen unwholesome states, calming arisen unwholesome states — four directions, done with adaptive mind, neither forcing nor slackening. The discourses use the lute-string image: too tight sounds jarring, too loose makes no music — only rightly tuned makes sound. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 6.55 (Soṇa Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism] Persistent yet adjusting striving — that is diligence in the Buddhist spirit (the "neither forcing nor slackening" meditation attitude is presented in article M.B3.2).

9. Summary and next steps

  • Striving is needed, certainly — for present striving is karma being made: intention, effort, training are all causes for the future.
  • False premise: karma is not fate; the very discourse on karma has a "karma's ending" section and a "path to karma's ending" (AN 6.63).
  • Fatalism ranked worst (DN 2) — for it denies the capacity of deliberate action, i.e. denies all practice's ground.
  • The past unerased but not locking the future: new conditional chains created today (the hot-temper example); "I am the owner of my karma" (AN 5.57) calls to responsibility, not a verdict.
  • Right effort is balanced — like a lute-string neither tight nor loose; striving is not self-coercion.

Glossary:

  • Karma (Pali: kamma): deliberate bodily - verbal - mental action; newly made each moment through intention.
  • Intention (Pali: cetanā): the core of karma — "Intention, I declare, is karma" (AN 6.63); also why striving (a new intention) is part of the karmic process.
  • Fatalism: most strongly criticized by Buddhism (DN 2); unlike karma in that karma is still being newly made.
  • Noble Eightfold Path: per AN 6.63, the "path to the ending of karma."
  • Right effort (Pali: sammā-vāyāma): nourishing wholesome states, preventing unwholesome states; done balanced, neither forcing nor slackening.
  • Dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda): the past is one part of present conditions; the present is creating future conditions.
  • Condition (Pali: paccaya).
  • Virtue - concentration - wisdom (Pali: sīla, samādhi, paññā).
  • Wholesome / unwholesome states (Pali: kusala / akusala dhamma): the objects of the fourfold diligence.
  • Right mindfulness (Pali: sammā-sati).
