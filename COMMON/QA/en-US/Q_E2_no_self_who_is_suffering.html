Q.E2. If the "self" does not exist, then who is suffering?

The previous article - Q.E1 ended with a difficult sentence: there is choosing behavior, but not necessarily a "self" behind choosing. The natural next question is: if the "self" does not exist, then when in pain, who is in pain? This is one of the hardest questions of the non-self (Pali: anattā - non-self) teaching. The answer closest to the discourses is neither "no one is in pain," nor "an invisible soul is in pain," but: suffering is arising within a conditioned body-and-mind process; yet no need to assume an unchanging "subject" behind owning that suffering.

1. The short answer

  • Suffering is real in experience: pain is real, sadness is real, loss is real, fear is real. Buddhism does not deny those — denial is the way we habitually add: "this is suffering of a fixed self."
  • A famous summary line of the Theravāda commentarial system captures this spirit: "There is suffering, but no sufferer; there is action, but no actor; there is cessation, but no one who ceases; there is a path, but no traveler on the path." [Source: Visuddhimagga - Theravāda Buddhism]
  • Hence "who is in pain?" needs exchanging for a more useful question: "which conditions are producing the experience of suffering, and how can suffering cease?" — answerable by observation, needing no metaphysical assumption.

2. Non-self does not mean "nothing exists"

This is the most common misunderstanding. Hearing "non-self," many think Buddhism teaches "people do not exist" — not so. You still hunger, thirst, ache, rejoice, grieve, speak, walk, work, remember loved ones, make karma, receive results. At the conventional level, saying "I ache," "I am sad" remains fully reasonable. The issue lies at the analytical level: seeking a self independent, unchanging, and fully mastering body-and-mind, Buddhism finds no such entity.

The canonical analysis: a person is composed of the five aggregates (Pali: pañcakkhandha - five aggregates) — form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness — and all five are impermanent, changing, unsuitable to grasp as "this is mine, this is me, this is my self." [Source: Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 22.59 (Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism] Note the discourse does not say "there is nothing" — it says nothing within the five aggregates qualifies to be grasped as a self. That difference is the whole issue.

3. What is really in pain?

Start from a simple example: you cut your finger. The experiential chain can be described: tissue damage → nerve signals → painful feeling → recognition → evaluating "pain" → discomfort. Here there is a real experience of pain — no one denies. But must an extra "pain-sufferer" entity be added outside that whole process? Per the five-aggregate analysis, experience is understood through: form (body and material processes), feeling (pleasant - unpleasant feeling), perception (recognition), formations (tendencies, volition), consciousness (knowing) — needing no separate "soul" behind making pain happen.

Put another way: suffering is real in experience, but the "owner of suffering" need not be a fixed entity. A close metaphor: the 8-o'clock flame and the 8:05 flame are not entirely the same matter — fire changes continuously with fuel, oxygen, temperature — yet no one says "there is no flame." There is a continuous process, not necessarily an unchanging object. "Me at age 10" is not entirely "me at 30" — yet there is causal continuity; no unchanging self is needed to explain experience's continuity (the causal-stream model was presented in article Q.B4).

And the rebuttal "without a self, why do I remember the past?" is answered the same way: Buddhism does not say "no continuity" — it says no unchanging entity grounding continuity. Memory, habits, language, body, relations, karma — countless conditions make continuity; state B is not entirely A, yet not separate from A (article Q.B2 on memory and karma).

4. Two levels of language: conventional and analytical

  • Conventional level: "I am in pain." — fully reasonable.
  • Analytical level: "A painful experience is arising based on body, feeling, recognition, and psychological conditions."

The two sentences need not contradict. The first is conventional language; the second is analytical language. Buddhism does not require us to stop using pronouns "I," "you," "they" — the Buddha himself used such speech daily to teach. What must be released is not pronouns, but the tacit belief: "there must be an unchanging 'I'-entity behind all experience." Grasping this separation, we avoid both errors: over-solemnizing non-self (talking without daring to use "I") and ignoring it (imagining non-self is mere ornament).

5. Two layers of suffering — when the self seizes pain

Consider a very real case: mother passes away. There may appear: memory images, emptiness, chest pain, weeping, thoughts "mother is gone," regret, longing for the departed to return. What we call "my pain" is in fact a whole network of experiences arising conditionally. Buddhism need not say "there is no one, so do not grieve" — but says: look directly at pain and see by which conditions it arises. The difference is huge: one side denies experience, the other sees experience clearly.

And within that network two layers can be separated:

  • Layer one — direct pain: one we love departs; sadness, missing, emptiness. This is pain, needing no denial.
  • Layer two — the self seizing pain: "Why was I abandoned?", "My life is over", "No one ever hurt like me", "I will never survive." Layer two can multiply suffering — turning "there is pain" into "I am a person defined by this pain."

Matching the canonical two-arrow image (already presented in article Q.D2): the first arrow is bodily pain - events; the second is mental suffering from reactions and clinging. Non-self is precisely the tool seeing the second arrow: initial pain may be only a feeling — but the self can turn it into an identity: "I am always unfortunate," "my life is unjust," "I cannot change." "Pain" differs from "I am the one in pain" — the second drags in a whole self-story the original experience need not carry.

6. How does non-self work in practice?

Non-self is no theory for debate — it can become a view instantly changing inner language:

  • Saying "sadness is present" instead of "I am a sad person."
  • Saying "fear is present" instead of "I am timid."
  • Saying "hatred is present" instead of "I am hot-tempered."

This small language change reflects a large perceptual change: occurring experience is not necessarily a self's nature. Sadness arises — will cease; unaccepted "as identity," it passes faster and adds no second suffering layer. This is why non-self is not an escape from pain but an escape from multiplying pain. And so "why did the Buddha teach suffering?" is answered: suffering does not depend on a permanent self — headaches still ache, the bereaved still grieve, however we analyze; non-self does not make painful feeling vanish at once, but changes how we understand "why pain?" and "why do I cling to this pain as part of myself?". Directly observing those states' arising-and-ceasing is Meditation practice: article group M.C2.3 presents observing arising-and-ceasing and the three characteristics — impermanence, suffering, non-self.

7. "No self" does not mean no responsibility

If non-self is understood as "no self, so whatever I do is fine" — that is misunderstanding. On the contrary, because actions are conditioned and fruited, each behavior still has consequences. At the conventional level, "I take responsibility for my actions" can fully be said; at the deep level, actions also arise from many conditions. The two do not exclude each other (article Q.E1 already presented: responsibility demands no absolute will, only behavior impactable by understanding and training).

Likewise, do not jump from "no unchanging self" to "no actor": there are body-and-mind processes, intentions, actions, consequences — lacking only an independent "controller." Precisely in that space, practice is meaningful: conditions can be retrained, and the person we call "me" can transform (article Q.B2 section 9: "we are continuously creating ourselves").

8. Summary and next steps

  • Suffering is real; the "owner of suffering" need not be a fixed entity — "there is suffering, but no sufferer" (Visuddhimagga).
  • Non-self does not mean "does not exist": the five aggregates still operate — only nothing in them qualifies to be grasped as self (SN 22.59).
  • Two levels of language: "I ache" (conventional) and "a painful experience is arising" (analytical) do not contradict.
  • Two layers of suffering: direct pain and suffering from the self seizing pain — the second turns feeling into identity; non-self is the tool seeing and loosening it.
  • Changing inner language ("sadness present" instead of "I am a sad person") is non-self's concrete practice step.
  • Responsibility still stands: actions, consequences, retrainability — lacking only an independent "controller."

Glossary:

  • Non-self (Pali: anattā): not meaning "does not exist"; meaning that within the five aggregates no independent, unchanging entity can be found to grasp as the "true self."
  • Five aggregates (Pali: pañcakkhandha): form (body), feeling, perception (recognition), formations (volition, mental habits), consciousness (knowing).
  • Self (Sanskrit: ātman): the concept the non-self teaching denies; three graspings criticized by the discourses: "this is mine, this is me, this is my self."
  • Conventional truth and Ultimate analysis: the two levels saying "I ache" and "a painful experience is arising" — both usable, each its own task.
  • Two arrows: the first is bodily pain - events; the second is mental suffering from reactions - clinging (SN 36.6; presented in article Q.D2).
  • Clinging (Pali: upādāna): the mechanism turning "there is pain" into "I am a person defined by pain."
  • Visuddhimagga (a great 5th-century Theravāda practice-compendium): source of the "there is suffering but no sufferer" summary; commentarial-treatise layer, not a discourse's verbatim.
  • Dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda): the frame explaining experience's continuity without a "ground-entity."
  • Karma (Pali: kamma): deliberate action with consequences; still operating within non-self.
