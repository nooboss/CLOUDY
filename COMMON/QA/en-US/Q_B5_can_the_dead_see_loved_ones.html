Q.B5. Can the dead see the loved ones they left behind?

The previous articles presented the Buddhist model of death: not a soul leaving the body and standing somewhere, but rebirth according to karma (previous article - Q.B4). Yet in life, many still carry a familiar image: a just-departed relative standing around the house, watching over children and grandchildren. So according to the discourses, can the dead see the living relatives? The most balanced answer is: possible, in some cases described by the discourses; but not every death is so.

1. The short answer

Depending on the realm of rebirth — not every death means seeing the living:

  • The Pāli Canon has discourses clearly describing one group of beings after death connected with the living: hungry ghosts (Pali: peta - hungry ghost) who know of relatives making offerings for them and benefit under suitable conditions.
  • But those same discourses state clearly: those reborn as humans, animals, in heaven, or in hell live by their own karma — they do not receive the living's offerings.
  • The discourses do not describe every dead person as a soul standing around the family, seeing everything day and night.

So the correct answer must be: it depends on the realm of rebirth — and what is most certain is that the living can still turn remembrance into wholesome deeds.

2. "Dead" is not the name of a realm

The popular picture is: the dead → a soul leaves the body → stands around the family → waits to be worshipped. This is not the Buddhist model. As article Q.B1 (section 6) presented, after death beings (all sentient beings with mind - Pali: satta - sentient being) are described as reborn according to karma into different realms: heaven, human, animal, hungry ghost, hell... Hence "the dead" refers to very many, very different cases — not one single shared state.

So the question "can the dead see the living?" needs to be exchanged for a more precise one: "after rebirth in which circumstance, to what extent can that being perceive the human world?" — and the answer differs case by case.

3. Do the discourses describe the departed knowing the living's affairs?

Yes — in specific cases. A famous short discourse of Theravāda Buddhism describes hungry ghosts gathering near the living — "standing outside walls, at crossroads, beside doors" — waiting for relatives to make offerings and direct merit to them; when the living give and dedicate, those ghosts are described as knowing it and benefiting. [Source: Tirokuḍḍa Sutta (Khuddakapāṭha 7; Petavatthu 1.5) - Theravāda Buddhism]

Another discourse answers this question even more precisely. The brahmin Jāṇussoṇi asked the Buddha: does giving for the departed benefit them? The Buddha answered: it depends on their realm. If the departed are reborn in the human, heavenly, animal, or hell realms — they live by their own meritorious karma and do not receive that share of giving. But if reborn in the hungry-ghost realm — a way of living dependent on others — then the living's good deeds and dedication can benefit them. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 10.177 (Jāṇussoṇi Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

These two discourses permit a careful statement: some beings after death can perceive what the living do concerning them — but this may not be widened into "every dead person sees everything their family does." A further reminder is needed: a hungry ghost is one realm of rebirth, not the default state of every dead person — a person may die and be reborn human, animal, heavenly, or as a hungry ghost; that is only one of the possibilities (article Q.H3 discusses more deeply: can offerings for the dead change their karma?).

In another direction, Vajrayāna Buddhism describes the intermediate state (Sanskrit: antarābhava - intermediate state) in detail: the newly dead are said to still see weeping relatives, to call out while the living cannot hear — a sad description very familiar to the feelings of those left behind. [Source: Bardo Thödol - Vajrayāna Buddhism] This is the presentation of one specific tradition; its details differ from the Theravāda model (rebirth immediately following death) — the differences among traditions were presented in article Q.B1 (section 7).

4. Where do the discourses end and speculation begin?

The discourses do not supply a detailed map of the kind: the dead hear every sentence, see through walls, follow the family day and night. Such details belong more to folk legend than to systematically presented teaching. The safest scriptural passage that can be stated is: some hungry ghosts can perceive actions of the living concerning them — no further details should be inferred from that.

Two related points need distinguishing:

  • Divine eye (Pali: dibbacakkhu - divine eye): the discourses describe specially trained practitioners — including the Buddha — with concentrated, purified mind able to see beings dying and being reborn in different realms, high and low, good and bad, according to their karma. [Source: Dīgha Nikāya, DN 2 (Sāmaññaphala Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism; similarly MN 12 (Mahāsīhanāda Sutta)] This is a special attainment of realized practitioners — showing Buddhism accepts the possibility of perceiving post-mortem realms under special spiritual conditions — but it is not presented as a "surveillance camera" on every dead person day and night.
  • One fully liberated (Pali: parinibbāna - final liberation): the discourses refuse to describe such a one after passing in terms of "exists or does not exist" (article Q.B1, section 8). Hence to say "the liberated one still stands watching descendants" drags the teaching back to a continuing self like a soul — which the discourses do not accept (article group Q.C discusses nirvana separately).

5. The feeling "loved ones are still beside us" — how to understand it rightly

Many feel a departed relative is still beside them. This feeling can have many layers, and each layer should be named correctly:

  • Psychological layer: memories and feelings about them continue in the living person's mind — a very real feeling, needing no metaphysical claim.
  • Cultural layer: many traditions — including Vietnamese culture — hold deep beliefs about ancestors and the departed; that is part of national spiritual life and deserves respect.
  • Scriptural layer: as section 3 presented, some cases (such as hungry ghosts described in the discourses) involve perceiving the living — the discourses allow this possibility, under specific conditions.
  • Speculative layer: "every dead person sees the family day and night" — the discourses do not affirm this.

The sentence "mother always lives in my heart" is a very beautiful expression of feeling — but it is symbol and memory, not a doctrinal statement about someone observing. Distinguishing these three layers helps us both honor feelings and avoid turning the unknown into certainty.

6. Morality needs no "dead watching"

Some families teach children with "grandparents are watching; do not do wrong." That may work temporarily, but Buddhism has a deeper moral foundation: one's own karma has consequences. Even when no one is observing: lying still has effects, hatred still shapes the mind, kindness still nourishes wholesome mind. Morality does not depend on whether some "dead person is watching" — it depends on the causal law of action itself (article G.C4). Living well from seeing clearly that the good brings peace to one's own and others' minds — that is a far firmer ground than living well from fear of being watched.

7. Turning remembrance into wholesome deeds

In the true spirit of the discourses in section 3, the most meaningful direction when remembering the departed is not worrying "do they see me," but turning remembrance into wholesome deeds: giving (Pali: dāna - generosity), offerings, helping those in difficulty, keeping precepts, meditating, developing loving-kindness — and dedicating (Pali: pattidāna - transferring of merit) those good deeds to the departed. This is the foundation of ancestral offerings, sūtra chanting, and deliverance practices in Vietnamese Buddhist life (article group Q.H discusses these themes separately; article G.C4 presents merit and dedication).

And a practical thought: if those loved ones truly love us, what they most wish is probably not that we live in fear or live procedurally worrying "they are sad," but that we live better because we remember them — less hateful, kinder, more cherishing of those still alive. The pain of losing loved ones, seen through the lens of impermanence (Pali: anicca - impermanence), can gradually turn into gratitude and compassion: they came, lived, touched our lives, then left — the way we continue loving them is the way we live from today.

8. Summary and next steps

  • "Dead" is not the name of a realm: after death, beings are reborn according to karma into different realms; the capacity to perceive the living depends on that realm.
  • The discourses do describe some clear cases: hungry ghosts (Tirokuḍḍa Sutta; AN 10.177) knowing and benefiting from relatives' good deeds for them — under suitable conditions.
  • No basis to affirm that every dead person sees the family day and night, or that one reborn human still stands around the old family.
  • The feeling "loved ones are still beside us" can be very real psychologically and culturally — but that is symbol and memory, not a metaphysical claim.
  • What is most certain: morality needs no observer; and remembrance of the departed can become wholesome deeds — giving, precept-keeping, practice, dedication.

Glossary:

  • Sentient being (Pali: satta).
  • Hungry ghost (Pali: peta): a realm of rebirth; per AN 10.177, this is the realm that can benefit from the living's giving and dedication, while other realms live by their own meritorious karma.
  • Wandering ghost: a Vietnamese folk term for the dead person's spirit, often implying an entity wandering around old places; a folk concept, not matching the discourses' model of rebirth by realm; discussed separately in article Q.B7.
  • Divine eye (Pali: dibbacakkhu): a special capacity of practitioners at deep concentration; the discourses describe it seeing beings die and be reborn according to karma.
  • Giving (Pali: dāna): one of the basic wholesome deeds; including material giving and giving of Dhamma.
  • Transferring of merit (Pali: pattidāna): wishing that the merit of one's good deeds be shared with others — here the departed; the theoretical foundation presented in article G.C4.
  • Final passing (Pali: parinibbāna): the complete ending of birth-and-death for a liberated one; also called "passing into extinction"; not described in the discourses as "exists or does not exist."
  • Impermanence (Pali: anicca).
  • Intermediate state (Sanskrit: antarābhava): a Vajrayāna description; Theravāda has no such interval — rebirth follows immediately.
  • Karma (Pali: kamma): in this article — the factor deciding each being's realm of rebirth.
