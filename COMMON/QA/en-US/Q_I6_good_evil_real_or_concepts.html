Q.I6. Do good and evil really exist, or are they only human concepts?

This is the question closing the Ethics-and-Doing-Good group with a foundational issue: do good and evil "exist" like objects out there, or only labels people assign one another? Seeming only two options: either good-evil are objective metaphysical entities, or they are wholly human inventions. Buddhism opens a subtler third direction: wholesome and unwholesome are concepts — yet they are anchored in real differences of mind, intention, action, and consequences. Names are conventional; what names point to has real consequences.

1. The short answer

Asking "does good exist like a physical object?" — no. One cannot weigh "three kilos of good," measure "two meters of evil," place "good" on a table. But asking "do mind-states called greed and mind-states called compassion truly differ?" — they differ, and those differences manifest as very different actions and consequences. One nourishing hatred creates a more hate-prone mind, tenser relations, more harmful behaviors; one nourishing compassion creates opposite fruits.

In the Canon, the root terms are wholesome (Pali: kusala - wholesome/skillful) and unwholesome (Pali: akusala - unwholesome/unskillful). They are evaluative concepts — yet tightly joined with mind-states, intentions, actions, and different causal directions toward suffering and liberation. Hence the answer can be summed in one formula: good-evil do not exist as metaphysical objects, yet neither are they arbitrary labels — they are concepts anchored in mind-and-action's causal structure. Two sentences to remember throughout: conventional ≠ arbitrary, and no self-nature ≠ no consequences.

2. "Really exists" has many meanings

Asking "is good real?", at least four meanings must be distinguished:

  • Real as a physical object? No — as said in section 1.
  • Real as a mind-state? Yes. Greed, hatred, compassion, ill will, remorse, generosity are experiential mental phenomena — the Meditation section presents directly observing them (article M.D1.3: "mind with greed, clearly knowing mind with greed").
  • Real as a causal structure? Yes. Nourishing hatred → easier angry reactions → causing conflicts → making suffering → further reinforcing hatred. This chain needs no "Evil-object" standing behind.
  • Real as an independently existing absolute value? This is a harder metaphysical question; Buddhism need not affirm a "Good-entity" or "Evil-entity" self-existing.

A familiar example: the word "poison" is a concept — yet poison harms even when no one knows the word "poison." Likewise, "unwholesome" is a concept — yet greed, hatred, delusion cause suffering whether people know the terms. Names do not make causality: calling hatred "passion" does not turn hatred into compassion; calling greed "aspiration" does not make it cease being greed; calling compassion "weakness" does not make it unwholesome.

3. Three unwholesome roots — where good-evil are "anchored"

The Canon clearly identifies the three unwholesome roots (Pali: akusala-mūla - three unwholesome roots): greed (Pali: lobha - greed), hatred (Pali: dosa - hatred), and delusion (Pali: moha - delusion). [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 3.69 (Akusalamūla Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism] And the three wholesome roots are the opposite: non-greed, non-hatred, non-delusion.

These two roots are real in the true sense: they correspond to different mind-operating ways. Greed — wanting to possess, fearing loss, never seeing enough, easily manipulating; non-greed — able to give, less dependent, less fearing loss, wider mind. Hatred — brewing ill will, wanting to harm; non-hatred — open mind, nourishing no enmity. Delusion — unseeing which actions lead to suffering, hence able to harm even while imagining seeking happiness; non-delusion — clearly seeing consequences, naturally reducing harm. This differs deeply from many models viewing "evil" only as bad will: evil is partly a cognition-issue — born from unseeing reality rightly.

The Canon further repeats a natural chain: wholesome mind leads to happiness, unwholesome mind leads to suffering — as shadow follows person. [Source: Dhammapada, Dhp 1–5 - Theravāda Buddhism] And intention — not outer results — is karma's core (article G.C4, AN 6.63 already presented).

This section's final important point: no permanent "evil nature" of a person exists. Buddhism says "this action is unwholesome" — needing no "this person has an Evil-nature." One who once acted cruelly may still repent, practice, transform, become compassionate. Were "evil" a fixed nature, that change could not occur. Hence say "unwholesome mind" instead of "evil person": the second easily creates prejudice and essentializing; the first helps see causes transformable — without removing responsibility: harm-doers still need stopping, accountability, education if possible.

4. The Kālāma Sutta: verifying for oneself

In the Kālāma Sutta, Kālāma villagers asked the Buddha: so many teachers, each praising their own doctrine blaming others' — whom to trust? The Buddha did not say "trust me." He guided them to verify themselves: do greed, hatred, delusion lead to benefit or harm, well-being or suffering? When self-seeing those states unwholesome, blameworthy, wisely blamed, leading to harm and suffering — then abandon them; when seeing non-greed, non-hatred, non-delusion wholesome, praiseworthy, leading to benefit and happiness — then enter and dwell in them. [Source: AN 3.65 (Kesamutti Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

This shows Buddhist morality is not simply a must-believe command-set, nor equates "majority-accepted" with "wholesome": a society may legalize or honor one act, yet that act may still cause suffering — law and morality do not coincide. The opposite extreme must also be avoided: the Kālāma Sutta does not say "whatever I like is good" — observers must still learn, examine, practice, and verify consequences. Good is not synonymous with personal pleasure; one wholesome act may displease others short-term (stopping a drunk driver may anger — yet that act protects them and others).

One final distinguishing pair: good is not necessarily "making others happy" (section 4), nor necessarily "blind sacrifice." Compassion needs walking with wisdom: compassion lacking wisdom may become harm; wisdom lacking compassion may become cold. Mahāyāna Buddhism calls these two virtues to be perfected together — wisdom + compassion (article G.E2).

5. "Good-evil made by society" — half-right

Half-right: expression-forms of moral standards bear social-cultural character — each community sets different laws, rules, customs. Half-wrong: consequences of mind and action are not made by society. Pain, fear, injury, hatred, clinging are not made by laws. If a community calls violent acts "glory," victims still ache, families still bear loss, harm-doers still reinforce hatred. Language is the interpreting layer; causality the operating layer — like the word "money" is conventional, yet money's social value has very real consequences.

So is morality "objective"? Depending on word-use. If objective means "existing as independent metaphysical objects" — not necessarily. If objective means "not arbitrarily changeable without changing real consequences" — yes: greed still creates greedy tendencies though we call it "aspiration"; names change, mind-structure unchanged. The compact formula: good/unwholesome concepts are conventional; the causal relations they point to are real in life. This understanding avoids all three poles: essentialism (good-evil eternal entities), extreme relativism (all assessments equal), and moral nihilism (nothing is good-evil).

Also distinguish concepts from phenomena: were one day no knowing subjects or languages left, the word "good" would not exist — yet if beings remained, actions could still cause suffering or bring benefit. Concepts need conceptualizers; harm/benefit structures do not necessarily need words.

6. Non-self does not erase morality

A large question: "Without self, who is good and who is bad?" The answer divides two levels: at the conventional level, there are wholesomely/unwholesomely acting people bearing responsibility; at the analytical level (seen per dependent origination), there are body — mind chains and causality, with no independent, unchanging self behind everything (article G.C5 on the five aggregates and non-self).

In other words: non-self does not erase morality — it only erases the idea of "an unchanging self owning all morality." Responsibility needs no eternal self: bearing responsibility without needing an eternal self — one of Buddhism's most beautiful philosophical points. One person may also be both wholesome and unwholesome in one life: kind to family yet greedy in business; truthful at work yet hateful in relations. "Good" is no uniform identity — it is concrete conditioned tendencies and actions, trainable (article Q.E2 deeply on non-self).

What about the awakened? At the conventional level, they still recognize wholesome and unwholesome — no killing, no lying, compassionate. But no longer clinging to "I am a wholesome person": wholesome action becomes natural, not building self around it. Non-clinging to good does not mean doing evil — it means doing good without building "I am better than others." This is the shift from morality to liberation: avoiding evil → doing good → purifying mind → transcending clinging even to "I am a wholesome person" images (article Q.C1 on the nirvana goal).

7. Seen from Mahāyāna Buddhism: emptiness is not nothingness

Mahāyāna Buddhism, especially the Madhyamaka (Sanskrit: Madhyamaka - Middle Way school) stream, presents this issue on a deeper level: all phenomena lack self-nature (Sanskrit: svabhāva - intrinsic nature) — including wholesome and unwholesome. But read very carefully: emptiness (Sanskrit: śūnyatā - emptiness) is not nihilism. Saying "good lacks self-nature" means good is no fixed independent substance — not meaning "there is no good" or "whatever one does is fine."

Some infer: "All is empty, so killing or saving are alike." This is a wrong inference — for emptiness does not erase dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda - dependent origination). If one act harms, consequences still exist; if one compassionate act, benefits still exist. Like a boat example: a boat has no independent "boat self-nature" — it depends on wood, shape, users, water — yet still carries people. Likewise, "good" has no independent self-nature yet still orients action. [Source: Madhyamaka tradition - Mahāyāna Buddhism] Article Q.J1 separately presents emptiness — article group Q.J1 → Q.J4 deepens related issues.

Thus, the multi-layered answer to the root question:

  • Physical level: good-evil are no objects.
  • Psychological level: real — as mind-states and experiences.
  • Social level: real — as rules, responsibilities, relations.
  • Karmic level: real — per Buddhism's causal model.
  • Ultimate level: no independent self-nature — yet efficacy within dependent origination remains intact.

8. Summary and next steps

  • Good-evil do not "exist" as two entities in the universe — no need to assume "Good-entities" or "Evil-entities."
  • Yet neither are they arbitrary labels: they are concepts anchored in real mind-states, intentions, actions, and consequences — conventional ≠ arbitrary.
  • Three unwholesome roots — greed, hatred, delusion (AN 3.69) — and three wholesome roots their opposite; evil is partly a cognition-issue, not only "bad heart."
  • The Kālāma Sutta (AN 3.65) returns morality to self-verifiable consequences — equating neither "majority-accepted" with "wholesome," nor turning good into "whatever I like."
  • Non-self does not erase morality: bearing responsibility without needing an eternal self; and "non-clinging to good" means doing good without building self — not doing evil.
  • Emptiness is not nothingness: no self-nature ≠ no consequences.

Glossary:

  • Wholesome / unwholesome (Pali: kusala / akusala): the Canon's two central classifications of mind and action; "skillful" meaning skillful toward reducing suffering and liberation, not technical efficiency.
  • Three unwholesome roots (Pali: akusala-mūla): greed (Pali: lobha), hatred (Pali: dosa), delusion (Pali: moha) — AN 3.69.
  • Non-greed, non-hatred, non-delusion (Pali: alobha, adosa, amoha): the three wholesome roots — the unwholesome three's opposite.
  • Ignorance (Pali: avijjā): delusion-root — since "evil" is partly a cognition-issue, morality tightly joins wisdom.
  • Intention (Pali: cetanā): karma's core — "Intention, I declare, is karma" (AN 6.63).
  • Karma (Pali: kamma): deliberate bodily - verbal - mental action with its natural results — the causal structure good/evil concepts anchor into.
  • Conventional (Pali: conventional truth/level): the level on which "persons," "good," "evil" are effectively used; opposed to dependent-origination analysis-level.
  • Non-self (Pali: anattā): erasing no responsibility and morality; only erasing the idea of an unchanging self owning them.
  • Dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda): everything happening thanks to conditional networks; explaining why different causes → different fruits.
  • Emptiness (Sanskrit: śūnyatā): the central Mahāyāna teaching; not nihilism — "no self-nature ≠ no consequences."
  • Madhyamaka (Sanskrit: Madhyamaka): the Mahāyāna thought-stream presenting emptiness; clearly distinguishing emptiness from nothingness.
  • Intrinsic nature (Sanskrit: svabhāva): what phenomena emptiness denies having; not denying phenomena's operation.
  • Loving-kindness and compassion (Pali: mettā and karuṇā): needing perfecting with wisdom — compassion lacking wisdom may harm, wisdom lacking compassion may grow cold.
  • Nirvana (Pali: nibbāna): the path's goal — good is the path to purification, not objects accumulating into a "good self."
