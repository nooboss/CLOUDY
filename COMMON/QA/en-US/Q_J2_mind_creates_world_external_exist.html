Q.J2. If the mind creates the world, does the external world really exist?

The previous article - Q.J1 clarified emptiness (Sanskrit: śūnyatā - emptiness) is not "nothing exists." The next question goes one level deeper: if Buddhism says mind plays a central role in building the world we experience — even traditions teaching "mind-only" — then does the external world really exist? The answer needs separating many layers, for "mind creates the world" carries many very different meanings, and conclusions may differ entirely depending on which meaning is used.

1. The short answer

  • If "mind creates the world" means "the world we directly live — views, feelings, interpretations — is co-created by mind" — then true, and this is the discourses' most important meaning.
  • If meaning "the physical universe is entirely my private imagination's product, only my mind exists" — then Buddhism does not teach so. That is solipsism, a conclusion unfitting Buddhism's liberation-for-all-beings goal.
  • "Is the external world real?" depends on "real's" meaning: real in the everyday sense — touchable, measurable, interactable, counter-acting; yet not real in the "self-standing, independent, unchanging" sense — exactly as the previous article - Q.J1 presented on intrinsic nature.

In short: the world we know is always the world passed through senses, feelings, cognition, and mind's interpretations; yet thence "the world is only my private dream" cannot be inferred. These two propositions are not synonymous.

2. "Mind creates the world" can mean many things

At least four understandings, from mild to strong:

  • Mind creates experience. A flower — colors arrive through light and eyes; yet "beautiful," "ugly," "lovely" are experiences mind helps build. In this sense, mind truly helps create "the world we live in."
  • Mind creates stories. The same sentence "How could you do that?" — one hears as praise, another as sarcasm. Sounds nearly identical, yet two different psychological worlds.
  • Community-mind creates the social world. A paper slip is only paper — yet people jointly assign and maintain meaning-systems making it "500,000 đồng." Money, status, honor all belong to this layer.
  • "Only mind exists" — no external world. This is the strongest position, close to metaphysical idealism. Great care is needed assigning this position to Buddhism (sections 6 – 8).

These four meanings greatly differ; using the fourth to criticize the whole "mind-creates-world" teaching, or using the first to affirm the fourth — both are confusions.

3. The discourses do not teach "the world is only mind" — the Bāhiya discourse

Taking the early Pāli Canon as ground, careful reading is needed. The Bāhiya discourse — one of the famous teachings on knowing — says: "In the seen, only the seen; in the heard, only the heard; in the sensed, only the sensed; in the known, only the known." [Source: Udāna, Ud 1.10 (Bāhiya Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

The discourse does not say "there is no external world." It points the knowing-way: do not add "I," "mine," and stories onto direct experience. Place two chains side by side:

  • sound → hearing → "he insults me" → "he always despises me" → "everyone opposes me" → hatred;
  • sound → being heard → stopping there.

The second chain does not deny sound is real; it removes interpretive layers making the "world" swell into the "self's" threat. This is a practice-instruction, not a physics-declaration that "no sound-waves exist outside mind."

4. "World" in the discourses — seen through six senses

In the Canon, the Buddha somewhere defines "world" not through astronomy, but through the six senses (eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, mind) with objects, consciousness, contact (Pali: phassa - contact) and feeling (Pali: vedanā - feeling): "where there is eye and forms, eye-consciousness, things knowable through eye-consciousness — there is called the world; that is the world's origin, the world's ending, the path ending the world." [Source: Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 12.44 (Loka Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

Reading this passage needs seeing the right focus: the discourses do not conclude "the material world does not exist." They point out that humans' "world" — where we live, want, fear, cling, and suffer — is the experiential field formed through six sense-doors and the dependent-origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda - dependent origination) chain. Because suffering is born at that experiential level, the liberation path also works at that level — needing no winning of a cosmological debate.

5. "The world I see" and "the world in itself" — two different questions

Looking at a tree, two very different questions:

  • Question A — "How am I experiencing the tree?" Greenness, shape, "tree" ideas, memories, feelings — this experience is built by mind and body.
  • Question B — "Does the tree exist independently of all cognition?" This is a metaphysical question, on another level.

From A's answer — "experience is mind-constructed" — B's answer — "the tree does not exist outside mind" — cannot automatically be inferred. The color example: trees reflect light-wavelengths; yet "beautiful green" is an experience. We know surely that the tree-as-experienced does not coincide with an experiencer-independently describable object — yet that decides not question B.

6. Three great answers in Buddhist history

When the question is pushed to the philosophical level, Buddhist schools have different approaches:

  • Some schools maintain outer objects' reality. Some early Buddhist experience-analyzing systems (with Abhidhamma (Pali: Abhidhamma - systematic doctrine) representative) and the Sautrāntika (Sanskrit: Sautrāntika - followers of the sūtras) school accept phenomena outside mind — though people know them through manifestations in cognition, not "directly naked."
  • Mahāyāna Buddhism developed the Yogācāra (Sanskrit: Yogācāra - mind-only school) system — placing very strong priority on cognition: everything we know appears as consciousness-forms (section 7).
  • The Madhyamaka system (Sanskrit: Madhyamaka - Middle Way school) rejects both poles: affirming neither "only matter" nor "only mind" — for both mind and objects lack intrinsic nature (Sanskrit: svabhāva - intrinsic nature).

These three answers are inter-school differences, not one common standard everyone must choose sides on (the multi-tradition learning spirit — article G.G2). More important commonality: no tradition takes "the world is illusory" as ground to erase morality and practice.

7. Does Yogācāra say "everything is only my mind"?

This is one of the most common misunderstandings of "Yogācāra." This system's central idea: all experience appears through consciousness — what we call the "outer world" always comes to us as mind-built and known images; no experience lies outside this. But thence inferring "everything is only my mind; I want, the world obeys" goes too far: solipsism is denied by that very tradition, for it unfits the liberation-for-other-beings goal.

The Yogācāra system — systematized from the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra and Asaṅga's and Vasubandhu's treatises — describes "mind" not as a personal self, but as a consciousness-stream with many layers, including storehouse consciousness (Sanskrit: ālaya-vijñāna) and seeds (Sanskrit: bīja - seeds) — the common ground explaining why many people can know "the same" world. [Source: Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra and Asaṅga's, Vasubandhu's treatises — Mahāyāna Buddhism]

It must also be stated clearly: Yogācāra is a later Mahāyāna philosophical development — it should not be assigned as the historical Buddha's verbatim teaching on matter's nature. For beginners, Yogācāra should be read at map-level to understand thought (article G.E2), detailed practice needing sources and guiding teachers (article M.E2.6).

8. Madhyamaka: why turn mind into an absolute entity?

Learning Yogācāra then thinking "so matter is unreal; only mind is real" — Madhyamaka will further ask: why turn mind into an ultimate entity? If trees are not self-standing, neither is mind self-standing: mind exists thanks to body, senses, objects, memories, language, and other mind-states — mind too is conditionally arisen. Nāgārjuna argued "seer," "seeing," and "seen" cannot self-establish as mutually separate independent entities. [Source: Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, chapter 9 — examining "seer" and "seeing" - Mahāyāna Buddhism]

So four options rank:

  • "Only matter is real" — no, that is one intrinsic-nature clinging.
  • "Only mind is real" — also no, also one intrinsic-nature clinging.
  • "Mind and matter are two independent entities" — no.
  • "Both mind and matter arise conditionally, without intrinsic nature" — close to the Madhyamaka view (article Q.J1).

And remember the previous article's lesson: "lacking intrinsic nature" ≠ "not existing." Madhyamaka denies independent-existing ways — not denying the operating world.

9. Two people looking at one tree — who is right?

This is a very strong rebuttal against solipsism. Were the world entirely made by my personal mind, why do you, I, and a third person see the same tree, measure the same height, photograph, see it grow together? Experience is intersubjective — for many people's mind-streams operate together in corresponding conditions, not each a closed private universe.

The rainbow image illustrates just enough: rainbows depend on light, droplets, viewer-position — with no independent "rainbow-object" lying somewhere; yet neither can "rainbows entirely do not exist" be said. They exist conditionally. Likewise: condition-dependent ≠ nonexistent, and mind-dependent ≠ merely personal imagination. If one says "this tree is beautiful" and another "this tree is ugly" — no contradiction, for "beautiful/ugly" depend on subjects; but "the tree is about 10 meters tall" can be jointly checked — for experience remains condition-limited; not all mind-contents are arbitrary.

10. Do not use this teaching to deny real suffering

A dangerous misuse: "everything from mind — you suffer because you think wrongly." The hungry, violence-suffering, ill, bereaved do not "create" those circumstances by thoughts; outer circumstances really act, and changing outer conditions is also part of wisdom.

Distinguish pain from added suffering: an accident makes bodily pain — the first arrow; mind may add "why always me?", "I am useless," "I will never recover" — the second arrow (the two-arrow image already presented in article Q.D2). Changing mind does not erase initial events; it may cut suffering added through interpretation, blame, and fear. So more precisely: mind does not necessarily create matter; mind surely creates how we live in matter — and creates most of the "suffering-world" we inhabit.

11. Where "mind creates the world" is clearest — and how to practice

The same room: the loving see "warm," the fearful see "something wrong," the sad see "empty." The room's physics unchanged — the experienced world changes. The same rain: "romantic," "annoying," "plan-breaking," "trees revived" — four psychological worlds from one rain.

Because the suffering-world is additionally made through reactions, mindfulness (Pali: sati - mindfulness) has real power: insulting words → recognizing → not adding stories → unpleasant feeling still exists, yet does not become "I am insulted" → suffering lessens. The gap between feeling and craving (Pali: taṇhā - craving) — exactly the link the Meditation section's article M.C2.2 trains observing, and clinging (Pali: upādāna - clinging) is the subsequent link (article M.D1.2 guides feeling-contemplation step by step).

And a memorable paradox: understanding "mind co-creates experiential world," we begin less blindly trusting mind — for mind can exaggerate, distort, misremember, label. "Feeling they hate me" may be very real — as a feeling; but "they hate me" is an interpretation. Real feeling ≠ surely-right explanatory story. Meditation does not deny the world; it asks: "how is the table being experienced?", "looking at them, what story is mind building?" — an observational approach, more than a metaphysical conclusion.

12. Summary and next steps

  • "Mind creates the world" has many meanings: creating experience, creating stories, creating the social world — true; "only my mind exists" — Buddhism does not teach.
  • The Bāhiya discourse points the direct-experience knowing-way — no physics-declaration; the discourses define "world" through six senses and the dependent-origination chain (SN 12.44).
  • Schools' three answers: outer objects exist (some Abhidhamma/Sautrāntika systems); cognition-priority (Yogācāra); neither mind nor objects intrinsically natured (Madhyamaka) — inter-school differences, no common standard.
  • Yogācāra is not solipsism: "mind" here is a multi-layered consciousness-stream, explaining intersubjectivity; a later Mahāyāna development, not root physics-teaching.
  • Madhyamaka asks back: were matter not self-standing, neither is mind self-standing — both conditionally arisen.
  • Do not use the teaching to deny real suffering — distinguishing pain (first arrow) from added suffering (second arrow).
  • "Mind creates the world" is clearest at suffering- and practice-levels: same circumstances, mind's receiving makes different "worlds" — and there mindfulness works.

Glossary:

  • Emptiness (Sanskrit: śūnyatā): all phenomena lack separate, self-standing natures; not synonymous with "nothing exists" (article Q.J1).
  • Intrinsic nature (Sanskrit: svabhāva): what emptiness denies.
  • Dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda): the principle all phenomena arise depending on conditions.
  • Six senses - six objects (Pali: saḷāyatana): the six "doors" the experiential world forms through.
  • Contact (Pali: phassa): the moment senses meet objects, feeling's starting source.
  • Feeling (Pali: vedanā): standing after contact, before craving in the dependent-origination chain.
  • Craving (Pali: taṇhā): the thirst making mind cling — suffering's root.
  • Clinging (Pali: upādāna): mind's grasping, holding-tight behavior after craving.
  • Mindfulness (Pali: sati).
  • Non-self (Pali: anattā): the ground presented in article G.C5 and articles Q.E2, Q.E3.
  • Abhidhamma (Pali: Abhidhamma): early Buddhism's experience-analyzing systems into "phenomena."
  • Sautrāntika (Sanskrit: Sautrāntika): an early Buddhist school; per ancient presentations, accepting outer objects, yet known through manifestations in cognition.
  • Yogācāra (Sanskrit: Yogācāra): the Mahāyāna philosophical school holding all experience appears through consciousness; not synonymous with solipsism.
  • Storehouse consciousness (Sanskrit: ālaya-vijñāna): the eighth consciousness in the Yogācāra system — the ground holding all experience-traces.
  • Seeds (Sanskrit: bīja): "seeds" in the consciousness-stream, whence later experiences arise.
  • Madhyamaka (Sanskrit: Madhyamaka): Bodhisattva Nāgārjuna's school, arguing both subject and objects lack intrinsic nature.
  • Solipsism: the conclusion both Buddhist traditions and the Yogācāra system reject.
  • Theravāda Buddhism: the Pāli-Canon-based tradition, popular in Sri Lanka, Thailand, Myanmar, Laos, Cambodia.
  • Mahāyāna Buddhism: one of Buddhism's three great traditions, popular in China, Vietnam, Korea, Japan.
