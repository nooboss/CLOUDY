Q.F3. Why didn't the Buddha answer questions about the existence of God?

The previous article - Q.F2 showed Buddhism takes no Creator as foundation. But a subtler question remains: when asked directly "is there a God?", the Buddha usually answered neither yes nor no — and that silence is sometimes misunderstood as evasion. In fact it is part of his very deliberate teaching method, clearly recorded in the discourses.

1. The short answer

The Buddha did not answer God-questions (and similar metaphysical questions) not from not knowing, but because such questions lie outside the teaching's purpose: they do not lead to suffering's ending. He chose answers helping questioners advance on the path — instead of philosophical propositions they cannot verify and need not for liberation. In the discourses, this attitude has its own name: undeclared (Pali: avyākata - undeclared). This is no evasive silence, but a form of answer.

2. Four questions met with "noble silence" — the canonical narrative

The discourses record the narrative of a monk named Māluṅkyāputta. He declared: unless the Buddha answered metaphysical questions, he would leave the order. Those questions were: is the world eternal or not eternal? is the world finite or infinite? are soul and body one or different? does the liberated one exist after death or not? The narrative ends with the famous image: a man shot with a poisoned arrow refusing extraction until knowing everything about shooter, bow, poison... He would die before knowing all. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 63 (Cūḷamāluṅkya Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

These four question-groups — with "is there a creator?" — belong to what the discourses call undeclared questions (Pali: avyākata - undeclared questions): declaring neither "right" nor "wrong" as questioners wish. Why? Because whichever direction answered, questioners still keep their "self" and "world" thinking, advancing not one step on the suffering-ending road — sometimes entangling deeper in theory.

3. Why no answer: the arrow lodged in the chest

The Buddha explained directly: his teaching teaches only suffering (Pali: dukkha - suffering), suffering's origin, suffering's ending, and the path to its ending — i.e. the Four Noble Truths (article G.C1). The "suffering" he wants to cure is suffering inside each person daily: greed, hatred, delusion, clinging, anxiety, birth-aging-illness-death. While creator-questions, universe-shape questions — do not help extract the arrow lodged in the chest.

Another equally important understanding: with "yes or no" questions, sometimes both answer-directions err at the root — for the very concepts building the question (a "world" separate from experience, a resident "self") already carry false assumptions. Answering "yes" reinforces clinging to a supreme entity; answering "no" reinforces absolute-nothingness view. Neither leads to releasing clinging. This is the deeper reason for the non-answering attitude (article G.C5 discusses non-self deeply — the ground of this view).

4. The extinguished flame — a question mismatching reality

Another very evocative narrative: the wanderer Vacchagotta asked the Buddha — "after death, does the Tathāgata exist, not exist, both exists and not, neither exists nor not?" All four options went unanswered. Instead, the Buddha asked back: a burning flame goes out — "the extinguished fire, did it go east, west, south, or north?" — a question mismatching the matter's nature. Fire goes out because fuel is exhausted, not because it "flew somewhere." Likewise, applying ordinary question-frames to the liberated state (and metaphysical issues of the same kind) is using the wrong tool. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 72 (Aggivacchagotta Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

The lesson: the undeclared attitude is not "hidden secrecy," but a declaration that the question itself — with its assumptions — does not stand. When someone poses a question on false assumptions, answering "yes" or "no" inadvertently confirms those false assumptions.

5. Ten unbeneficial questions and two kinds of questions

The Māluṅkyāputta narrative itself lists ten such questions — world eternal or not, finite or infinite, body and soul one or different, the liberated after death exists or not — all classed undeclared; while questions on suffering, origin, path are all taught clearly. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 63 (Cūḷamāluṅkya Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism] In other teachings, he distinguished plainly: which questions to answer categorically, which to analyze, which to counter-question, which to set aside. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 4.42 - Theravāda Buddhism] This distinction shows silence is a choice within the teaching system, not incapacity.

On God specifically: Buddhism takes no creator as foundation (previous article - Q.F2), yet with sincere inquirers the Buddha still answered in another direction — toward what questioners can verify right in their own experience: which actions lead to suffering, which to peace. This teaching manner is narrated many times in dialogues with other-religion followers.

6. Was the Buddha afraid of answering wrongly?

No. Were it merely fear of error, he could choose a cautious "maybe." But the undeclared attitude stems from something deeper: the teaching's purpose. A physician need not answer "who is the patient, their name, age" before treating wounds — not from not knowing, but because those answers are irrelevant to curing. The Buddha likened himself to a physician pointing the sickness-suffering cure. And one important consequence: liberation does not demand solving all metaphysical questions. Practitioners need not prove a creator exists or not to reduce hatred, lessen greed, live wakefully. The path starts from what is immediately practicable.

This point greatly liberates beginners: no one must "believe a metaphysical proposition" before starting practice. Buddhism invites verification through experience (article G.A3 on faith and wisdom — faith verified through experience).

7. So how should sincere questioners listen?

If readers carry "is there a God?" and truly need an answer to live by, the Buddhist spirit suggests reframing: instead of "is there an almighty being somewhere out there?", ask "what is making suffering in my life right now, and which conditions lessen suffering?". The second is answerable by observation, practice, and real change. As suffering lessens through practice, confidence in the teaching's view naturally grows — that is "verification," not "blind faith." 8. Summary and next steps

  • The Buddha left metaphysical questions undeclared (Pali: avyākata) — including creator-questions — for they do not lead to suffering's ending.
  • The Māluṅkyāputta narrative with the poisoned-arrow image (MN 63) illustrates priority order: cure suffering first, speculate later.
  • No answer also because the question itself carries false assumptions — answering "yes" or "no" both reinforce the root error.
  • The extinguished-flame image shows: applying ordinary question-frames to mismatching issues is the wrong tool.
  • Silence is a deliberate teaching choice — neither secrecy, nor evasion, nor incapacity.
  • Liberation demands no solving of all metaphysical questions; the path starts from verifiable practice.

Glossary:

  • Undeclared questions (Pali: avyākata): metaphysical questions the Buddha did not answer in "yes/no" form — for they do not help end suffering and carry false assumptions.
  • Bhikkhu (Pali: bhikkhu): a male Buddhist monastic; Māluṅkyāputta in the narrative.
  • Four Noble Truths (Pali: catu-sacca): suffering, suffering's origin, suffering's ending, the path ending suffering — Buddhism's central doctrinal frame (article G.C1).
  • Suffering (Pali: dukkha): not only pain, but the inherent unsatisfactoriness of all conditioned things.
  • Clinging (Pali: upādāna): the key drive keeping suffering continuing.
  • Middle Way (Pali: majjhimā paṭipadā): the way avoiding both extremes — neither falling into "absolute existence" nor "absolute nothingness."
  • Tathāgata (Pali: Tathāgata): how a Buddha refers to himself; in the flame narrative, post-mortem-existence questions about him are answered with the extinguished-fire image.
  • Direct experience: in the Buddhist spirit, teaching needs verifying through practice-experience, not merely believing words.
  • Realization: recognizing through experience, not believing — verifying teaching through practice — unlike "blind faith" resting only on words.
