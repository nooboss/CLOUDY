Q.G3. How does Buddhism explain people who claim to see their "past lives"?

The previous article - Q.G2 discussed those who say they remember past lives. This article goes one step deeper: those who see — seeing images, scenes, "themselves" in another life — how should they be understood from the Buddhist view? The short answer: Buddhism has a very clear concept of knowing previous lives, but does not equate every "past-life-seeing" image with that insight; and even genuine insight does not prove an unchanging soul.

1. The short answer — four possibilities to distinguish

When someone says "I saw my past life," Buddhism need not at once answer "right" or "wrong." At least four possibilities must be distinguished:

  • Genuine recollection of past lives (Pali: pubbenivāsānussati-ñāṇa - recollection of past lives) — a special insight described by the discourses, attained when mind is deeply concentrated;
  • Images appearing in meditation, dreams, or imagination — possibly very vivid yet not synonymous with genuine insight;
  • Memories or imaginings formed or suggested — especially noteworthy with regression hypnosis;
  • An experience with insufficient data to determine — not to be hastily turned into teaching or proof.

This is also why "seeing a past-life-like scene" differs from "knowing through insight that one once lived so."

2. "Seeing" differs from "knowing / recollecting"

When modern people say "I saw a past life," we usually picture an "inner screen" playing a film, with one recognizing "that is me in a past life." But the discourses do not describe past-life recollection simply as a film before the eyes. In the discourses, the Buddha describes remembering many lives with: names, clans, appearances, foods, pleasant-painful feelings, lifespans, death circumstances, and subsequent rebirth. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 4 (Bhayabherava Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism] This is a structured retrospective insight — not necessarily visual imagery.

Hence "seeing" and "knowing / recollecting" are not entirely one. A vivid image may be only an inner experience; an investigable claim needs concrete verifiable information — names, places, circumstances — not merely a visual impression.

3. Mind can create images by itself — a Meditation practice principle

Why doesn't Buddhism equate every "seeing" with genuine? Because mind can create images. Right within Buddhist meditation systems, practice may produce: images, lights, inner sounds, presence-feelings, memories, dreams, unusual consciousness-states. An image may be very clear "as real" — yet vividness does not by itself prove origin.

The principle meditation traditions stress: do not judge a meditation experience only by how impressive it is; meditation-born images need checking against the teaching and verifying through practice-results, instead of being tacitly taken as supernatural messages (handling meditation side-phenomena is presented in article M.A2 on "meditation signs" and article M.A3 on meditation safety). The discourses also show a very deep warning: some practitioners attaining concentration and remembering many past lives, yet wrongly concluding an "eternal self" — i.e. extraordinary experiences can still be wrongly interpreted. [Source: Dīgha Nikāya, DN 1 (Brahmajāla Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

Three cases can be pictured: (A) "While meditating, I saw myself in ancient clothes, living in a strange house" — that is an image-experience; (B) "I know names, places, family, occupation, and death circumstances, and this information is independently verified" — that is newly an investigable claim; (C) "I saw myself as a great king" but with no independent data — still only an inner experience.

4. What do the discourses say is "remembered"? SN 22.79

This is the Buddhist answer's deepest part. One discourse states plainly: those remembering many past lives are in fact remembering the five clung-to aggregates — or one of those aggregates. "In the past I had such form" — is remembering form (material body); "I had such feeling" — is remembering feeling; likewise with perception (recognition), formations (volition, reactions, mental habits), consciousness (knowing). [Source: Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 22.79 (Khajjaniya Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

The meaning is huge: even with genuine past-life remembering, Buddhism needs no "soul" as fixed subject to explain that memory. The discourse shifts speech from "I was..." to "there was such an aggregate-configuration" — fully fitting non-self (Pali: anattā - non-self) (article G.C5).

5. Who remembers? — the paradox resolved by dependent origination

"Without an unchanging soul, who remembers past lives?" — Buddhism answers not "there is a hidden soul behind," but explains by continuing causal stream: a previous life had aggregates combined with karma, craving, clinging; the later life causally continues from the prior stream, yet is no intact unchanging self transferred to a new body. Hence "That was precisely me in a past life" can be made more precise as: "That was a previous life in the same rebirth causal stream" — neither two entirely independent entities, nor one unchanging self (article Q.B4 analyzes with the field - seed - moisture image).

From this, two concepts are clearly distinguished: rebirth — causal continuity; and soul transmigration — an unchanging entity moving from body to body. Buddhism accepts the first and rejects the second.

6. Four questions to examine an experience

Instead of quick conclusions, four questions can be used:

  • Did the experience appear when mind was very settled? — The discourses join recollection insight with deep concentration; a flashing image in a restless state is not the same kind.
  • Is there concrete verifiable information? — Names, places, family, occupation, events; recorded before seeking confirmation.
  • Is there any ordinary information-source explaining it? — Books read, stories heard, films, others' suggestions.
  • What does that experience lead to? — Humility, reduced self-clinging, deeper understanding of rebirth? Or "I am special," "I was once a king," "everyone must listen to me"? The fourth question matters most for practice.

7. When special experiences nourish conceit

Imagine someone truly having a very accurate experience — then thinking: "I remember 3 past lives, so I am more special than others." Result: conceit (Pali: māna - conceit) grows. A capacity that could help see impermanence becomes food for the self. This is the familiar warning of meditation traditions: psychic capacities can become conceit's source if misused.

Conversely, a "wrong" experience may still make one live better: a dream of death in a previous life, though no true memory, may make one less afraid of death, cherish relatives, reflect on impermanence. But note: psychological meaning does not prove supernatural origin — the two faces must be kept apart. And for frightening or painful experiences, the healthy response is listening, noting, keeping normal routines, avoiding strong suggestive methods, and seeking professional support when needed (article M.A3).

8. Summary and next steps

  • Buddhism acknowledges recollection of past lives — insight remembering previous lives through deep concentration (MN 4, MN 19, MN 77) — but does not equate every "seeing" image with that insight.
  • Image vividness does not prove origin; meditation experiences need checking against the teaching, not judging by impressiveness.
  • SN 22.79: what is remembered are aggregates, not a soul — even past-life memory proves no unchanging self.
  • Rebirth = causal continuity (accepted); soul transmigration = unchanging entity moving (rejected).
  • Four experience-examining questions: mind-conditions, verifiable data, ordinary information-sources, and practice consequences.
  • Right experiences can nourish conceit; wrong experiences may still carry psychological meaning — two different matters.

Glossary:

  • Recollection of past lives (Pali: pubbenivāsānussati-ñāṇa): a transcendent insight attained when mind is deeply concentrated, described in the awakened and some practitioners.
  • Five clung-to aggregates (Pali: pañca upādānakkhandha): form (material body), feeling, perception (recognition), formations (volition, reactions, mental habits), consciousness (knowing).
  • Non-self (Pali: anattā): the ground of this article's "who remembers" explanation.
  • Dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda): the continuing causal stream between lives, needing no unchanging subject.
  • Rebirth — distinguished from soul transmigration: Buddhism accepts the former and rejects the latter.
  • Conceit (Pali: māna): one of the fetters; the typical danger when special experiences build identity.
  • Meditation sign (Pali: nimitta): images arising in concentration practice; this article uses that spirit for "past-life" images in meditation.
  • False memory: the main risk of hypnotic regression.
