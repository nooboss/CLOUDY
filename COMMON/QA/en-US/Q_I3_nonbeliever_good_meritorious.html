Q.I3. Can a person with no religious belief who lives a good life still be considered meritorious?

This question touches a sensitive boundary: is morality the "exclusive" property of religious believers? A person believing in no God, following no religion, even disbelieving karmic retribution — yet living truthfully, harming no one, knowing how to give, knowing how to forgive — is such a person "meritorious"? In the discourses' analysis of karma, the answer is possibly yes: no rule says merit-making obligatorily requires believing in a god. Yet both halves must be kept whole: a good-living non-believer is not synonymous with meritless; and being meritorious is not synonymous with already walking the liberation path.

1. The short answer

Place four people side by side:

  • The first person believes in no deities, yet lives kindly, gives, harms no one → many wholesome factors.
  • The second person believes in the Buddha, yet lives selfishly, lies, hates → belief does not turn bad behavior wholesome.
  • The third person is a Buddhist keeping precepts, compassionate, meditating, learning Dhamma → wholesome karma plus wisdom-nourishing conditions.
  • The fourth person is a non-believer yet deliberately harms, deceives → still makes unwholesome karma.

Four cases show at once: the "non-believer" or "religious" label insufficiently determines whether one is meritorious. What decides is intention (Pali: cetanā - intention) and bodily, verbal, mental action — not the religious name recorded in papers.

2. What does "merit" mean in Buddhism

"Merit" is the familiar translation of merit (Pali: puñña - merit). In the Buddhist context, merit is not "reward for being a good person" — but close to: wholesome behaviors, mental states, and wholesome cultivation capable of bringing wholesome fruits.

The Aṅguttara states three merit-making-activity grounds: giving (Pali: dāna - generosity), virtue (Pali: sīla - moral conduct), and mental cultivation (Pali: bhāvanā - mental cultivation). [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 8.36 (Puññakiriyāvatthu Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]

Looking at those three grounds shows: none first demands believing in a creator. Giving is giving-away behavior; virtue is a harmless lifestyle; cultivation is mind-training work. Merit in Buddhism is thus not tightly tied to praying, worshipping a god, or belonging to a religion in the social sense. (How Buddhism views God is a separate theme — articles Q.F2 and Q.F3.)

3. "Not believing" and "doing evil" are two different matters

Many unconsciously think: "Not believing in the Buddha means no morality." This is no necessary conclusion. One may disbelieve karmic retribution, rebirth, heavens, hells, deities — yet still act from empathy, love, fairness, responsibility: "I do not believe I will be rewarded after death. I help this person because I see them suffering." From karma-study's view, that helping volition still carries moral value. Buddhism needs no "supernatural spectator" standing beside to make wholesome action wholesome.

Also carefully distinguish wrong view (Pali: micchā-diṭṭhi - wrong view). In the Canon, wrong view is not simply "not believing this religion," but wrong views of causality and action — e.g. denying wholesome actions bear wholesome fruits, harming bears suffering fruits, or denying behavior-responsibility. "Non-belief" in the modern sense — not believing in a god — does not automatically equal wrong view. Conversely, right view (Pali: sammā-diṭṭhi - right view) — the Noble Eightfold Path's first branch — is clearly seeing wholesome-unwholesome causality and the suffering-ending path (article G.C2).

4. One unknowingly "accumulating merit" still makes merit

This is an interesting point. Suppose one says: "I do not care about karma concepts. I only want to live kindly." Then they help an elder across the road, save an animal, give money to the hungry, not retaliate against insulters, raise children lovingly. They do not know the word "puñña," do not think "I am accumulating merit." So is there merit?

Per the Canon's karma principle, the answer may be yes — for karma attaches to volition and action, not depending on actors knowing the term "karma" (article G.C4). [Source: AN 6.63 (Nibbedhika Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism] Names are not results' causes: you need not call a seed a "seed" for it to sprout.

This joins a famous short Dhammapada line: mind precedes all phenomena — wholesome mind leads to happiness as shadow follows person. [Source: Dhammapada, Dhp 1–2 - Theravāda Buddhism] Wherever wholesome volition and wholesome action exist, there is wholesome karma — whatever name actors call it.

5. Four people, four cases

Returning to section 1's four people, two dimensions need drawing.

First dimension: belief does not automatically make wholesome karma. The second person believes in the Buddha yet lives selfishly, lies, nourishes hatred — the "karma is real" belief does not turn hateful mind and conceit wholesome. Conversely, the first person believes in no deities yet their volition is helping, harmless — per the Canon, they are making wholesome karma without using Buddhist language to name it. Briefly: practice matters more than labels.

Second dimension: practice-paths are not thereby identical. The third person — Buddhists giving, keeping precepts, meditating, nourishing loving-kindness — is making wholesome karma plus factors the Canon calls the third merit-making ground: mental cultivation (AN 8.36). This difference is not for ranking people, but factual: whoever is unaware of cultivation concepts naturally does not actively train it. Both wrong generalizations must be avoided: "good-living non-believers are fully like Buddhists on the practice path" — untrue, lacking the mind-training piece; and "non-believers have no merit" — even more untrue, contradicting the volition principle.

One noteworthy paradox: one disbelieving moral causality may still live rightly per wholesome karma — always helping, avoiding harm, living sincerely; while one strongly believing karma may still despise the poor, do charity to show off. Hence "what do you believe?" is not the sole measure; the deeper question is "what is your mind creating?" (article Q.I1 already used this question with money matters).

6. Merit and liberation are two different levels

If the question is "can good-living non-believers make merit?" — possibly yes. But if the question is "is that enough to attain nirvana (Pali: nibbāna - liberation)?" — the Canon's answer is not yet. One may be generous, kind, moral — yet still strongly self-clinging, fearing loss, clinging to the "self," unseeing impermanence (Pali: anicca - impermanence), non-self (Pali: anattā - not-self), and dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda - dependent origination).

Three levels can be pictured:

  • Living evilly → unwholesome karma → suffering.
  • Living wholesomely → wholesome karma, merit → wholesome fruits.
  • Living wholesomely + insight → seeing impermanence, suffering, non-self → reducing greed — hatred — delusion → toward liberation.

A good-living non-believer may fully stand on the second level — and that is no small thing. The Nibbedhika Sutta (AN 6.63) speaks not only of karma-making, but of karma's ending and the path thereto. Merit is good, but liberation goes further than accumulating merit (article G.C7 on the liberation goal; article M.C2.3 on the three characteristics contemplated in insight meditation).

7. Buddhism still goes further than "being a good person"

Buddhism does not only want people to "be good." Buddhism further asks: why do people still suffer despite trying to live well? A kind person still ages, falls ill, loses loved ones, disappoints, anxieties, fears death, clings to self. Hence the teaching proceeds: from morality → wholesome karma → concentration → wisdom → destroying greed — hatred — delusion → liberation.

It must also be stated clearly: early Buddhism does not monopolize morality. The discourses do not say "only Buddha-believers can do good." Buddhism provides a subtler map for understanding why wholesome behaviors benefit — not necessarily creating all moral values from zero. And this entire content is presented in a non-sectarian spirit, not judging people by religious labels (article G.G2).

8. Summary and next steps

  • Non-belief is not synonymous with meritlessness. Karma rests on volition; giving, virtue, and mental cultivation are merit-making grounds — none demanding belief in a god.
  • "Not believing" is not automatically wrong view — wrong view is wrong views of causality and behavior-responsibility.
  • Names do not make results: one unknowing the term "merit" may still make merit; karma-believers may still make unwholesome.
  • Yet merit is not yet awakening: wholesome mind ≠ liberating wisdom; merit brings wholesome fruits, not automatically ending greed — hatred — delusion.
  • The pivotal question: not "do you believe in the Buddha?", but "what is your mind creating, and which direction are you living?".

Glossary:

  • Non-belief: not believing in a creator god; an existence-view, not a moral assessment; in this article — not automatically synonymous with immoral or meritless.
  • Merit (Pali: puñña): "fruits" of giving, precept-keeping, practice; wholesome deeds' natural causal direction, not rewards granted by anyone.
  • Intention (Pali: cetanā): karma's core — "Intention, I declare, is karma" (AN 6.63).
  • Three bases of meritorious action (Pali: puññakiriyāvatthu): giving (Pali: dāna), virtue (Pali: sīla), mental cultivation (Pali: bhāvanā) — AN 8.36.
  • Wrong view (Pali: micchā-diṭṭhi): wrong views of causality and behavior-responsibility — e.g. denying wholesome actions bear wholesome fruits; unlike modern-sense "non-belief."
  • Right view (Pali: sammā-diṭṭhi): clearly seeing wholesome-unwholesome causality, suffering, and the suffering-ending path — the Noble Eightfold Path's first branch.
  • Wholesome karma (Pali: kusala kamma): giving, helping people, living truthfully...; "wholesome" speaks of action-quality.
  • Karma (Pali: kamma): deliberate bodily - verbal - mental action with its natural results.
  • Nirvana (Pali: nibbāna): complete ending of suffering; ending greed — hatred — delusion and craving-born rebirth; unlike worldly blessed rewards.
  • Impermanence (Pali: anicca), non-self (Pali: anattā): two of the three characteristics of all phenomena; with suffering (Pali: dukkha) forming the three characteristics contemplated in insight meditation.
  • Dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda): everything happening thanks to converging conditional networks.
  • Insight: directly seeing impermanence — suffering — non-self in real experience; the Meditation section presents in detail.
