Q.D5. If there is cause and effect, who is the one judging a person?

This question touches a very natural intuition: if good deeds receive good fruits and evil deeds bad fruits, surely some subject must stand outside observing, recording, weighing, and deciding reward – punishment. But that intuition hides a very strong assumption: wherever there is verdict, there must be a judge. The Buddhist understanding of karma (Pali: kamma - intentional action) needs no such assumption. Cause and effect, in Buddhism, is closer to a law of cause – condition – fruit than to a court; and looking more closely still, even the "judged person" in the sense of an unchanging self does not exist as we imagine. This article works step by step through that, while distinguishing oft-mixed things: fruition from punishment, responsibility from a fixed "self," causality from fate, and causality from the idea of a judging god. 1. The short answer

  • No "judge" is necessarily needed. Causality operates as a cause – condition – fruit law: deliberate behavior creates conditions, and when conditions suffice, fruit appears — like seeds sprouting with enough soil, water, warmth, not because someone "judges" the seeds.
  • Fruit is not punishment. Punishment needs a punishing authority; while fruit (Pali: vipāka - ripening) is the naturally ripened result of a causal process.
  • The "judged person" also does not exist as an unchanging self. Buddhism teaches non-self (Pali: anattā - non-self); yet no unchanging self does not mean no continuity — there remains a continuing body – mind process-stream (article Q.B4 presented in detail).
  • No judge does not mean no responsibility. On the contrary: because no one repairs consequences for us, responsibility becomes more direct — precisely what we nourish and repeat daily is shaping our lifestream.

2. Distinguishing "cause and effect" from "judgment"

In ordinary language, "judgment" implies at least three components: an observing subject; that subject's right – wrong standard; and that subject's decision about another. A judge hears evidence, applies law, pronounces sentence — an external model: law in one place, adjudicator in another, adjudicated in yet another. Applying this model to karma, we easily picture a "Lord of Causality" seeing all deeds, recording everything, weighing good – evil, then issuing rewards or punishments. That is a very natural religious-intuitive picture, but not the only way to understand causality.

A simple example outside morality: a person touches fire and burns. Asking "who judged them?" — the answer is no one. The burn occurs because under suitable conditions, heat-contact injures. Here there is causality, but no judge. Likewise, seeds meeting soil, water, light sprout — needing no gardener "judging" seeds to sprout.

Hence "who judges?" may be a linguistically correct question, but wrong at the model level. The more exact question is: what within the very process of action, psychology, and conditions makes one behavior produce corresponding consequences? Shifting from court-model to conditions-and-consequences model is this whole article's key.

3. Karma is deliberate action — needing no recorder

The most important passage for this question lies in the karma-defining discourse: "Intention, I declare, is karma" — intention (Pali: cetanā - intention) is karma's core, not outer results. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 6.63 (Nibbedhika Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism] Article G.C4 has presented this theme in full.

This definition carries great significance. Karma is not synonymous with "everything happening": not "whatever happens to me is karma," nor "meeting bad things means I surely did bad in the past." The focus lies in volition — i.e. the orientation, motive, purpose behind action. Two people speaking one truth alike: one speaks to save an innocent, the other to humiliate — outward behavior alike, yet the psychological-moral structure behind differs, so karma differs.

Seeing this, "who scores karma?" changes shape: the answer is not "some god knowing your motives," but the very structure of deliberate action and corresponding mind – body conditions is karma-causality's center. The Dhammapada (Pali: Dhammapada) stresses: mind precedes all states; speech and action arise from mind; acting from unwholesome mind, suffering follows as the wheel follows the draft-animal's foot; acting from purified mind, happiness follows as a shadow never leaving form. [Source: Dhammapada, verses 1–2 - Theravāda Buddhism] No judge-figure in that image — only mind, behavior, habits, and successive consequences.

4. Fruit is not punishment

The word "karmic retribution" sometimes makes people think at once of "requital" as wrongdoing → punished. But "fruit" here is closer to ripening: fruit ripening from seed through a conditional chain, not a verdict pronounced by anyone. Place the two models side by side:

  • Court model: behavior → dossier → trial → verdict → punishment. Needs an authority with punishing power.
  • Conditional model: volition → action → condition-making → accumulation → ripened fruit. No subject necessarily required.

The difference lies here: one model needs a judge; the other does not. And a noteworthy detail: the very karma-defining discourse above does not stop there, but presents six points in full — karma; karma's origin; kinds of karma; karma's fruit; karma's ending; and the path to karma's ending (precisely the Noble Eightfold Path); the discourse also links karmic fruit with rebirth realms and different ripening times. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 6.63 - Theravāda Buddhism]

That point has deep implication: were karma merely "verdict," the concept of "ending karma" would contradict itself — no one ends a wholly pre-decided verdict. Buddhism's issue is not "serving punishment through," but understanding suffering-making mechanism and ending that mechanism — the suffering-ending path presented in article G.C2 (Noble Eightfold Path) and the Four Noble Truths ground in article G.C1.

5. Without a judge, who receives the fruit?

This is the philosophically hardest point. Saying "karma returns to its maker," the question at once appears: which "person"? — while Buddhism simultaneously teaches non-self: no unchanging personal entity, like a fixed "soul" standing behind all experience. Without a permanent self, who makes karma and who receives fruit?

Buddhism's answering direction: no unchanging self is needed for conditional continuity. Yesterday's child, the teenager ten years ago, and today's adult are not one fixed "object," yet there remains continuity of memory, habits, body, psychology, relations, and consequences. The 8-o'clock and 9-o'clock candle flames are not entirely "one unchanging object," yet not entirely unrelated. There is continuity, but no need for a permanent self. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 135 (Cūḷakammavibhaṅga Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism] This discourse presents the principle "beings are heirs of their karma, karma their inheritance" — the fruit-receiver is the causally continuing process-stream from action, not an unchanging soul being sentenced by a judge. "What passes from one life to another" was thoroughly discussed in article Q.B4; the whole death-and-rebirth theme in article group Q.B.

A concrete example. You get angry, then nourish anger daily: rethinking, retelling old stories, self-justifying, training mind accustomed to fury. After a while, you become easily irritable. Who "judges" you? No one needed. Precisely what you continuously nourish has made your mind's conditions. This is the deep meaning of "karma returns to its maker": not someone bringing it back, but because the action-maker helped make conditions for that very consequence-chain to keep developing.

6. Causality is not fate — and do not play "cosmic judge"

6.1. Causality is not fate

Some understand karma as: "I am poor from karma," "I am ill from karma," "that person suffers because they deserve so." This turns causality into a moral fatalism — precisely the doctrine the discourses criticize most strongly (previous article - Q.D4). Karma is one of the strong causal groups, but not the sole factor deciding all present events; health, social circumstances, nature, others' actions... all act together (article Q.D2 already presented). Hence one cannot look at a single event and conclude surely "this is this person's bad karma."

6.2. Do not use karma to condemn others

Ordinary people lack full insight into the whole conditional network, so conclusions like "he is poor surely bad karma," "she is ill surely did evil in a past life" exceed what we truly know. The paradox here is clear: one believing in causality yet using causality to condemn others is abandoning the very compassionate-humble spirit of the teaching. If we do not see the whole conditional network, we should not play "cosmic judge." "Why do good people still meet misfortune, bad people live in comfort?" is discussed separately in article Q.I4 (article group Q.I on ethics and doing good).

Personifying the law must also be avoided — saying "karma comes to make you pay," "the universe punishes," "heaven punishes." Such sayings may have educational or literary value, but in principle: a law is not a person. We say "gravity pulls objects down" but do not think gravity "gets angry" and pulls. Karma is a causal principle with moral-psychological character, not necessarily a governing god.

7. So what decides good and evil?

Without a law-giving god, why is one action "wholesome" and another "unwholesome"? In Buddhism, morality need not rest entirely on commands — it attaches to mind's structure and suffering. Behavior driven by greed – hatred – delusion (Pali: lobha, dosa, moha - greed, hatred, delusion) usually reinforces states leading to suffering; behavior from compassion, non-greed, non-hatred tends to reduce suffering and brighten mind. Hence "wholesome" means not only "will be rewarded," but the operating direction of mind and action reducing greed – hatred – delusion, reducing suffering, increasing wakefulness-capacity; "unwholesome" is not only "will be punished," but action driven by unwholesome roots, helping maintain suffering.

Legal justice must also be distinguished from causal reasonableness. Society needs laws, courts, evidence, procedures — to determine responsibility before community. But one may break law undetected, or be acquitted yet live in fear and torment; conversely, an unpraised, unrewarded good deed still transforms its doer toward good. "Karmic justice" is not a metaphysical version of criminal court.

And the Buddha — is he the judge? No. In Buddhism's basic understanding, the Buddha is one pointing out the path, not an arbitrary judge granting rewards or punishments: showing where fire burns, why it burns, how to extinguish, and how not to kindle again. One doing good only from hell-fear remains ruled by fear; one doing good from clearly seeing suffering's causes operates at a deeper level (the difference between doing good for merit and doing good from wisdom is discussed in article Q.I2).

8. What changes when the "judge" image is dropped?

Dropping "someone scores good – evil then grades at life's end" is not losing a refuge — but changing a whole way of living:

  • Less fear: no more psychology of dodging an invisible verdict.
  • More responsibility: no one repairs for us — what we sow today, our own lifestream reaps.
  • Less judging others: for we do not see their whole conditional network.
  • Observing motives more closely: karma attaches very deeply to volition, so the practice question is not "what do I do?" but "what does mind want?". Observing mind's reactions is a concrete Meditation object (article M.C2.2); observing feelings in daily life is presented in article M.G1.3.
  • Focusing on causes instead of only praying for fruits: instead of "how do I end suffering?", asking "what is making suffering?" — shifting from rescue-pleading to knowing and transforming.

An easy-to-remember formula of the karma-causal chain:

Volition → action → tendency → conditions → fruit → new reaction → new karma

In short: we do not only do deeds; deeds are also making the person we become. One small act repeated many times becomes habit, habit becomes tendency, tendency affects choices, choices make circumstances, circumstances in turn act back on mind. A close image is a river: flow depends on terrain, slope, obstacles, water volume — needing no "god of the stream" standing on banks deciding which way water must flow. That is a far better image than "some heaven scores good – evil then grades at life's end."

9. Summary and next steps

  • No judge necessarily exists: causality is a cause – condition – fruit law, not a cosmic court. Touching fire → burn, seeds → sprouts: causality exists, no judge.
  • Karma is deliberate action ("Intention, I declare, is karma" — AN 6.63); fruit is ripening in the conditional network; the very discourse on karma has a "karma's ending" section and a "path to its ending" — showing the goal is understanding and ending suffering, not serving punishment through.
  • No unchanging self needed for responsibility: the fruit-receiver is the causally continuing process-stream from action, not an unchanging soul sentenced (article Q.B4).
  • Causality is not fate: do not reduce all misfortune to past karma, and still less use karma to condemn others — we do not see the whole conditional network.
  • The Buddha points the way, not judges: wholesome is the direction reducing greed – hatred – delusion, reducing suffering; and more important than "who will punish me?" is "right now what am I sowing into mind, action, relationships?" — then causality shifts from a fear-making doctrine into a responsibility doctrine.

Glossary:

  • Karma (Pali: kamma): deliberate bodily - verbal - mental action together with its natural consequences; neither fate, nor verdict, and needing no "recorder."
  • Intention (Pali: cetanā): the core of karma — "Intention, I declare, is karma" (AN 6.63); with the same outer result, different motives mean different karma.
  • Karmic fruit (Pali: vipāka): the ripened result of karma in the conditional network — like ripe fruit from seed, unlike "punishment" pronounced by a subject.
  • Dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda): everything happening thanks to a conditional network; helping understand why causality needs no "operator" behind.
  • Condition (Pali: paccaya).
  • Non-self (Pali: anattā): denying a permanent, independent personal entity; but not denying the conditional continuity of the mind - body process.
  • Fixed self: what Buddhism regards as illusion — unlike the still-real conditional-continuity stream.
  • Greed - hatred - delusion (Pali: lobha, dosa, moha): the three main sources of unwholesome behavior and suffering.
  • Noble Eightfold Path: per AN 6.63 the "path to the ending of karma."
  • Fatalism: unlike causality in that causality is still newly made through present intention; fatalism most strongly criticized by the discourses.
  • Loving-kindness (Pali: mettā) and Compassion (Pali: karuṇā): two of the four immeasurables (four mental qualities cultivated as meditation objects); article M.D2.1 presents loving-kindness, article M.D2.4 equanimity.
  • Equanimity (Pali: upekkhā): the mental quality helping look at people and things without judgment — what this article shows is much needed speaking of causality.
  • Mindfulness (Pali: sati): the practice tool for clearly seeing mind's motives and reactions — where karma begins.
  • Direct knowledge and vision (Pali: ñāṇa-dassana): in this article — the condition for concluding; ordinary people lack insight into the whole conditional network, so should not play judge.
