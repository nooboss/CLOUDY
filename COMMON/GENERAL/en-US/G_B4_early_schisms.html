G.B4. Early Schisms

Following the disagreement at Vesālī described at the end of the previous article, the Buddhist community entered a period of division: over 2–3 centuries, a single community developed into about 18–20 early schools — each preserving a closely related body of scriptures, its own Vinaya, and different interpretations of doctrine. This article answers three questions: what happened (the first schism), what the map of the schools looked like, and how Buddhism moved beyond India through the ruler most famous as a royal patron of the Dharma. This provides the background for understanding how the three major traditions — the subject of the next article — emerged.

1. The first schism — the Sthavira and Mahāsāṃghika

1.1. From the ten points dispute to two camps

The Second Council at Vesālī (about 100 years after the Buddha's final passing, as described in the previous article) rejected the ten points practiced by the Vesālī group of monks — keeping personal money, eating after noon, and several other disciplinary matters. But the council's decision did not end the disagreement: the rejected group did not simply rejoin the majority, and according to several sources they held their own recitation of scriptures and discipline. The next major development — according to the most common traditional account — occurred about a century later and was connected with a harder question: an initial disagreement over discipline expanded into disagreements over doctrine.

The dispute covered several subjects, but a prominent set involved five questions about the nature of an arahant (Pali: arahant) — could such a liberated person still have minor faults, receive information from others, experience doubt, need a teacher to point out the way, or was liberation already complete? The community debated these issues from multiple angles; when consensus could not be reached, it divided into two camps:

  • Sthavira (Sanskrit: Sthavira) — the more conservative camp: maintaining a stricter textual Vinaya and treating new discourses and new formulations of doctrine with greater caution.
  • Mahāsāṃghika (Sanskrit: Mahāsāṃghika) — the more open camp: relaxing some Vinaya rules (including some points connected with the Vesālī dispute) and developing new ways of expressing doctrine.

1.2. How to read the history today

The story of a "single split into two" above is the way Buddhist traditions have narrated the process; modern research suggests that schism was a gradual process lasting for centuries, with many smaller rounds of disagreement: groups separated geographically, differed over discipline, and developed different formulations of doctrine — rather than dividing in one single event. Factors identified include the community spreading across different kingdoms (with different Vinaya traditions), the difficulty of convening the whole community as centers became distant, and vigorous philosophical exchange that produced new interpretations. Therefore, when older books say "Buddhism split in two in 300 BCE," it is better understood as a simplified account of a much more complex process.

The most important point to remember about this period is that the early schools did not simply call one another "heretics" or "outsiders" — they shared devotion to the Buddha, preserved the Three Baskets (Pali: tipiṭaka) in their respective versions, and took refuge in the Three Jewels (Pali: ratanattaya); they differed mainly in textual versions of the Vinaya and in doctrinal formulation. This is why modern scholarship uses terms such as Nikāya Buddhism for this stage — a period in which no early school survives today as a fully intact independent lineage, yet later Buddhist traditions grew partly out of these developments.

2. A map of the early schools

2.1. The two early camps and the emergence of 18–20 schools

From the two initial camps, the schools continued to divide over the next 2–3 centuries. Buddhist sources — especially Chinese and Tibetan translations — list around 18–20 schools; the exact lists differ across sources, like maps drawn at different times. There is no need to memorize them all; what matters is recognizing several major groups and which ones left lines of continuity visible today:

  • Sarvāstivāda (Sanskrit: Sarvāstivāda) — a major school in northern India and Central Asia; it developed a very extensive Abhidharma system of its own, and Sanskrit scriptures from the school were later translated into Chinese.
  • Vibhajyavāda (Sanskrit: Vibhajyavāda) — a tradition associated with central India and Sri Lanka; according to Theravāda sources, it was the ancestor of Theravāda Buddhism today.
  • Dharmaguptaka (Sanskrit: Dharmaguptaka) — a northwestern school; its Vinaya, transmitted through Chinese translation, became the Vinaya used today by monastics in East Asia, including Vietnam.
  • Mahāsāṃghika and its branches — developed strongly in eastern India; some ideas from these branches contributed to later Mahāyāna Buddhism.
  • Other schools — Mahīśāsaka, Kāśyapīya, Dharmottarīya, and many smaller groups — are known mainly through lists preserved in Chinese and Tibetan sources.

2.2. Which schools survive today?

The short answer is: the Vibhajyavāda line in Sri Lanka is the one said to continue into Theravāda Buddhism today; the other schools no longer survive as independent monastic communities, but they left important legacies: the Dharmaguptaka Vinaya lives on in East Asian monastic life; Sarvāstivāda Sanskrit scriptures survive in the Chinese Buddhist canon; and some Mahāsāṃghika ideas continued through later Mahāyāna developments. In other words, the age of the early schools did not simply "die" — its elements were dispersed into later traditions.

Understanding this map helps beginners reading the history of later traditions (groups D, E, and F) avoid confusion: for example, why the Pali Canon contains an Abhidhamma different from the Chinese Buddhist canon; why Vietnamese monastics follow a Vinaya different from monastics in Sri Lanka; and why some concepts "not found in the Pali Canon" appear in other traditions — different lineages preserved different textual collections.

3. King Aśoka — Buddhism spreads beyond India

3.1. From conquering king to Buddhist patron

Around 268–232 BCE, a ruler of the Magadha empire — King Aśoka (Pali: Asoka, Sanskrit: Aśoka, meaning "without sorrow") — profoundly changed Buddhism's historical trajectory. Tradition says that after the bloody conquest of Kaliṅga, witnessing the resulting deaths and suffering, the king underwent a complete transformation: he took refuge in Buddhism, renounced warfare as a means of conquest, and governed according to Dharma (Sanskrit: dharma) — justice, compassion, and respect for other religions. This story has direct archaeological evidence: the stone pillars and rock edicts that Aśoka himself commissioned and inscribed — an unusually rich source for the ancient world. In one edict, the king explicitly reflects on the aftermath of the Kaliṅga war and his remorse.

3.2. Pillars, edicts, and the missionary network

Across his empire, Aśoka erected stone pillars bearing edicts (royal edicts) that instructed people in ethical conduct: avoiding killing, respecting parents, treating servants humanely, and respecting all religions. The edicts were written in languages understood by ordinary people rather than in learned Sanskrit, making them among the earliest substantial records of political and ethical policy in the ancient world. The edicts also record the king granting caves to other religious ascetics (the Ājīvikas), donating caves or support to Brahmins, and encouraging religious groups to practice restraint — a notable example of religious coexistence in antiquity.

As for Buddhism, Buddhist sources — rather than Aśoka's own edicts — say that he organized a missionary network sending delegations to surrounding regions and kingdoms: southward to Sri Lanka, traditionally led by the elder Mahinda and the king's daughter Saṅghamittā, and to regions in northwestern, southern, and eastern India. The mission to Sri Lanka was especially important: there, in the 1st century BCE, the Pali Canon was written down (as described in the previous article), and from Sri Lanka Buddhism later spread into Southeast Asia.

It is important to distinguish the two kinds of sources: Aśoka's edicts confirm that the king adopted and promoted Dharma, visited sacred sites (including Lumbinī — the Buddha's birthplace, article G.B2), and sent delegations within and beyond his empire; Buddhist sources add details about the names of the leaders and the destinations. The two bodies of evidence do not necessarily conflict — they complement one another and together suggest a broader picture: with Aśoka's patronage, Buddhism developed from a regional movement into a transregional religion.

3.3. The significance of the Aśokan period

Three things are worth remembering. First, Buddhism spread in part through royal patronage — but not by military conquest: Aśoka's edicts promote ethical and religious practice rather than territorial occupation. Second, the movement of monastic and scriptural lineages into different regions helped lay the groundwork for their later separate development. Third, the image of the Dharma-protecting king became an influential ideal for Buddhist rulers for centuries afterward — from Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia to East Asia.

4. Summary and next steps

  • After the Second Council, disagreement over the ten disciplinary points expanded into disagreements over doctrine; the community became associated with the Sthavira and Mahāsāṃghika camps.
  • Modern research treats schism as a gradual process lasting many centuries rather than a single dividing event.
  • From the two early camps, further schools emerged, producing 18–20 early schools; among the most important were Sarvāstivāda, Vibhajyavāda, Dharmaguptaka, and Mahāsāṃghika.
  • The Vibhajyavāda line in Sri Lanka is traditionally presented as continuing into Theravāda Buddhism today; the other schools left dispersed legacies — the Dharmaguptaka Vinaya in East Asian monasticism, Sarvāstivāda Sanskrit scriptures in the Chinese canon, and Mahāsāṃghika ideas in later Mahāyāna developments.
  • King Aśoka — after the Kaliṅga war — adopted Buddhism, commissioned pillars and edicts, and supported missionary delegations, including Mahinda's mission to Sri Lanka, helping Buddhism become a transregional religion.

Glossary:

  • Sthavira (Sanskrit: Sthavira): the elder-oriented camp associated with stricter Vinaya interpretation; in the traditional lineage narrative, an ancestor of the Theravāda tradition.
  • Mahāsāṃghika (Sanskrit: Mahāsāṃghika): the broader camp associated with more open Vinaya interpretation and new doctrinal formulations; some of its branches contributed to later Mahāyāna ideas.
  • Sarvāstivāda (Sanskrit: Sarvāstivāda): a major school of northern India and Central Asia, known for its extensive Sanskrit Abhidharma system.
  • Vibhajyavāda (Sanskrit: Vibhajyavāda): a school associated with analytical approaches; according to Theravāda sources, an ancestor of the Theravāda line today.
  • Dharmaguptaka (Sanskrit: Dharmaguptaka): an early school whose Vinaya, transmitted through Chinese translation, is the Vinaya used by East Asian monastics, including in Vietnam.
  • Arahant (Pali: arahant): a person who has eliminated the mental defilements; a liberated ideal in early Buddhist scriptures.
  • Abhidhamma / Abhidharma (Pali: abhidhamma, Sanskrit: abhidharma): analytical Buddhist teachings concerning mind, phenomena, and causal relations; different early schools developed their own systems.
  • Nikāya Buddhism: a modern scholarly term for the period of early "school-based" Buddhism.
  • King Aśoka (Pali: Asoka, Sanskrit: Aśoka): the Magadha ruler (c. 268–232 BCE) who adopted Buddhism after the Kaliṅga war and supported Buddhist missions and ethical governance.
  • Rock and pillar edicts: Aśoka's inscriptions in languages used by ordinary people — an unusually direct source of evidence from the ancient world.
  • Kaliṅga war: Aśoka's campaign against Kaliṅga, described by the king himself in an edict as a turning point associated with remorse.
  • Mahinda and Saṅghamittā (Pali: Mahinda, Saṅghamittā): the two figures traditionally said to have led the Buddhist mission to Sri Lanka; Saṅghamittā is also said to have brought the bhikkhunī lineage to the island.
