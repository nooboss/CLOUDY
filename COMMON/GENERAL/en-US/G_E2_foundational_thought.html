G.E2. Foundational Thought

This article maps central Mahāyāna ideas. They are best read as connected perspectives on the path rather than as a list to memorise: the bodhisattva path begins with bodhicitta, is practised through the perfections, and is illuminated by emptiness, Buddha-nature, the three bodies, and analyses of mind.

1. The bodhisattva and bodhicitta

A bodhisattva is one who seeks complete awakening for the benefit of all sentient beings. Bodhicitta is that awakening-oriented intention: it joins wisdom with compassion, so that practice is neither merely self-concerned nor sentimental goodwill without insight. Mahāyāna sources distinguish aspirational bodhicitta—the resolve to awaken—and engaged bodhicitta—the resolve expressed in a disciplined path.

2. The six perfections

The six perfections (pāramitās) are generosity, ethical conduct, patience, diligence, meditation, and wisdom. They are not six isolated virtues: generosity without wisdom can reinforce attachment, while wisdom without compassion can become remote. The sixth, wisdom, understands emptiness; the other five put that insight into compassionate activity.

3. Emptiness — an introduction

Emptiness (śūnyatā) does not mean that nothing exists. It means that persons and phenomena do not possess an independent, permanent, self-existing essence. They arise through causes, conditions, naming, and mutual relation. In Madhyamaka, emptiness is another way of seeing dependent arising (G.C3), avoiding both eternalism and nihilism.

4. Buddha-nature

Buddha-nature (tathāgatagarbha) is language used by many Mahāyāna texts for the capacity of all beings to awaken. It should not be casually treated as an eternal personal soul: different texts and schools interpret it differently. Its practical force is inclusive—no being is finally excluded from the path to buddhahood.

5. The three bodies of the Buddha

The three bodies (trikāya) are commonly explained as the dharma body, the truth or awakened reality; the enjoyment body, a sublime form accessible to advanced bodhisattvas; and the emanation body, such as the historical Śākyamuni Buddha. This framework explains the Mahāyāna language of many buddhas without reducing the historical Buddha's life to insignificance.

6. Yogācāra — an introduction

Yogācāra, often called “Consciousness-Only,” analyses how experience is constructed through consciousness. Its eight-consciousness model includes the storehouse consciousness (ālaya-vijñāna), a way of explaining continuity, habits, and karmic seeds. It does not simply claim that the external world is imaginary; its purpose is to show how grasping and mistaken constructions are transformed into wisdom.

7. Major Mahāyāna buddhas and bodhisattvas

Amitābha is associated with the Western Pure Land; Bhaiṣajyaguru with healing; and Maitreya with the future Buddha. Avalokiteśvara represents compassion, Mañjuśrī wisdom, Samantabhadra great vows and practice, and Kṣitigarbha concern for beings in difficult realms. They are not merely figures to request favours from: in Mahāyāna practice, they also make qualities of awakening concrete and memorable.

8. Summary and next steps

  • The bodhisattva path begins with bodhicitta and is developed through the six perfections.
  • Emptiness means lack of independent self-nature, not nothingness; Buddha-nature expresses universal capacity for awakening.
  • Trikāya and Yogācāra offer complementary ways of understanding awakened presence and experience.
  • Buddhas and bodhisattvas embody qualities to cultivate.

Glossary:

  • Bodhisattva: one dedicated to awakening for all beings.
  • Bodhicitta: the resolve for awakening for self and others.
  • Pāramitā: a perfection cultivated on the bodhisattva path.
  • Emptiness (śūnyatā): lack of independent self-nature.
  • Buddha-nature (tathāgatagarbha): the capacity for buddhahood.
  • Trikāya: the three bodies of a buddha.
  • Yogācāra: a Mahāyāna school analysing experience and consciousness.

Source notes

  • These ideas are presented across Perfection of Wisdom, Buddha-nature, and Yogācāra texts, as well as the major East Asian traditions that interpret them.
  • Terms such as emptiness and Buddha-nature require their doctrinal context and should not be reduced to everyday English equivalents.
