G.C6. The Three Trainings and the Way of Life

The previous article showed the three characteristics of everything that arises dependently — and the remaining question is: through what steps does that clear seeing develop? The canonical answer is simple enough for a beginner to remember in one sitting: the Buddhist path is a three-step training program — the three trainings (Pali: tisso sikkhā, Sanskrit: trīṇi śikṣāṇi): ethical conduct, concentration, and wisdom. Alongside this training program, this article introduces four qualities of mind — loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity — and how this "way of life" supports all three trainings day by day.

1. The three trainings — three steps on the path

The three trainings are not a new teaching — they are a way of reorganizing teachings already encountered into a sequential training program. The three trainings are:

  • Ethical conduct (Pali: sīla, Sanskrit: śīla) — living carefully in speech and action — the content of the ethics group within the Noble Eightfold Path (Pali: aṭṭhaṅgika magga) described in the previous article: right speech, right action, and right livelihood; for lay practitioners, this includes the five precepts.
  • Concentration (Pali: samādhi) — practices for training and stabilizing the mind — right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration; detailed practice guidance belongs to the Meditation section of the app.
  • Wisdom (Pali: paññā, Sanskrit: prajñā) — seeing reality clearly — right view and right intention; seeing the Four Noble Truths (article G.C1) and the three characteristics (previous article) through direct experience, not merely through books.

All three trainings aim at one destination: liberation — the complete ending of suffering, whose destination is named Nibbāna (Pali: nibbāna, Sanskrit: nirvāṇa — this will be the subject of the next article). Three trainings, one "degree" — and that degree is not something hung on a wall: it is the mind of the practitioner itself.

2. The sequence: ethical conduct supports concentration, concentration supports wisdom

What makes the three trainings more than a list of three words is their dependent sequence. The texts summarize it in one sentence: "Ethical conduct for concentration, concentration for wisdom, wisdom for liberation." The sequence then unfolds naturally: one who keeps the precepts has no remorse; without remorse there is gladness; from gladness comes joy; with joy comes tranquillity; with tranquillity comes happiness; a happy mind is easily concentrated; concentration enables one to see things as they really are; seeing things as they really are leads to disenchantment with what should be abandoned; disenchantment leads to dispassion; dispassion leads to liberation. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 11.1 (Kimatthiyā Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism].

Read backward, the image becomes very clear — like a house:

  • Ethical conduct is the foundation: a life filled with harm, dishonesty, and recurring remorse keeps the mind like disturbed water — concentration is difficult to establish. Ethical conduct is not a list of prohibitions designed to create guilt; it is the work of clearing the ground so the mind can become calm. A familiar way of putting it: when you keep the precepts, you can go to sleep without "any unfinished cases" to replay in your head.
  • Concentration is the frame: a steady mind is where wisdom can stand. A restless mind — pulled all day by news, anxiety, and desire — cannot see subtle things clearly; only still water can reflect the moon.
  • Wisdom is the roof: only wisdom can uproot ignorance (article G.C3); ethical conduct and concentration without wisdom may produce a beautiful life, but do not yet bring suffering to an end.

And as the previous article showed with the eight factors: the three steps have a sequence but also interweave — wisdom makes ethical conduct easier (kept because one sees its consequences, not because one is forced), while concentration sharpens wisdom — an upward spiral, not three separate compartments.

3. One shared framework, different emphases

The three trainings are a shared framework across Buddhist traditions — but each tradition emphasizes different points, and this is useful to know before entering groups G.D/G.E/G.F:

  • Theravāda Buddhism: retains the full three-step sequence, emphasizing a clearly structured, step-by-step path — purified ethical conduct, meditative attainments, and contemplation of the three characteristics.
  • Mahāyāna Buddhism: emphasizes wisdom (prajñā wisdom — seeing emptiness) together with the bodhisattva's compassion; ethical conduct is expanded into bodhisattva precepts — training for the benefit of all beings; some schools, such as Chan/Zen, closely integrate concentration and wisdom.
  • Vajrayāna Buddhism: develops on a Mahāyāna foundation and adds powerful methods (ritual, imagery, visualization) as part of concentration — always together with a foundation in ethical conduct and wisdom.

The differences lie in emphasis — the three-part framework remains shared: no tradition says "discard ethical conduct and go straight to wisdom," and no tradition treats ethical conduct as the final destination. This way of reading the traditions will make the different styles of practice in later groups less surprising.

4. Loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity — four sublime states of mind

Along with the three trainings, Buddhist tradition points to four qualities of mind to cultivate — called the four divine abodes (Pali: brahmavihāra): cultivating these four qualities gives the mind a spacious place to dwell rather than trapping it inside a small sense of self.

  • Loving-kindness (Pali: mettā, Sanskrit: maitrī): wishing others to be happy — a warm form of care that does not seek possession; it differs from craving in that craving wants to keep someone for oneself, while loving-kindness wants the other person to be well, even when they are not with you.
  • Compassion (Pali: karuṇā): the heart responds to another's suffering and wishes for that suffering to end — unlike pity, compassion does not stand above the suffering person; it stands alongside them.
  • Sympathetic joy (Pali: muditā): rejoicing in another person's happiness — precious and difficult in a competitive society because it runs against the reflex to compare; sympathetic joy is an antidote to envy.
  • Equanimity (Pali: upekkhā): a balanced and even mind — not indifference: one still cares fully, but is not frantic and does not collapse into despair when circumstances change.

The four qualities keep one another in balance: loving-kindness without equanimity can become clinging; compassion without equanimity can become despair or exhaustion; sympathetic joy without right view can become excessive excitement; equanimity without the other three can become detachment or apathy. That is why the tradition teaches them as one set of four, not as isolated qualities. The texts record the Buddha presenting these four states of mind as a path toward a vast, expansive mind free from hostility. [Source: Dīgha Nikāya, DN 13 (Tevijja Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]. Detailed practices for cultivating these qualities belong to the Meditation section of the app.

5. The four qualities in family and work life

The strength of the four-quality framework is that it can be tested immediately in everyday relationships:

  • Loving-kindness in the family: caring for a child without turning care into control — "I want my child to be happy" is different from "I want my child to live according to my script"; caring for aging parents through presence, not only through things.
  • Compassion at work: when a colleague is struggling — instead of focusing on praise or blame, ask, "Is there anything I can do to help?"; compassion toward customers means not selling something you know will harm them — consistent with the spirit of right livelihood (previous article).
  • Sympathetic joy when others succeed: a colleague gets promoted, a sibling buys a house — an honest moment of self-checking: does your heart expand or contract? Sympathetic joy can be trained — start by offering a sincere congratulations, even if it feels awkward at first.
  • Equanimity when results are beyond your control: do your part as fully as possible, while recognizing that outcomes depend on many conditions (dependent origination — article G.C3) — a manager's evaluation, the market, the weather; equanimity is not giving up, but not torturing yourself over what you cannot control.
  • And toward yourself: this direction is often forgotten — after failure, people can be merciless toward themselves. Loving-kindness toward yourself when you stumble and equanimity toward yourself when facing what cannot be changed — not indulgence toward your mistakes, but the condition needed to get back up and make real corrections.

A practical observation: many family and workplace conflicts do not lack kindness; they lack the balanced set of four: care that becomes clinging (loving-kindness without equanimity), concern that becomes exhaustion (compassion without equanimity), or discomfort with another person's happiness (lack of sympathetic joy). Seen through this fourfold framework, the "way of life" becomes a concrete practice that can be examined every day.

6. Summary and next steps

  • The three trainings = ethical conduct — concentration — wisdom: the three-step training program for the whole path; the destination is liberation, Nibbāna.
  • The dependent sequence: ethical conduct supports concentration, concentration supports wisdom, wisdom supports liberation — the natural sequence from "no remorse" to "liberation" (AN 11.1); the three steps have a sequence but also interweave.
  • A shared framework across traditions — different emphases: Theravāda retains the methodical sequence; Mahāyāna emphasizes wisdom and bodhisattva compassion; Vajrayāna adds powerful methods within concentration.
  • The four divine abodes: loving-kindness — compassion — sympathetic joy — equanimity; they form one balanced set and can be practiced in ordinary relationships.
  • Application: loving-kindness in family life, compassion at work, sympathetic joy when others succeed, equanimity with results beyond your control — and with yourself as well.

Suggested next reading: the path now has a map (the Four Noble Truths), a prescription (the Noble Eightfold Path), a foundation (dependent origination and karma), an object of examination (the five aggregates and non-self), a training program (the three trainings), and a way of life (loving-kindness — compassion — sympathetic joy — equanimity). So what exactly is the destination? The next article — the final one in the shared foundational teachings group — answers: what is Nibbāna: not annihilation, not a heavenly realm, but what remains when the fires of suffering have gone out. After that, groups G.D/G.E/G.F will open the concrete paths of each tradition.

Glossary:

  • The three trainings (Pali: tisso sikkhā, Sanskrit: trīṇi śikṣāṇi): ethical conduct — concentration — wisdom; the training framework for the whole Buddhist path.
  • Ethical conduct (Pali: sīla, Sanskrit: śīla): training in ethical speech and action — the first training, the foundation for concentration.
  • Concentration (Pali: samādhi): training the mind to become steady and focused — the second training, the foundation for wisdom.
  • Wisdom (Pali: paññā, Sanskrit: prajñā): seeing reality clearly through direct experience — the third training, leading toward liberation.
  • Nibbāna (Pali: nibbāna, Sanskrit: nirvāṇa): the complete ending of suffering — the destination of the three trainings.
  • The four divine abodes (Pali: brahmavihāra): four spacious "places to dwell" for the mind — loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity; also called the four sublime abodes or the four immeasurables.
  • Loving-kindness (Pali: mettā, Sanskrit: maitrī): wishing others to be happy without seeking possession.
  • Compassion (Pali: karuṇā): responding to suffering and wishing for its end; standing alongside rather than above.
  • Sympathetic joy (Pali: muditā): rejoicing in another person's happiness — an antidote to envy.
  • Equanimity (Pali: upekkhā): balanced calm — still caring, without agitation; not indifference.
  • Bodhisattva precepts: the Mahāyāna expansion of ethical conduct, practiced for the benefit of all beings.
  • Emptiness (Sanskrit: śūnyatā): the lack of any fixed essence in phenomena — a central subject of Mahāyāna prajñā wisdom (explored in group G.E).
  • Pity: standing above someone who is suffering — clearly distinguished from compassion.
  • The sequence of eleven qualities (AN 11.1): keeping the precepts → no remorse → gladness → joy → tranquillity → happiness → concentration → seeing things as they really are → disenchantment → dispassion → liberation of mind — liberation through knowing and seeing.
  • The Noble Eightfold Path (Pali: aṭṭhaṅgika magga): the eightfold path — reorganized here as the three trainings; details in the previous article.
  • The four foundations of mindfulness (Pali: satipaṭṭhāna): body — feelings — mind — phenomena; a central tool within the training of concentration.
