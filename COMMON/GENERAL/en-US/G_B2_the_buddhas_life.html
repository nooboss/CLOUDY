G.B2. The Buddha's Life

The life of Śākyamuni Buddha is one of the most frequently retold stories in the history of Asian religions: a prince leaves the palace, spends six years seeking a path, awakens beneath a tree, and then spends the rest of his life teaching that path. Here, the story is not merely something to hear — it is also a key to understanding Buddhist teachings: nearly every core concept of Buddhism (the Middle Way, impermanence, karma, and so on) is connected to a stage of his life. This article presents his whole life chronologically, with notes on the degree of certainty attached to different layers of the tradition, so that beginners can hear the story while also learning how to read Buddhist life narratives.

1. The overall timeline

1.1. The whole life in one article

Buddhist traditions recount the Buddha's life through a timeline of the following major milestones:

  • Born at Lumbinī (Pali: Lumbinī, in present-day Nepal) — Queen Māyā gave birth to him while traveling to her parents' home.
  • Grew up in Kapilavastu — a sheltered princely life in the palace.
  • Went forth at age 29 — left the palace to seek a way beyond aging, illness, and death.
  • Six years of seeking — studied meditation with two teachers, practiced severe asceticism for six years, and then abandoned self-mortification.
  • Awakened under the Bodhi tree at Uruvelā (Bodh Gaya) at age 35 — fully realized the path and became a Buddha.
  • Taught for 45 years — traveled across central India, established the Saṅgha, and taught people from every social background.
  • Entered final passing at Kushinagar (Sanskrit: Kuśinagara) at age 80.

1.2. The problem of chronology

An important note about the numbers of years and age: traditional Buddhist accounts date the Buddha's birth to 623 BCE and his final passing to 543 BCE (some East Asian traditions differ by one year because of different calendrical systems). Modern scholarship, based on cross-checking with non-Buddhist historical sources, often places his lifetime around 480–400 BCE. The two chronologies differ substantially, and this difference reflects the very issue of "layers of information" discussed in section 8: the major milestones (going forth, awakening, final passing, and their associated ages) are highly stable within the tradition, while absolute calendar dates remain unsettled. Throughout this article, ages follow the traditional chronology; when books give different years, this is why.

Regarding terminology: in these articles, the Buddha is a respectful title; before awakening, his name was Siddhattha (Pali: Siddhattha, Sanskrit: Siddhārtha), from the Gotama/Gautama lineage. After awakening, he is commonly called Śākyamuni (Sanskrit: Śākyamuni), "the sage of the Śākyas." Around these chronological milestones, the first part of the story to tell is the prince's youth.

2. Prince Siddhartha and the four sights

2.1. Life in the palace

According to tradition, Siddhartha was born among the Śākyas — a clan republic on the northern edge of the Middle Country, as the previous article discussed — and was the son of clan leader King Suddhodana and Queen Māyā. A few days after his birth, a renowned seer examined him and predicted that the child would either become a wheel-turning king (Sanskrit: cakravartin) — a ruler over the whole world — or leave home and become a sage. His father, wanting him to inherit the throne, chose the first possibility: he raised Siddhartha in luxury, shielding him from every form of suffering, and built three palaces for the three seasons. Siddhartha grew up, married Princess Yaśodharā, and had a son, Rāhula.

For readers today, what matters is the way the scriptures tell this story: palace details, omens, festivals, and other scenes are narrated in rich color, but the main characters — father, mother, wife, and son — belong to the historical layer of the story. These are among the firmer elements of the tradition. Buddhist legends (Sanskrit: avadāna) add many beautiful details with a legendary character, and traditions differ considerably at this level.

2.2. The four sights

Tradition says that his father kept him inside the palace and prevented him from seeing aging, illness, and death — but one chariot ride outside the city changed everything. Along the way, he successively encountered four sights:

  • An old person — worn body, fallen teeth, dim eyes: a stage every human being will pass through.
  • A sick person — bedridden and groaning in pain: suffering does not spare anyone.
  • A dead person — a funeral procession carrying a corpse: the fate no one can avoid.
  • A renunciant — a calm śramaṇa (Pali: samaṇa), free from worldly bonds and seeking a way beyond all three.

The four sights presented a simple but shocking calculation: aging, illness, and death come to everyone — kings and commoners, palace and cottage alike — and yet someone was already looking for a way out. For a prince living in comfort, this was a moment of "awakening": the pleasures of the palace suddenly looked like temporary happiness on a sinking ship. Tradition says that that very night he looked at his sleeping wife and child, exchanged a final look, and left. The legend makes the scene vivid, but the core — a person deciding to give up everything he has in search of a way beyond suffering — is the most stable part of the story and is shared across traditions.

3. Going forth and six years of seeking

3.1. Leaving the palace

At age 29, Siddhartha went forth (Pali: pabbajjā) — leaving family, status, and palace life to become a śramaṇa, a free seeker (as introduced in the previous article). He removed his princely clothing, shaved his hair and beard, put on the ochre robe, and traveled east — where meditation teachers were gathering. The story of a wealthy prince giving up everything to go on alms round (Pali: piṇḍapāta) shocked the capital; and the reason for that act — not anger, despair, or heartbreak, but seeing suffering and seeing that a way out might exist — became a model for Buddhist renunciation that continues today.

3.2. Studying meditation with two teachers

His first destination was two renowned meditation teachers of the time: Āḷāra Kālāma and Uddaka Rāmaputta. In a short time, he reached the highest states of deep concentration taught by the two teachers: the sphere of infinite space (Pali: ākāsānañcāyatana) with the first, and the sphere of infinite consciousness (Pali: viññāṇañcāyatana) with the second. But each time he succeeded, he asked himself: "Is this truly the path to complete awakening?" — and answered: not yet, because those states still arise and pass away and do not reach the root of suffering. Both teachers invited him to remain and lead their communities with them; he declined both invitations and continued onward. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 26 (Ariyapariyesanā Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism].

3.3. Six years of asceticism

He left the settlements and went to the Uruvelā region, practicing asceticism (Sanskrit: tapas) with a group of five companions led by Koṇḍañña. For six years he practiced self-mortification: reducing his food intake day after day — tradition says he eventually consumed only one sesame seed and one grain of rice; his body became so emaciated that the bones could be felt through the skin of his abdomen. He tried the severe ascetic practices of the period — lying on thorns, standing on one leg, prolonged fasting — yet his mind did not move toward clear understanding. The reason he abandoned asceticism is stated very clearly in the scriptures: sensual indulgence is low, but self-mortification is no different — neither leads to awakening. The right path lies between them: the Middle Way (Pali: majjhima paṭipadā) — training body and mind appropriately, without sensual indulgence and without self-mortification.

Tradition tells the turning point this way: one morning he collapsed from fasting; a young woman named Sujātā offered him a bowl of milk-rice; he accepted it and felt his strength return. The five companions, disappointed because they thought he had "given up," left him. From then on, he bathed, ate normally, restored his body, and remained determined to continue on his own path. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 36 (Mahāsaccaka Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]. The story of Sujātā's milk-rice and the five companions leaving is told somewhat differently across traditions, with additional legendary details, but the central meaning — severe asceticism does not lead to liberation, and the body needs to be healthy enough for practice — is the most stable element.

The great practical lesson of those six years is that the spiritual path is not a war against one's own body. That balance later became part of the foundation of meditation practice: exert the right amount of effort, without forcing (this belongs to the app's Meditation section).

4. Awakening under the Bodhi tree

4.1. The night of meditation at Uruvelā

After regaining his health, he went to a forest near the Nerañjarā River, where there was a fig tree — later known as the Bodhi tree (Pali: bodhirukkha). He laid down a meditation seat (Pali: niṣīdana) beneath it and made a vow not to leave until he had found the path. That night — traditionally a full-moon night in the month of Vesākha, a date still commemorated today in many Theravāda Buddhist countries — he entered meditation and passed through the deepest stages of insight. The area was then called Uruvelā; today it is Bodh Gaya, one of the four major sacred sites of Buddhism.

The Pali Canon describes the stages of the night of awakening as follows: first, he attained the four meditative absorptions (Pali: jhāna) through his balanced path; then, during the night, his mind was led through special knowledges:

  • First knowledge (first watch of the night): recollection of past lives — his own, across many lifetimes — seeing clearly the continuing chain of birth and death.
  • Second knowledge (middle watch): seeing beings arise and pass away through successive lives, according to each person's karma.
  • Third knowledge (last watch): seeing the Four Noble Truths (suffering – origin – cessation – path); the mental defilements were completely exhausted and the mind was fully liberated. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 4 (Bhayabherava Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism].

As the last light of the night receded, a human being had awakened completely — becoming a Buddha (Pali: buddha). From this point, traditions differ more clearly: the Pali Canon describes the three watches as above, while some other traditions describe different stages — but all traditions share the central point that this person had fully realized the path to the ending of suffering.

4.2. The temptation of Māra

Legends say that before the night of awakening — or during its various stages, depending on the tradition — there was a confrontation with Māra (Pali: Māra), a figure representing the forces that pull the mind toward sensuality and doubt. Māra first sent his beautiful daughters to tempt the Buddha; when they failed, he came himself and challenged him: "This seat does not belong to you." The Buddha replied that the seat belonged to one who had cultivated merit through many lives and stopped all harmful actions — then he called the earth to witness, the earth-touching gesture (Sanskrit: bhūmisparśa-mudrā), familiar from many Buddha images. Māra was defeated, and his armies dispersed.

4.3. The meaning of the awakening story

Two things are worth remembering about the night of awakening. First, awakening is presented as the result of a human being's own effort — not a gift from the gods, not a ritual achievement, and not a divine favor. Second, the elements told in legendary form (Māra, the special knowledges, and the three watches of the night) are ways the scriptures express the meaning of the event: a mind that has overcome temptation, clearly seen karma and reality, and ended suffering completely. The distinction between the "event layer" and the "meaning layer" is discussed more fully in section 8.

5. Turning the Wheel of Dhamma — the first discourse

5.1. Teaching the five former companions

After awakening, according to the scriptures, the Buddha considered: the Dharma he had seen was very deep; people were still entangled in sensual pleasures and mistaken views, so would they understand it? Brahmā (Pali: Brahmā — a heavenly deity), in the scriptural narrative, came to request that he teach the world. The Buddha looked over the world and saw the five former companions at Uruvelā who had "little dust in their eyes" — and traveled to the Deer Park (Sanskrit: Ṛṣipatana, now Sarnath) near Vārāṇasī. The five companions, who had left him, saw him from afar and decided not to greet him formally; but as he approached, they could not maintain that decision — each hurried to take his robe and bowl and arrange a seat. The scene suggests that something had changed in their former teacher.

There the Buddha delivered his first discourse — the Turning the Wheel of Dhamma (Pali: Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta), often treated as the "birth certificate" of Buddhism. The discourse contains:

  • The Middle Way: avoiding two extremes — sensual indulgence and self-mortification — and following a balanced path.
  • The Four Noble Truths: suffering (diagnosis), origin (cause), cessation (possible outcome), and path (the treatment) — each truth is to be understood, practiced, and realized through three phases of insight.
  • After hearing it, Koṇḍañña attained the Dhamma eye (Pali: dhammacakkhu); soon afterward, all five attained the first stage of awakening — stream-entry (Pali: sotāpanna).

[Source: Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 56.11 (Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism].

5.2. Why is it called "turning the wheel"?

The image of "turning the wheel" comes from the world of that time: the wheel of a wheel-turning king symbolized a ruler whose authority extended everywhere he went. The Buddha "turned the wheel of Dhamma": wherever the teaching arrives, mistaken views are dismantled. With this discourse, the Dharma wheel began to turn — a path for ending suffering that could be passed from person to person — and it has continued turning to the present day. For a beginner, this is one of the discourses worth reading in full from the Theravāda collection — it is available directly in the app's foundational teachings (article G.C1).

6. 45 years of teaching and the first Saṅgha

6.1. The Saṅgha — a community of learning and practice

From the Deer Park, the Saṅgha grew: the original five companions, then Yaśas with his family and friends, then the group of the "three Kassapa brothers" — famous fire-worshipping ascetics with a thousand disciples — who entered the community; and when the Buddha returned to his home at Kapilavastu, many relatives went forth, including his son Rāhula, who became a novice monk (Pali: sāmaṇera). Father, son, cousins, and many members of the clan successively entered the religious life; traditional accounts say that the number of young men leaving prominent families moved the Buddha's father to request one rule: from then on, minors should not be ordained without parental permission — a rule that remains in force today. [Source: Vinaya Piṭaka, Mahāvagga - Theravāda Buddhism].

The Saṅgha (Pali: saṅgha) was not organized by caste but by stages of practice; the life of the monastics was governed by a code — monastic discipline (Pali: vinaya) — developed gradually from real cases. Its most famous daily practice was alms round: going from house to house for food, without cooking or storing provisions.

Among the disciples, the best known were Sāriputta — regarded as foremost in wisdom; Moggallāna — foremost in psychic powers; and Ānanda — an outstanding attendant with extraordinary memory, who would later recite the teachings at the first Buddhist council (the next article). During this period, the Buddha's foster mother and aunt, Mahāpajāpatī Gotamī, requested ordination; after repeated requests and Ānanda's intervention, the Buddha allowed it, beginning the order of bhikkhunīs (Pali: bhikkhunī). [Source: Vinaya Piṭaka, Cullavagga - Theravāda Buddhism].

A note about the figure of 45 years: the Pali tradition records that the Buddha taught for 45 years (from age 35 to 80); some East Asian traditions record 49 years — a small discrepancy that depends on calendrical and traditional calculations.

6.2. Teaching people from every social background

The 45 years of teaching are the story of a teacher walking across central India: visiting major kingdoms (Magadha and Kosala), clan republics, towns, and villages; speaking with kings, merchants, farmers, cleaners, the murderer Angulimāla (whose name means "finger garland"), philosophical communities, and ascetics. The scriptures preserve thousands of conversations, each tied to the particular circumstances of its listeners — the same truth expressed differently according to the audience (Sanskrit: upāya). Buddhist texts also record that the Buddha encouraged his disciples to develop their own languages for teaching: each should use the language of their own community — a principle reflected throughout the later history of Buddhism. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 3.110 - Theravāda Buddhism].

Major events during these 45 years included organizing the community (rules of life and the rains retreat, when monastics stayed in one place during the rainy season), major dialogues with King Bimbisāra and King Pasenadi, and the famous women disciples Khemā and Uppalavaṇṇā — honored as the two foremost female disciples. The whole period is richly represented in the scriptures; the articles on Saṅgha life (group G.D3) will draw on those sources directly.

7. Final passing at Kushinagar

7.1. The final days

At age 80, after 45 years of walking and teaching, the Buddha's body had grown tired. The scriptures say that at Beluva, where he spent his final rainy season, he told Ānanda that if asked, he could remain for another human lifespan — but Ānanda, with his mind obscured by Māra, did not make the request in time. Māra then came and invited the Buddha to enter final passing, and he agreed: three months later he would relinquish conditioned life. Ānanda regretted it and returned; the Buddha's reassurance contains a lesson about something everyone experiences: even at the final moment, an opportunity can disappear if it is not taken.

7.2. Final instructions and passing

At Kushinagar (Sanskrit: Kuśinagara), between two sal trees, the Buddha lay on his right side — the familiar image of a reclining Buddha — and gave his final instruction: "Be diligent; all conditioned things are impermanent" (everything conditioned changes and does not last forever). Then, step by step, he passed through the meditative absorptions and entered final passing. These final days are recorded in detail in the Mahāparinibbāna Sutta (Pali), a discourse on the Great Passing — the longest discourse in the Pali Canon. [Source: Dīgha Nikāya, DN 16 (Mahāparinibbāna Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism].

7.3. Relics and cremation

After the final passing, the body was cremated according to the rites used for a wheel-turning king; from the ashes came relics (Sanskrit: śarīra) — hard remains that were believed to survive the fire — which were divided among different kingdoms into eight portions for the construction of reliquary stupas. The veneration of relics — and the building of stupas (Sanskrit: stūpa) — became a major cultural stream in Buddhist history; the pillars and stupas associated with King Aśoka developed on this foundation.

8. Historical record and legend — how to read them

8.1. Two layers of information

When reading articles about the Buddha's life, it is important to distinguish two layers of information:

  • The event layer — relatively stable milestones shared across traditions: a man of the Śākya lineage was born in central/northern India; at age 29 he went forth; he studied meditation, practiced asceticism, and awakened; he taught for decades and established the Saṅgha; he entered final passing at age 80.
  • The legend layer — narrative details used to convey meaning: the four sights with their vivid imagery, Māra and his armies, the three special knowledges of the night, and so on.

The key point for beginners is this: legendary details are not there simply to be believed or disbelieved, but to be understood for the meaning the community sought to transmit. The scriptures do not present themselves as modern biographies; these details belong to the storytelling dimension of a culture transmitted orally over many centuries. This layered approach helps beginners read accounts of the Buddha calmly — without losing confidence because some details are symbolic, and without feeling compelled to accept every detail as a literal historical event.

8.2. Historical sources

Information about the Buddha's life reaches us through the following sources:

  • The Pali Canon — the most detailed source, preserving the Buddha's life through discourses that include autobiographical passages (especially MN 26, MN 36, MN 85, DN 2, and DN 16); preserved by Theravāda Buddhism.
  • Scriptures of other traditions — Chinese and Tibetan translations preserve parallel discourses; differences in detail arise naturally, like different retellings of the same story.
  • Legends and biographies — such as Buddhacarita (literally, "Acts of the Buddha"), an epic biography of the Buddha from around the 1st–2nd centuries CE, together with later collections of legends.
  • Archaeological evidence — Aśoka's Lumbinī pillar (3rd century BCE) records the king's visit to the Buddha's birthplace; sacred sites identified through pillars and archaeological stupas provide some of the most stable evidence.

The firmest historical core is this: a teacher named Siddhārtha Gautama, belonging to the Śākya clan, taught in central India and established a Saṅgha — he really existed; symbolic details belong to the legendary layer. For beginners reading these life stories, this attitude is useful: traditions preserve the Buddha's life in rich and beautiful forms, while distinguishing the layers of information makes the reading both easier and deeper.

9. Summary and next steps

  • The Buddha's life includes the major milestones of birth at Lumbinī, upbringing at Kapilavastu, going forth at age 29, six years of seeking, awakening at age 35 at Uruvelā (Bodh Gaya), 45 years of teaching, and final passing at age 80 at Kushinagar.
  • The four sights — aging, illness, death, and a renunciant — were the turning point that led the prince to go forth.
  • Six years of asceticism and Sujātā's bowl of milk-rice led to the discovery of the Middle Way: neither sensual indulgence nor self-mortification.
  • On the night of awakening under the Bodhi tree, the Buddha fully realized the path through human effort; Māra in the legends represents temptation and doubt.
  • The Turning the Wheel of Dhamma — the first discourse — presented the Middle Way and the Four Noble Truths; the Saṅgha formed and opened to people of all backgrounds, women and men alike.
  • When reading accounts of the Buddha's life, distinguish the event layer (stable milestones) from the legend layer (narrative forms used to convey meaning).

Glossary:

  • Śākyamuni Buddha (Sanskrit: Śākyamuni): the awakened teacher of the Śākya lineage; before awakening, his name was Siddhartha, from the Gotama/Gautama lineage.
  • Deep concentration (Pali: jhāna): deep states of mental unification and concentration, acknowledged by the Buddha as stages on the path rather than its final goal.
  • Three special knowledges of the night: the way the Pali Canon describes the night of awakening — recollecting past lives, seeing beings arise and pass according to karma, and seeing the Four Noble Truths.
  • Māra (Pali: Māra): a figure representing temptation and doubt; Māra's temptations are a scriptural way of expressing inner challenges.
  • Earth-touching gesture (Sanskrit: bhūmisparśa): the Buddha's gesture of calling the earth to witness; in images it is shown as the earth-touching mudrā (Sanskrit: bhūmisparśa-mudrā).
  • Deer Park (Sanskrit: Ṛṣipatana): the place where the Buddha delivered his first discourse, now Sarnath near Vārāṇasī.
  • Dhamma eye (Pali: dhammacakkhu): the "eye of the Dharma" — seeing the path clearly, attained upon hearing the first discourse.
  • Event layer / legend layer: a way of distinguishing stable milestones (going forth, awakening, final passing) from symbolic details (Māra, the four sights) used to convey meaning.
  • Avadāna (Sanskrit: avadāna): Buddhist legends told for edification, containing many beautiful but legendary details.
  • Buddhacarita (Sanskrit: Buddhacarita): an epic biography of the Buddha from around the 1st–2nd centuries CE.
  • Alms round (Pali: piṇḍapāta): the monastic practice of going from house to house for food, without cooking or storing provisions.
  • Novice monk (Pali: sāmaṇera): a young monastic who has not yet received the full bhikkhu ordination.
  • Bhikkhunī (Pali: bhikkhunī): a fully ordained Buddhist nun; the bhikkhunī order began when the Buddha allowed his foster mother to go forth.
  • Relics (Sanskrit: śarīra): remains collected after cremation and traditionally divided into portions for enshrinement in stupas.
  • Stupa (Sanskrit: stūpa): a structure built to enshrine relics, an important ancestor of later Buddhist reliquary architecture.
  • Mahāparinibbāna Sutta (Pali: Mahāparinibbāna Sutta): the longest discourse in the Pali Canon, recording the Buddha's final days.
  • Turning the Wheel of Dhamma (Pali: Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta): the first discourse, presenting the Middle Way and the Four Noble Truths.
