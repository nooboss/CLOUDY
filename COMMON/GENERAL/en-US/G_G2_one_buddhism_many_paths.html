G.G2. One Buddhism, Many Paths — A Non-Sectarian Attitude

The previous article — G.G1 — placed the three traditions side by side in a comparative table — and that conclusion is itself the heart of this article: differences are about skillful means, not about true versus false. G.G2 develops that insight into a lived attitude for reading, studying, and meeting practitioners from other lineages — whether Theravāda Buddhism, Mahāyāna Buddhism (Sanskrit: Mahāyāna), or Vajrayāna Buddhism (Sanskrit: Vajrayāna) — the attitude this app calls "non-sectarian": respecting every path, without turning study into a race for prizes. This article covers: why every tradition deserves respect; how great teachers studied across lineages; how to read pieces that disparage other schools while staying balanced; and an answer to the familiar question — "which school is the most correct?".

1. Why respect every tradition

Four reasons are already present in the earlier articles, now placed side by side:

One — one common root. The comparative table has shown it: all three traditions honor the same Buddha, share the same four bodies of foundational teachings, and aim at the same goal of Nirvāṇa (article G.G1 — the "major common ground" section). To disparage one branch of the same tree is to disparage the very tree one lives in.

Two — each path was born from a genuine need. Theravāda preserves monastic life and the early scriptural collection; Mahāyāna opens the bodhisattva path to all walks of life; Vajrayāna gathers the earlier insights into practice systems transmitted directly from teacher to student (articles G.D1, G.E1, G.F1). Each form has endured for more than two thousand years and nourished hundreds of millions of people — proof enough that the path can be walked.

Three — history shows exchange, not hatred. There were indeed debates — sudden versus gradual in Zen (article G.E4), the councils (article G.B4) — but the larger pattern is reception: China received the scriptures from India, Japan received them from China, and Vietnamese Buddhists visit both northern- and southern-tradition temples (articles G.E5, G.B7). The lineages have "borrowed" from one another far more than they have "fought" one another.

Four — and this the app states directly: under editorial principle no. 1, the order "basic → advanced" is only a learning pathway for knowledge, never a ranking; phrases such as "Theravāda is the root and Mahāyāna the branch" or "Vajrayāna is higher" — such sentences are not used. In the same spirit: the term Hīnayāna (Sanskrit: Hīnayāna - Lesser Vehicle) — found in some ancient Mahāyāna texts — is not used today as the name of a tradition, because the word itself carries a judgment ("inferior"); when the early schools need to be mentioned, they are called "the early Buddhist schools," or "Theravāda Buddhism" when that lineage is meant (article G.B5). Changing how we name things is itself a practice of respect — not "political correctness," but letting go of a word that plants comparison.

2. Teachers who studied across lineages

A non-sectarian attitude is not a modern "easygoing" idea — the most revered teachers themselves lived it:

The Rimé movement. In eastern Tibet (Kham) in the 19th century, the two great scholars Jamgön Kongtrul and Jamyang Khyentse Wangpo launched the Rimé movement (Tibetan: ris med - non-sectarian movement) — deliberately studying and collecting the teachings of all four schools, Nyingma, Kagyu, Sakya, and Gelug, gathering back lineages on the verge of being lost instead of letting them fade through rivalry (article G.F2). Rimé founded no separate school and did not "melt" the lineages into one — it honored each lineage and preserved the rich spirit of them all. This spirit shapes most Tibetan teachers teaching in the West today, including the 14th Dalai Lama — who belongs to the Gelug school yet always encourages study and reverence toward every lineage.

Zen–Pure Land combined practice in Vietnam. Since the Lý–Trần era, the Trúc Lâm Zen school (article G.B7) refined and harmonized the Zen streams entering Vietnam; centuries later, Vietnamese temple life naturally became a form of "dual practice": morning Zen chanting, afternoon Buddha-recitation — the temple does not divide "my school" from "yours" but uses whichever method fits each person. This is exactly what article G.E4 describes: the East Asian schools share the same Vinaya, chant together, and do not exclude one another — a "school" is more like a specialization, as a department within one institute, not a separate church.

Teachers who bridge traditions worldwide. Zen Master Thích Nhất Hạnh (articles G.E3, G.B7) brought Vietnamese Zen, grounded in Mahāyāna, to the world and entered into dialogue with Theravāda meditation methods; the Thai scholar-monk Buddhadāsa read Mahāyāna sūtras to enrich his Theravāda teaching; Western meditation halls invite teachers from all three streams. Such "cross-fertilization" does not dilute the Dhamma — it shows the Dhamma is vast enough to illuminate every culture.

3. Reading a piece that disparages another school

Today's learners easily come across articles, videos, and comments that disparage a school — "Vajrayāna is mere magic" or "Theravāda cultivates only oneself and abandons sentient beings" are two familiar examples (each already addressed in its own article — G.F1 and G.D1). Editorial principle no. 10 requires a neutral, respectful tone; below is a tool so readers can keep that same balance.

3.1. Look for the context first — much "criticism" is finished internal debate

Most sharp words between schools belong to debates of their own time: the sudden–gradual dispute in 8th-century Zen, the Samye debate of the 8th century (article G.F1), the doctrinal classifications ranking "which school is higher" in China and Japan (articles G.E4, G.E5). When you meet a disparaging line, ask: when was this said, by whom to whom, and is that dispute still alive today? — in most cases the answer is "it ended long ago"; keeping the heat of old disputes alive is carrying other people's business into your own mind.

3.2. Distinguish criticism from contempt

Three kinds of statements need to be separated:

  • Criticism of a conduct — valuable: pointing out abuse of teacher authority (article G.F2 — the healthy-boundaries section), commercialization, or misleading presentations — such criticism helps the whole field improve, and the school itself needs it.
  • A different interpretation — for example "what is Nirvāṇa" is explained differently across lineages (editorial principle no. 5): a different explanation does not mean someone is wrong — it is a different emphasis upon the same goal.
  • Contempt for an entire tradition — "that school is a demonic path," "that is not Buddhism" — this is the kind of line to read slowly: who wrote it, on what evidence, and aimed at whom. The app does not publish such pieces — and you have the choice not to take them in.

3.3. Return to the common ground and verify

The Buddha did not encourage blind belief; his famous teaching on verification runs: do not accept a thing merely because of oral tradition, because of legend, because of scriptures, or because of the authority of the speaker — but know for yourselves whether these things are wholesome or unwholesome. [Source: Kālāma Sutta (Kesamutti Sutta), AN 3.65 (Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 3.65) - Theravāda Buddhism] — this rule, ironically, applies to disparaging pieces as well: verify by returning to the original sources. Three short steps when you meet a disparaging claim: (1) trace it to its origin — what does the original text actually say, in what context (the source-layering principle — editorial no. 3: scriptures → commentaries → treatises → research → customs); (2) place it beside the major common ground of all three traditions (article G.G1 — section 4) — most accusations collapse when set against that shared foundation; (3) if doubt remains — ask a teacher inside the tradition being criticized, just as one understands a household best by asking someone within it rather than an outsider describing it.

4. "Which school is the most correct?" → "Which school suits me?"

The question "which school is the most correct?" carries a hidden assumption: if one path is right, the rest must be wrong. But article G.G1 has shown that assumption does not hold — all three paths climb the same mountain. A more useful question is "which school suits me?" — like choosing a field of study: not "which field is the best" but "which field suits me and which I will study seriously." Article G.A4 discussed in detail how to choose according to living environment, scriptural language, and a practice fitting one's character; here only three quick criteria are recalled:

  • Living environment — which lineage's temple is nearby, which community can support you week by week; the Dhamma is learned through community more easily than through books.
  • Language and texts — Pali discourses in Vietnamese translation, Chinese–Vietnamese sūtras, or Tibetan texts in translation: what you can actually read is what you can keep.
  • A practice fitting your character — if you like step-by-step analysis, you may find the Theravāda path or the lamrim congenial; if you love direct insight and poetic language, Zen may fit; if you love a tightly structured path with direct guidance from a teacher, the Tibetan lineages may fit. What "suits" you is a starting point, never a verdict upon the other paths.

Two further points for balance. First — "suitable" does not mean "casual": each path has its own discipline and sequence (articles G.D3, G.E2, G.F2); changing methods three times a week leads nowhere on any path. Second — you may change paths and you may walk more than one: Vietnamese Buddhists attend a Zen temple and read Theravāda books, Westerners practice Theravāda meditation and then study Mahāyāna — the teachers in section 2 did this all their lives. "Non-sectarian" does not mean "pathless" — it means walking your own path while respecting the paths of others.

5. Three principles to carry with you

  • Editorial no. 1 — no ranking. "Advanced" means only that more background is needed, never that "that school is higher." Any formula like "this lineage is authentic, that one diluted" — drop it at once.
  • Editorial no. 10 — neutrality and respect. Among schools, between Buddhism and other religions, and toward sensitive issues — present the sources, take no faction; apply this same principle to yourself when reading what others write.
  • Editorial no. 3 — source layering. Note clearly what is scripture, what is treatise, what is custom; when meeting a disparaging claim — trace it back to the root layer before believing or grieving.

6. Summary and next steps

  • Respect every tradition because: one common root, each path born of a genuine need, history as exchange, and naming itself as a practice of respect (never using "Hīnayāna").
  • Exemplary teachers — the Rimé movement, the East Asian schools practicing together, the bridge-teachers worldwide — lived this attitude centuries before us.
  • When meeting disparagement: find the context → distinguish criticism from contempt → trace the source and set it beside the common ground — in the spirit of the Kālāma Sutta.
  • Turn "which school is the most correct?" into "which school suits me?" — choose by environment, language, and character; walk seriously; you may change, you may combine.

Glossary:

  • Theravāda Buddhism: the tradition preserving the Pali Canon, centered in Sri Lanka and mainland Southeast Asia; often called the Southern school.
  • Mahāyāna Buddhism (Sanskrit: Mahāyāna): the tradition centered on the bodhisattva ideal, flourishing in East Asia; often called the Northern school.
  • Vajrayāna Buddhism (Sanskrit: Vajrayāna): the esoteric tradition of the Himalayan–Mongolian region, within the Mahāyāna foundation; often called the Esoteric school.
  • Hīnayāna (Sanskrit: Hīnayāna - Lesser Vehicle): a term appearing in some ancient Mahāyāna texts for other streams; carrying the judgmental overtone of "inferior" — the app does not use it; replaced by "early Buddhist schools" (the early schools) or "Theravāda" when that lineage is meant.
  • Rimé (Tibetan: ris med - non-sectarian movement): the 19th-century impartial movement in eastern Tibet (Kham) — studying and preserving the teachings of all four schools, without founding a separate school.
  • Jamgön Kongtrul (1813–1899) and Jamyang Khyentse Wangpo (1820–1892): the two great scholars who launched the Rimé movement; Jamgön Kongtrul's "Encyclopedia of Knowledge" (Sheja Kunkhyab - shes bya kun khyab) symbolizes the spirit of gathering every lineage.
  • Zen–Pure Land combined practice: the common practice of East Asian temples, especially in Vietnam — combining Zen inquiry and Buddha-recitation within the same temple life, according to each person's capacity.
  • Trúc Lâm: the Zen school founded by King Trần Nhân Tông in the 13th century — a purely Vietnamese Zen stream, refined from the incoming Zen lineages.
  • Buddhadāsa Bhikkhu (1906–1993): Thai monk and scholar — expounding the Theravāda discourses in an open spirit, in dialogue with Mahāyāna sūtras and modern thought.
  • Criticism / contempt: an important distinction when reading — criticism points out a conduct or a presentation to correct it (valuable); contempt denies an entire tradition (excluded from study).
  • Kālāma Sutta (Kesamutti Sutta), AN 3.65: the famous discourse on the spirit of verification — not accepting a teaching merely from authority or hearsay, but examining it for oneself; the foundation for a balanced reading attitude.
  • Lamrim (Tibetan: lam rim - stages of the path): the graduated study sequence of the Gelug school — an example of a tightly structured learning path.
