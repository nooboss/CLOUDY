G.A4. Cross-tradition Orientation

The final article in the introductory group answers a question almost every newcomer eventually asks: Buddhism has many traditions — so which one should I start with? It is a reasonable question, but it is often asked earlier than necessary. This article does three things: it explains why there is no need to rush; gives a quick view of the three major traditions so you know what the choices actually are; and offers three practical criteria for choosing — along with several ways to "try before committing" for the long term.

1. The good news first — you do not need to choose yet

The most important thing to know, and also the reassurance for anyone who is still undecided, is this: the foundational teachings of Buddhism are shared across all three traditions. The Four Noble Truths, the Noble Eightfold Path, dependent origination, karma, the five aggregates, and loving-kindness, compassion, appreciative joy, and equanimity — the whole set of foundational teachings introduced in article G.A2 is accepted across all three traditions. Learning this common foundation means a learner does not have to "bet" on any particular tradition — which is precisely why the learning path recommends starting here.

In practice, most of what beginners need in their first few years — understanding the life of the Buddha, understanding the Four Noble Truths, and beginning to practice ethical conduct and mindfulness — does not depend on choosing a tradition. The traditions differ in how they develop the path: which aspects they emphasize, which scriptural systems and languages they use, what practices and rituals they employ, and what their cultural life looks like (article G.B5 tells the story of how the three traditions formed). In other words, "which tradition should I choose?" is a question for someone who has already traveled part of the road — a beginner only needs to know that the choice is waiting further ahead.

That is the spirit of this article: do not choose because of pressure; learn because of curiosity — and if you do need to choose early (for example, because you need a nearby temple where you can study), the criteria in section 3 can help you choose with a clear mind rather than simply following a trend.

2. The three traditions — a quick overview

Before choosing, you first need to know what there is to choose from. Here are the three major traditions in very brief "profiles":

  • Theravāda Buddhism: preserves its scriptural canon in Pali and presents a clear, methodical sequence of training — ethics, meditative absorptions, and contemplation; it is widespread in Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand, Laos, and Cambodia; in Vietnam, it includes ethnic Vietnamese Theravāda (Vietnamese practitioners who use the Pali scriptures) and Khmer Theravāda (the Khmer community in the Mekong Delta — introduced in article G.B7).
  • Mahāyāna Buddhism: its scriptures were transmitted largely through Chinese translations; its central bodhisattva ideal is to practice for the benefit of all beings; it includes a rich variety of methods — meditation (Chan/Zen), Buddha-recitation (Pure Land), and systematic study of Buddhist teachings; it is widespread in China, Vietnam, Japan, and Korea; in Vietnam, the Northern tradition around us is largely part of this tradition, with dual practice of Chan and Pure Land as a characteristic feature.
  • Vajrayāna Buddhism: developed on a Mahāyāna foundation and adds powerful methods — mantras, visualization, and ritual — under the guidance of a teacher; it is widespread in Tibet, Bhutan, Mongolia, and Himalayan communities.

Three things are worth remembering when looking at these three profiles. First, there is no "higher" tradition — the app does not rank them, and the Buddhist spirit is often expressed through the image of "many paths up one mountain." Second, the three traditions have continually interacted — they are not three completely separate religious bodies (as article G.B5 explains); in practice, many people study across traditions. Third, each tradition contains many schools with very different styles — East Asian Mahāyāna alone includes Chan, Pure Land, and others; in practice, people often choose a particular teacher and community rather than a large label.

3. Three criteria for choosing

When the time comes to choose — or when you need to choose a place to begin — the experience of many learners can be summarized in three criteria, in order of practical importance:

  • Criterion 1 — Your living environment: the Buddhist path is sustained through community — a temple, teacher, and fellow practitioners nearby are often more sustainable than something that merely looks good "on paper." In Vietnam, most people live closer to Northern-tradition temples (Mahāyāna) — many of which now offer open meditation and Dharma classes; larger cities also have ethnic Vietnamese Theravāda communities, modern meditation groups, and revived Trúc Lâm Zen monasteries. If your local area offers only one realistic option, that may simply be the practical answer, and it does not put you at any disadvantage.
  • Criterion 2 — The language of the scriptures and books you can read: the path needs to be nourished by the Dharma — and Dharma that you can actually understand in your own language is what can take root. Vietnamese translations of Pali scriptures, Sino-Vietnamese Buddhist texts, and books translated from Tibetan traditions each provide a different pool of resources; choose the tradition whose available resources you can understand — even if, at first, that means only introductory books and Dharma talks.
  • Criterion 3 — A practice that fits your temperament: practices are different "skillful means" for different kinds of minds — the scriptures record the Buddha teaching different approaches according to his listeners. Someone whose mind is easily scattered may find Buddha-recitation helpful (keeping the whole mind with one Buddha-name); someone who likes to look directly into the mind may prefer sitting meditation and kōan introspection; someone who values systematic clarity may prefer studying Buddhist teachings and then practicing; someone comfortable with visualization and ritual may be drawn to methods involving imagery and ritual, often found in Vajrayāna Buddhism. No practice is "stronger" — a practice that fits you is one you can stay with, and staying with it is what allows progress.

The order of these three criteria is also practical: your living environment determines whether you can go; language determines whether you can understand; a suitable practice determines whether you can keep going steadily. When all three are in place, the choice is usually clear; when one is missing, strengthen that part first.

4. Try before committing

Just as with choosing a career, a few small experiences can help you decide much more clearly than theory alone. Several forms of "trying" are quite practical:

  • Visit two or three places from different traditions: attend a practice service at a Northern-tradition temple, spend a day meditating at a Theravāda or Trúc Lâm monastery, and listen online to a Dharma talk from another community — experience the culture, chanting, and rhythm of daily life directly.
  • Try two or three practices for a few weeks each: two weeks of Buddha-recitation, two weeks of mindful breathing — record your actual experience (article G.A3 discusses the spirit of "provisional confidence for the sake of testing"). A short period is enough to learn whether "this practice speaks to me," but it is not long enough to judge results — do not rush to a conclusion in either direction.
  • Read the comparison before you "marry": the app has a dedicated overall comparison of the three traditions (article G.G1) — compare scriptures, ideals, practices, and festivals in a table; then the tradition-specific groups (D/E/F) allow you to sample all three before going deeply into one.
  • Ask someone who actually practices: ask a person who has been on the path for several years what keeps them there and what has been hardest; honest answers are often more convincing than any brochure.

And one final piece of advice, in the spirit of the entire introductory group G.A: people choose a tradition in order to receive support for walking the shared path — not to define themselves. Buddhism's thousands of years across many cultures have created a vast storehouse of skillful means from which each person can find a workable way forward; an app reader does not need to carry a label in their pocket — only a clear direction and a kind community. Then "multiple traditions" stops being a difficult problem and becomes a resource.

5. Summary and next steps

  • You do not need to choose immediately: foundational teachings are shared across all three traditions — most of a beginner's early learning does not depend on the choice; choosing is a question for someone who has already traveled some distance.
  • The three traditions are Theravāda Buddhism (Pali, systematic progression), Mahāyāna Buddhism (Chinese-transmitted scriptures, the bodhisattva ideal, a rich range of practices), and Vajrayāna Buddhism (a Mahāyāna foundation plus powerful methods under teacher guidance) — none is higher than the others; in practice, people choose a particular teacher and community.
  • Three criteria for choosing: living environment (whether you can go) — scriptural language (whether you can understand) — practice that fits your temperament (whether you can keep going).
  • Try before committing: visit several places — try practices for a few weeks — read the comparison — ask actual practitioners.

Glossary:

  • Multi-tradition approach: an approach to learning and respecting all major Buddhist traditions rather than taking sides too early.
  • Theravāda Buddhism: the tradition that preserves the Pali Canon and is widespread in mainland Southeast Asia; in Vietnam, this includes ethnic Vietnamese Theravāda and Khmer Theravāda.
  • Mahāyāna Buddhism: a tradition whose scriptures were transmitted largely through Chinese; centered on the bodhisattva ideal and widespread in East Asia; in Vietnam, the Northern tradition.
  • Vajrayāna Buddhism: the tradition that developed on a Mahāyāna foundation with tantric methods; widespread in the Himalayas and Mongolia.
  • Ethnic Vietnamese Theravāda: a Vietnamese community practicing the Theravāda tradition through the Pali scriptures, established in the early twentieth century.
  • Khmer Theravāda: the Theravāda tradition of the Khmer community in the Mekong Delta, closely connected with Khmer language and festivals.
  • Northern tradition: the common Vietnamese term for East Asian Mahāyāna transmitted through China.
  • Pure Land: the Buddha-recitation practice of aspiring to rebirth in the pure realm of Amitābha Buddha — a major practice tradition within East Asian Mahāyāna.
  • Chan–Pure Land dual practice: a characteristic form of Vietnamese Northern-tradition practice — combining kōan introspection and Buddha-recitation.
  • Bodhisattva: the Mahāyāna ideal of practicing toward awakening for the benefit of all beings.
  • Kōan introspection: a Chan practice using a question or phrase that is not solved through ordinary logical reasoning, turning the mind inward.
  • Buddha-recitation: a practice of gathering the mind around a Buddha's name or title.
  • Practice method: a specific method of entering and practicing the Dharma — the idea of "different gates into the same house."
  • Practice session: a scheduled period at a temple for chanting, bowing, meditation, or related forms of practice.
  • Trúc Lâm Zen monastery: centers of the revived Trúc Lâm tradition — the Vietnamese Zen lineage associated with Trần Nhân Tông (article G.B7).
