G.G1. Comparing the Three Major Traditions — A Reference Table

The previous article - G.F5 closed the cluster on Vajrayāna Buddhism (Sanskrit: Vajrayāna - Diamond Vehicle) — and with it, the three clusters G.D/G.E/G.F have each completed the portrait of the three major traditions. This article places all three side by side in a single comparative overview — to be used as a reference map: whenever you need a quick comparison of any two traditions on any criterion (scriptures, ideals, practices, festivals...), this is the place to return to. The article includes: three principles for reading the table; the comparative overview; an explanation of each criterion; a list of the major points of common ground shared by all three; and a conclusion — where the differences lie, and where they do not.

1. Before comparing — three principles for reading the table

One — the three traditions share a single root. Theravāda, Mahāyāna, and Vajrayāna are all lines of development of the same Buddhism opened up by Śākyamuni Buddha — not three different religions. That history — early period → schools → three great streams — was told in article G.B5; the three traditions have continuously intersected rather than standing as three separate "churches".

Two — compare in order to understand, not to rank. The editorial principle of the app is that "basic → advanced" is a sequence for learning, not a ranking of traditions; formulations such as "Theravāda is the root, Mahāyāna the branch" or "Vajrayāna is higher" are not used. The question "which tradition is most correct?" should be reframed as "which path suits me?" — that healthy attitude is the theme of the next article - G.G2.

Three — each cell of the table is a whole article. This table is a map, not an in-depth treatment; each criterion has dedicated articles that present it fully (cited in section 3). After reading the table, follow the branch you need.

2. Comparative overview

2.1. Theravāda Buddhism

  • Other names: Southern school; School of the Elders
  • Main regions: Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand, Laos, Cambodia
  • Scriptures: Pāli Canon
  • Path ideal: Arahant — personal liberation
  • Representative practices: Samatha–vipassanā meditation; alms-round; rains retreat; observance days
  • Guides: Saṅgha and virtuous teachers
  • Major festival: Vesak (Buddha Day)
  • Festival calendar: Southern Buddhist lunar calendar
  • In Vietnam: Kinh Theravāda; Khmer Theravāda

2.2. Mahāyāna Buddhism

  • Other names: Northern school
  • Main regions: China, Vietnam, Korea, Japan
  • Scriptures: Chinese Buddhist Canon (translated mainly from Sanskrit)
  • Path ideal: Bodhisattva — awakening together with all beings
  • Representative practices: Daily liturgical practice; Buddha-recitation; meditation; sūtra chanting
  • Guides: Abbots, meditation masters
  • Major festivals: Buddha's Birthday and Ullambana
  • Festival calendar: Vietnamese–Chinese lunar calendar
  • In Vietnam: Northern school — the majority; Trúc Lâm

2.3. Vajrayāna Buddhism

  • Other names: Esoteric school
  • Main regions: Tibet, Bhutan, Mongolia, Nepal; Russian communities
  • Scriptures: Kangyur and Tengyur (in Tibetan)
  • Path ideal: Bodhisattva ideal with tantric methods (empowerment, tutelary deities...)
  • Representative practices: Empowerment; mantra; maṇḍala; tutelary deities
  • Guides: Lamas within a lineage
  • Major festivals: Losar (New Year) and Saga Dawa
  • Festival calendar: Tibetan calendar
  • In Vietnam: A number of centres

3. Each criterion in depth

3.1. Scriptures and languages

Three scriptural systems — three linguistic carriers: the Pāli Canon (Pali: tipiṭaka - Three Baskets) of Theravāda — the most intact early corpus still remaining (article G.D2); the Chinese Buddhist Canon (Chinese Buddhist canon) of Mahāyāna — translated mainly from Sanskrit (article G.E3); and the Kangyur–Tengyur pair of Vajrayāna — translated into Tibetan (article G.F3). The point to see: all three systems contain the early teachings — the Chinese Canon holds the Āgama collections corresponding to the four Nikāyas of Pali; the Kangyur preserves the same foundational sūtras. In other words, "different scriptures" mainly means different newly added collections, not different foundations.

3.2. Path ideal

Theravāda takes the arahant (Pali: arahant, Sanskrit: arhat - liberated one) — complete liberation in this very life — as the summit (articles G.C7, G.D1); Mahāyāna takes the bodhisattva (Pali: bodhisatta, Sanskrit: bodhisattva - enlightenment being) — vowing to bring all beings along — as the ideal (article G.E2); Vajrayāna keeps the bodhisattva ideal and adds tantric methods (articles G.F1, G.F2). These are two to three different emphases of the same Buddhism, not higher and lower levels; the Pali corpus itself contains the Jātaka (Pali - birth stories) — the bodhisattva ideal is a shared heritage, not a separate invention.

3.3. Regional presence

Theravāda — South Asia and mainland Southeast Asia (article G.D4); Mahāyāna — East Asia (article G.E5); Vajrayāna — the Himalayas, Mongolia, and Russian communities (article G.F4). But the map is not a hard boundary: Vietnam has both Theravāda and Mahāyāna (article G.B7); the West has received all three; and migrant communities have brought all three traditions to every continent (article G.B6).

3.4. Representative practices

The prominent methods of each stream — samatha–vipassanā meditation, alms-round, and rains retreat in Theravāda (article G.D3); daily liturgical practice, Buddha-recitation, and meditation in Mahāyāna (articles G.E4, G.E6); empowerment, mantra, maṇḍala, and tutelary deities in Vajrayāna (article G.F2). But read vertically as well as horizontally: meditation is present in all three — Theravāda meditation, East Asian Zen, and the mind-recognition practices of Nyingma–Kagyu — three "meditation families" are three ways of working with the same capacity of mind; detailed practice belongs to the app's Meditation section.

3.5. Festivals

Each stream follows its own calendar: Theravāda follows the Southern Buddhist lunar calendar (the great festival of Vesak (Pali: Vesākha - Vesak) — the full-moon day of the month of Vesākha, around April–May of the solar calendar — article G.D5); East Asian Mahāyāna follows the Vietnamese–Chinese lunar calendar (Buddha's Birthday — lunar 15/4 in Vietnam, 8/4 in China; Ullambana — lunar 15/7 — article G.E7); Vajrayāna follows the Tibetan calendar (Losar (Tibetan: lo gsar - Tibetan New Year) and the month of Saga Dawa — differing from the Vietnamese lunar calendar by 1–2 months depending on the year — article G.F5). The same great festival — the Buddha's Birthday — exists in all three, but its date and customs differ by calendar system: this is where newcomers are most easily confused, so always note the calendar system when speaking of festivals.

3.6. In Vietnam

The picture of Vietnamese Buddhism today (article G.B7): the Northern school — the majority, with a combined Zen–Pure Land style; Kinh Theravāda and Khmer Theravāda; the purely Vietnamese Zen lineage of Trúc Lâm; and a number of centres following Tibetan lineages. Vietnamese learners therefore in practice "live among" two — sometimes three — traditions: visiting a Northern monastery, perhaps stopping by a Khmer monastery in the southwest, and reading books by teachers of all three streams — that is an advantage, not a confusion.

4. Major points of common ground

The table above makes the differences easy to see; but no less important are what all three traditions affirm together — the foundational teachings presented in full in cluster G.C:

  • Three Jewels — taking refuge in Buddha, Dhamma, and Saṅgha is the common entry point of every tradition (article G.A1); outer forms may differ, the content is one.
  • Four Noble Truths (Pali: cattāri ariyasaccāni - Four Noble Truths) — suffering, origin, cessation, path: the shared "backbone" of the teachings (article G.C1).
  • Noble Eightfold Path (Pali: aṭṭhaṅgika magga, Sanskrit: āṣṭāṅga mārga - Noble Eightfold Path) — the eightfold path gathered into virtue – concentration – wisdom (articles G.C2, G.C6).
  • Vinaya (Sanskrit: Vinaya - monastic discipline) — monastic life rests on Vinaya collections of shared early origin (article G.B4); monastics of all three traditions alike receive precepts and uphold the spirit of the Vinaya.
  • Saṅgha (Pali: saṅgha - community) — the community that preserves the teachings, one of the Three Jewels in all three streams (article G.A1).
  • Nirvana (Pali: nibbāna, Sanskrit: nirvāṇa - nirvana) — the final goal is one (article G.C7); the traditions express it differently, which does not mean they have different goals.

Other shared foundations include: dependent origination, the five aggregates – not-self, karma and rebirth — all contents of cluster G.C — "teachings affirmed by all three traditions". A tip for reading sources: when you meet a sentence of the form "this tradition does not have..." — check it first against cluster C; most such "does not have" cases only mean not emphasized or called by another name.

5. Conclusion — different in means, not in genuineness

Placing the two preceding parts side by side, the conclusion draws itself: the major differences lie in means — the newly added scriptural collections, the chosen methods, the forms of organization — and in emphasis — personal liberation or awakening together with beings, foundational or tantric; while the goal and doctrinal core are shared. Many further surface differences are in fact local culture — Buddhism wearing the "garments" of each receiving culture: a Thai temple differs from a Japanese temple not because the teachings differ, but because the cultures differ — the very phenomenon of "Sinicization" and "Tibetanization" seen in articles G.E5, G.F1.

A familiar image to keep: three paths up the same mountain — the paths start from different sides, the scenery along the way differs, but all lead to one summit. The image is right in affirming the shared ground; but do not stretch it into "all paths are identical" — each path has its own discipline and sequence (articles G.D3, G.E2, G.F2). And in every case, "which path is correct" is not the right question — "which path fits, and how to learn in a healthy way" is — the theme of the next article - G.G2.

6. Summary and next steps

  • Comparative overview — the three traditions differ in: scriptures (Pali / Chinese / Tibetan), ideals (arahant / bodhisattva / bodhisattva + tantra), regions (Southeast Asia / East Asia / Himalayas–Mongolia), practices, and festival calendars.
  • But on one foundation: Three Jewels, Four Noble Truths, Noble Eightfold Path, Vinaya, Saṅgha, Nirvana — along with the early teachings present in all three scriptural systems (Āgamas in the Chinese Canon, foundational sūtras in the Kangyur).
  • The differences concern means and emphasis — together with local cultural differences — not "which school is genuine and which is false"; no tradition is ranked above another.
  • In Vietnam, learners "live among" two to three traditions — that is direct evidence of "one Buddhism, many paths".

Glossary:

  • Theravāda Buddhism (Pali: Theravāda - Way of the Elders): the tradition that preserves the scriptural canon in Pali, centred in Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand, Laos, and Cambodia; in Vietnam it is often called the Southern school.
  • Mahāyāna Buddhism (Sanskrit: Mahāyāna - Great Vehicle): the tradition centred on the ideal of bringing all beings to awakening; widespread in East Asia; in Vietnam it is often called the Northern school.
  • Vajrayāna Buddhism (Sanskrit: Vajrayāna - Diamond Vehicle): the tantric Buddhist tradition of the Himalayan region and Mongolia — the tantric strand within Mahāyāna; often called Esoteric Buddhism.
  • Arahant (Pali: arahant, Sanskrit: arhat - liberated one): one fully liberated — the summit of the Theravāda path.
  • Bodhisattva (Pali: bodhisatta, Sanskrit: bodhisattva - enlightenment being): one who vows to bring all beings to awakening together — the ideal of Mahāyāna and the foundation of Vajrayāna.
  • Pāli Canon (Pali: tipiṭaka - Three Baskets): the complete scriptures of Theravāda — Vinaya, Sutta, Abhidhamma; the most intact early corpus still remaining.
  • Chinese Buddhist Canon (English: Chinese Buddhist canon): the Chinese-language canonical collection of East Asian Mahāyāna — translated mainly from Sanskrit; including the Āgama collections (corresponding to the four Nikāyas).
  • Kangyur (Tibetan: bka' 'gyur - Translation of the Word) / Tengyur (Tibetan: bstan 'gyur - Translation of the Treatises): the two Tibetan-language canons of Vajrayāna — "words taught by the Buddha" and "commentarial treatises".
  • Jātaka (Pali - birth stories): the collection of birth stories — evidence that the bodhisattva ideal is already present in the Pali corpus.
  • Vesak (Pali: Vesākha - Vesak): the Buddha Day festival of Theravāda — the full-moon day of the month of Vesākha (around April–May of the solar calendar).
  • Losar (Tibetan: lo gsar - Tibetan New Year): the Tibetan New Year — the greatest festival of the Vajrayāna festival cycle; Saga Dawa — the month commemorating the enlightenment in the Tibetan calendar.
  • Ullambana (Sanskrit: Ullambana - Ullambana festival): the East Asian festival of filial devotion — lunar 15/7.
  • Four Noble Truths (Pali: cattāri ariyasaccāni - Four Noble Truths): suffering – origin – cessation – path; the shared doctrinal foundation of all traditions.
  • Noble Eightfold Path (Pali: aṭṭhaṅgika magga, Sanskrit: āṣṭāṅga mārga - Noble Eightfold Path): the eightfold path — gathered into virtue – concentration – wisdom.
  • Vinaya (Sanskrit: Vinaya - monastic discipline): the collection regulating monastic life — of shared early origin in all three traditions.
  • Saṅgha (Pali: saṅgha - community): the practising community — one of the Three Jewels in all three traditions.
  • Nirvana (Pali: nibbāna, Sanskrit: nirvāṇa - nirvana): the extinguishing of the fires of greed – hatred – delusion — the shared goal; traditions express it differently.
  • Empowerment (Sanskrit: abhiṣeka - empowerment): the initiatory rite conferring "authorisation to practise" a tantric method.
  • Mantra (Sanskrit: mantra - mantra) / Maṇḍala (Sanskrit: maṇḍala - mandala) / Tutelary deity (Sanskrit: iṣṭadevatā, Tibetan: yidam - tutelary deity): the trio of tantric means — sacred syllables, sacred cosmological diagrams, the central visualised figure.
  • Lama (Tibetan: bla ma - guru): a spiritual teacher with a lineage — the characteristic guide of Vajrayāna.
  • Samatha (Pali: samatha - tranquillity meditation) / Vipassanā (Pali: vipassanā - insight meditation): the two branches of Theravāda meditation — calming the mind; seeing reality clearly.
  • Alms-round (Pali: piṇḍapāta - alms-round): the practice of monastics receiving food in the bowl each morning; Rains retreat (Pali: vassa - rains retreat): three months of the rainy season devoted to intensive practice.
  • Kinh Theravāda / Khmer Theravāda: the two Theravāda streams in Vietnam — ethnic Vietnamese from the early 20th century; the Khmer community of the southwest.
  • Trúc Lâm: the meditation lineage founded by King Trần Nhân Tông (13th century) — a purely Vietnamese Zen lineage.
