G.A2. How to Start Learning

Article G.A1 answered "What is Buddhism?" 1. A learning map — where a beginner should start

Beginners often ask first, "Which scripture should I read first?" — but the better question is: What should I understand first? The teaching tradition and the experience of many learners can be summarized as a five-step path, with each step laying the foundation for the next:

  • Step 1 — Understand the basic concepts: what Buddhism is, who the Buddha was, and how Buddhist teachings view life — the introductory layer (group G.A, beginning with article G.A1).
  • Step 2 — Hear the Buddha's story: studying the teachings before knowing the person who revealed them can feel abstract; learning through the story of his life gives the teachings context (group G.B).
  • Step 3 — Learn the foundational teachings: the Four Noble Truths, Noble Eightfold Path, dependent origination, and karma — the shared vocabulary of Buddhist traditions (group G.C).
  • Step 4 — Explore the tradition closest to you: how the three major traditions develop the foundational teachings; for Vietnamese readers this is often the Mahāyāna tradition around them — but learning about all three gives you a sounder basis for understanding the differences (the map of the three traditions is in group G.B, with details in groups G.D/G.E/G.F).
  • Step 5 — Combine study with practice: learning is one leg, practice is the other — meditation, mindfulness, and ethical training; the app has a dedicated Meditation section with a progression from the beginner level.

Two important notes about this path. First, you do not have to follow it in strict order — someone who is still exploring can read several introductory articles first, while someone eager to practice can enter the Meditation section immediately and return to theory later; the path simply shows a natural sequence that helps prevent gaps in the foundation. Second, starting with the foundational teachings (group G.C) is the safest route — because this is the part shared by all three traditions: once you understand it, you can read most articles in any tradition without having to choose a side first.

3. How to read scriptures and sources — five source layers

When searching online, the same Buddhist statement may be explained differently in different places — not necessarily because someone is lying, but because sources belong to different layers. Knowing how to distinguish the layers is a foundational skill for a learner — a very practical skill for avoiding misinformation. The five layers below are arranged from closest to farthest from the earliest teachings:

  • Layer 1 — Scriptures: discourses that record teachings (the Sutta Piṭaka and Vinaya Piṭaka — while Abhidhamma belongs to a separate layer discussed below). This is the "base" layer — later explanations should be checked against it.
  • Layer 2 — Commentaries: works by ancient masters explaining individual words and discourses (for example, the major commentarial tradition of Theravāda Buddhism). Commentaries are important, but they are interpretations, not the scriptures themselves — they may reflect the perspective of the commentator's school.
  • Layer 3 — Treatises: works that systematize the teachings into the philosophical framework of a particular school (for example, Abhidhamma/Abhidharma treatises and doctrinal treatises of different schools). Treatises build systems — very worth studying, but remember that they speak in the voice of that school.
  • Layer 4 — Modern explanatory books: books by modern scholars, meditation teachers, and Dharma teachers — accessible and practical, but often select and re-express material for a particular audience.
  • Layer 5 — Online articles and aggregators: the fastest for quick reference, but often the easiest to get wrong — a quotation may be cut from context, a discourse number copied incorrectly, or different teachings mixed together. Treat layer 5 as a signpost — for confidence, go back to the higher layers and check.

Why can one statement be explained differently? Take "the truth of suffering" as an example: the scriptures list forms and causes of suffering; a commentary analyzes the categories in detail; a school's treatise builds a system of phenomena; a modern book turns it into "acceptance and letting go"; an online article may reduce it to "Buddhism is pessimistic." Each layer adds another layer of interpretation — no layer is automatically "more correct" in every respect; each has its own function, and a learner who knows which layer they are reading knows how to use it. A traditional comparison reminds us that the Dhamma is like a raft for crossing a river — scriptures and treatises are means for getting across; do not cling to the words so tightly that you forget the far shore. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 22 (Alagaddūpama Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism].

Regarding choosing a Vietnamese translation: the source scripture may be in Pali, Sanskrit, Classical Chinese, or Tibetan — Vietnamese readers usually encounter it through translation, so translation quality determines a large part of what they understand. Some criteria: the translation should be from the original source (not translated through a third language), identify the source clearly (canon and discourse number), and provide terminological notes; when possible, compare two translations for an important passage: where two versions agree, that often points to the core meaning; where they differ, that is precisely where careful consideration is needed.

4. When two sources conflict — how to ask the right questions

Conflicts between sources are something you will encounter often — Buddhism has passed through many countries, languages, and schools. The scriptures themselves anticipate this and provide a tool: in the four great references (Pali: mahāpadesa - four great references), a teaching, whoever may have spoken it, should be checked against the discourses and checked against the Vinaya — if it matches, accept it; if it does not, set it aside. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 4.180 (Mahāpadesa Sutta, Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 4.180) - Theravāda Buddhism]. This spirit can be turned into a few practical questions when two sources seem to conflict:

  • What layer does this source belong to? An early scripture, commentary, treatise, modern book, or online article? A difference in source layer is often the most common cause of an apparent "conflict."
  • Which tradition is speaking? The same term, such as "nirvana" or "emptiness", may be expressed differently across traditions — two sources may be speaking in different conceptual languages without actually contradicting one another (the comparison in group G.G will help distinguish them).
  • Which scripture is being cited? A reliable source gives the reference (name and number); when two sources both cite scripture but interpret it differently, return to the scripture itself — read the whole passage, not just one sentence.
  • What does the scripture actually say about this? If the earliest source is silent (for example, about details of a story outside the scripture), then this is an area where traditions develop their own explanations — read them as the views of particular traditions, not as universal doctrine.

Most importantly, conflicts among sources are not a reason to become discouraged — they are part of a living tradition, much like universities contain different schools of thought. The balanced attitude for a learner is to stay open while checking sources — neither believe blindly nor dismiss too quickly (article G.A1 has already introduced this spirit through the Buddha's teaching to the Kālāma people).

5. Levels of commitment — from interest to ordination

Buddhism does not have a single "gate" that you either pass through completely or not at all — the path opens through different levels of commitment, and every level has a legitimate place:

  • Interested reader: has not taken refuge or undertaken precepts — reads, listens, and tries mindfulness. Buddhism does not require "belief before learning" — this is a completely legitimate and very common starting point (many app readers are here).
  • Lay Buddhist who has taken refuge (Pali: gihī, upāsaka/upāsikā - lay follower): performs the threefold refuge — taking refuge in Buddha, Dhamma, and Saṅgha (the ceremony and meaning of each phrase are presented in the next article) — and undertakes the five precepts as an ethical training commitment. The scriptures describe lay followers through qualities such as confidence in awakening, ethical discipline, generosity, and learning. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 8.26 (Jīvaka Sutta, Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 8.26) - Theravāda Buddhism]. This is the most common level — living an ordinary life, raising a family, working, while maintaining the path.
  • Buddhist observing the eight precepts (Pali: aṭṭhasīla - eight precepts): a more intensive level of training, usually observed one day at a time (on Uposatha days or during retreats): additional training concerning food, adornment, entertainment, and sleeping on high or luxurious beds; anyone may undertake it according to their capacity, and each day observed is a day of practice.
  • Ordained monastic: novice monk (Pali: sāmaṇera), bhikkhu (Pali: bhikkhu), and bhikkhunī (Pali: bhikkhunī) — enter full-time monastic life under the Vinaya (Pali: Vinaya), living in a monastic community, observing extensive rules, and not living in the ordinary family pattern. Practical conditions include health, psychological preparation, and a teacher; procedures differ among traditions.

These levels are not a "scoreboard of who is higher" — the scriptures honor both virtuous laypeople and diligent monastics; lay followers can attain high spiritual attainments in the canonical traditions. Which level a person chooses is a personal matter at each stage of life — and it can change with circumstances: many people take refuge for years before considering a deeper commitment.

6. Clearing up a misunderstanding: must learning Buddhism mean vegetarianism or ordination?

Two misunderstandings make many people hesitant to take the first step — they should be cleared up immediately:

  • "Learning Buddhism means you must be vegetarian." This is not true for most people. Vegetarianism is not a requirement for lay Buddhists — none of the five precepts requires it. Vegetarianism is a choice: some East Asian traditions encourage it as an expression of compassion (and monastics in those traditions commonly practice it); the Vinaya of Theravāda Buddhism permits monastics to eat meat under the threefold-purity condition, when the animal was not seen, heard, or suspected to have been killed for them; many lay Buddhists choose vegetarian days (such as the first and fifteenth days of the lunar month) as a practice of compassion. Thus: vegetarianism is a respected practice, not a condition of participation.
  • "Learning Buddhism means you must ordain." Not so. As section 5 showed, most Buddhists throughout history have been laypeople; the scriptures record many lay followers attaining high spiritual results. The monastic community has a distinct role — preserving the teachings and guiding the community — but it is part of the whole, not a compulsory model. Learning Buddhism within family life and work is precisely the path that group G.A3 will present.

A concise way to put both points: Buddhism opens its door "one step at a time, by the steps you choose" — no one has to burn the bridge just to begin, and no one is trapped on one side forever.

7. Summary and next steps

  • The five-step learning path: concepts → the Buddha's story → foundational teachings → a familiar tradition → practice; it does not have to be followed strictly; the foundational teachings (group G.C) are the safest common starting point.
  • The app follows that map: A introduction — B history — C shared teachings — D/E/F the three traditions — G comparison — H reference — plus the Meditation section.
  • Reading skills: distinguish the five source layers (scriptures — commentaries — treatises — modern books — online articles); choose translations from the source language and identify the source; when sources conflict, ask: which layer — which tradition — which scripture — what does the scripture say?
  • Levels of commitment: interested reader → lay refuge → one-day eight-precept observance → ordination; no level forces the next.
  • Vegetarianism and ordination are not requirements for learning Buddhism — the path opens one step at a time through personal choice.

Glossary:

  • Canon (a collection or body of texts): the major scriptural collections; Buddhism has three principal baskets: Sutta, Vinaya, and Abhidhamma (collectively, the Three Baskets).
  • Abhidhamma / Abhidharma (Pali: Abhidhamma, Sanskrit: Abhidharma): the body of analytical texts that organizes teachings into conceptual systems; traditions differ in how this material is structured.
  • Commentaries (Pali: Aṭṭhakathā): works explaining scriptures, composed by ancient masters — the second interpretive layer after the early scriptures.
  • Treatises (Sanskrit: śāstra): works that systematize the teachings according to a school — unlike commentaries, they construct a distinct framework rather than simply explaining individual discourses.
  • Four great references (Pali: mahāpadesa): a scriptural method for checking a teaching — compare it with the discourses and with the Vinaya.
  • Threefold Refuge (Pali: tisaraṇa, Sanskrit: triśaraṇa): taking refuge in Buddha — Dhamma — Saṅgha; the foundational ceremony of becoming a Buddhist (details in the next article).
  • Lay follower (Pali: gihī): a Buddhist living an ordinary lay life; a male is an upāsaka and a female an upāsikā.
  • Five precepts (Pali: pañcasīla): five ethical training commitments for lay followers — refraining from killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, false speech, and intoxicants (details in the next article).
  • Eight precepts (Pali: aṭṭhasīla): a more intensive set of eight training commitments than the five precepts — usually observed one day at a time; "Uposatha" refers to the observance day.
  • Novice monk (Pali: sāmaṇera): an ordained male novice before full bhikkhu ordination; the female equivalent is sāmaṇerī.
  • Bhikkhu / bhikkhunī (Pali: bhikkhu / bhikkhunī): ordained male / female monastics.
  • Vinaya (Pali: Vinaya): the monastic discipline governing the life of the Saṅgha — the foundation of ordained life.
  • Uposatha day: days for observing precepts and reciting discipline according to the lunar calendar (new moon, full moon, etc.).
  • Threefold purity regarding meat: the Theravāda Vinaya condition for monastics eating meat — one did not see, hear, or suspect that the animal was killed specifically for oneself.
  • Raft parable (Pali: kullūpama): the canonical comparison of the teaching to a raft for crossing a river — a means for crossing, not something to carry on one's back forever.
  • Meditation section: the app's practice section (meditation, mindfulness, and methods) — separated from conceptual articles so each area can be developed in depth.
