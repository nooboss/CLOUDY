G.D5.23. Laos: Boun Khao Padab Din — Offering Merit to the Departed
Full moon of the tenth Buddhist month

Boun Khao Padab Din is a distinctly Lao observance held on the full moon of the tenth Buddhist month, near the end of the rains-retreat season. It is an occasion for dedicating merit to the departed: families offer rice and food to monastics, then dedicate the merit to relatives who have died. The shared doctrinal basis for this practice—the dedication of merit—is found in all three major Buddhist traditions and is introduced in G.C4.

1. What does “Khao Padab Din” mean?

The Lao name can be read by its parts:

Reading the Lao name

  • Boun (Lao): a merit-making festival (G.D5.17).
  • Khao (Lao): rice or cooked rice, recalling the central offering of food.
  • Padab din (Lao): in a common folk explanation, “beneath the earth,” associated with ancestors and other deceased relatives imagined as resting in the earth.

The name is therefore often understood in popular usage as a rice offering for those beneath the earth. Its underlying Buddhist practice, however, is giving and dedicating merit (G.C4): the wholesome results of generosity are dedicated to relatives who have died. It is important to distinguish the two levels: dedicating merit is a shared Buddhist practice, while the image of “those beneath the earth” belongs to Lao folk belief and custom.

2. How is the day observed?

Three layers for understanding Boun Khao Padab Din in Laos

  • Foundational meaning: generosity and the dedication of merit to the departed (G.C4), in keeping with scriptural teachings on sharing merit with relatives.
  • Shared practice: families prepare rice, food, and requisites, and offer them to monastics at the temple. During the offering, they may name or remember deceased relatives and dedicate merit to them. Some communities also visit stupas or burial places associated with family members.
  • Custom: the form of naming donors or the departed, local foods, and visits to stupas vary by region. Two weeks later, Boun Khao Salak continues the same theme of dedicating merit (G.D5.24).

Khao Padab Din and Khao Salak are often spoken of together as Laos's late-rains merit-dedication observances. Both are directed toward the departed, although their modes of offering differ (G.D5.24).

3. What newcomers should know

  • This is a quiet observance centred on family and ancestors, not a boisterous festival.
  • Newcomers may join the ordinary temple offering; remembering deceased relatives is a private matter for each family.
  • For the doctrinal basis of dedicating merit, see G.C4.

4. Summary and next steps

  • Boun Khao Padab Din is a Lao observance for dedicating merit to the departed, held on the full moon of the tenth Buddhist month; its shared Buddhist basis is giving and dedicating merit (G.C4).
  • The name carries a folk image of those “beneath the earth”; this customary layer is distinct from the shared Buddhist practice.

Next: G.D5.24 introduces Boun Khao Salak, a lottery-draw offering observance on the full moon of the eleventh Buddhist month that likewise includes dedication of merit to the departed.

Glossary:

  • Boun Khao Padab Din (Lao): a Lao observance for dedicating merit to the departed; folk explanations of its name should be distinguished from its doctrinal basis.
  • Dedication of merit: dedicating the wholesome results of a good action to others, including deceased relatives; a practice shared by the three major Buddhist traditions (G.C4).
  • Boun (Lao): a merit-making festival; introduced in G.D5.
  • Buddhist Era (BE): a calendar era traditionally reckoned from the Buddha's final passing.

Source notes

  • The doctrinal basis of giving and dedicating merit to the departed is presented in G.C4 (merit, fields of merit, and dedication of merit), a practice shared across the three major traditions.
  • The name Boun Khao Padab Din, named offerings, visits to stupas, and folk explanations of the name are described in accounts of Lao Buddhism; the customary and folk layers are distinguished from the foundational teaching.
  • The full moon of the tenth Buddhist month is the standard festival-date notation used in this subsection; it is not converted into a fixed Gregorian date, which varies by year.
