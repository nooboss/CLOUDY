G.D5.5. Vietnam: Beginning the Rains Retreat (Vassa)
Sixteenth day of the sixth lunar month

The rains retreat (Pali: vassa) is not an ordinary single-day festival but a three-month season of practice. Monastics stop travelling and remain in one place for focused meditation and study of the Dhamma. This article concerns its beginning, recorded in Vietnam's calendar notation as the sixteenth day of the sixth lunar month, one day after Dhamma Day (G.D5.4). The season closes with Pavāraṇā (G.D5.6).

1. What is the rains retreat, and where did it come from?

The Vietnamese term an cư means remaining settled in one place; vào hạ, literally “entering the rains,” is the familiar Vietnamese Buddhist expression for beginning the retreat. According to the Vinaya, the rule arose from a practical setting: wandering during the rainy season was difficult, and people complained that monastics travelling through the rain damaged grass and small living beings. The Buddha therefore required monastics to remain in one place for the three months of the rains. Seasonal residence was also customary among other Indian renunciants at the time. [Source: Vinaya Piṭaka, Mahāvagga - Theravāda Buddhism]

It should therefore be understood as a season, not only a day: the opening day begins three months of practice for the monastic community, with lay support and participation. In the Theravāda festival sequence, it is the axis that connects Āsāḷha Pūjā (G.D5.4), Pavāraṇā (G.D5.6), and the kaṭhina robe-offering season (G.D5.7).

2. Why does it begin on the sixteenth day of the sixth lunar month?

The retreat begins on the day after the Āsāḷha full moon. Countries express the date in their own calendars:

The beginning of the rains — date notation in different calendars

  • Thailand: first day of the ninth Buddhist month — Khao Phansa, “entering the rains.”
  • Laos: first day of the ninth Buddhist month — Boun Khao Phansa, with boun meaning a merit-making festival.
  • Cambodia: first day of the ninth Buddhist month — Chol Vossa, “entering the rains.”
  • Vietnam: sixteenth day of the sixth lunar month.

The season is also recorded differently: Thailand, Laos, and Cambodia mark it from the first day of the ninth month to the eleventh-month full moon; this article's Vietnamese notation is the sixteenth day of the sixth lunar month to the fifteenth day of the ninth. These are two labels for the same three-month season, ending at the Pavāraṇā full moon (G.D5.6). Because local calendars do not align perfectly, do not assume Gregorian dates always coincide; check the particular year when needed (G.D5.1).

3. What happens during the three months?

The community follows a distinctive rhythm during these three months:

Three months of the rains retreat — three areas of activity

  • Focused monastic practice: monastics stay at their monastery and increase meditation, study of the scriptures and Vinaya, and instruction. The Vinaya permits an absence of up to seven days (Pali: sattāha) for a legitimate need; staying away longer means the retreat is not completed.
  • Seniority counted by seasons: the number of completed rains retreats measures a monastic's years of standing. Vietnamese Buddhists still speak of how many “rains” a monastic has completed.
  • Lay merit-making and support: laypeople offer support for the monastics' three-month residence, visit to hear the Dhamma more regularly, and may undertake the eight precepts (Pali: aṭṭhasīla) or join retreats (G.D3).

The season can be pictured as the practice year's “term”: it begins with a commitment, continues through three months of focused practice, and closes with Pavāraṇā, when monastics invite one another to speak of what they have seen or heard during the retreat (G.D5.6).

4. The rains retreat in Vietnam

Apply the three-layer approach introduced in G.D5.1—foundational teaching, shared practice, and custom—to Vietnam's rains retreat:

Three layers for understanding the rains retreat in Vietnam

  • Foundational meaning: the Vinaya provision for a three-month residence during the rains; this core is the same across countries.
  • Shared practice: monasteries may open the retreat with chanting and a formal commitment, then hold regular chanting, retreats, and Dhamma talks. Laypeople offer support; monastic rains-retreat life is introduced in G.D3.
  • Custom: “entering the rains” is a familiar expression in Vietnamese Buddhism, and Vietnamese Mahāyāna communities also observe a rains retreat, often using the same lunar date. For Khmer Theravāda communities in the Mekong Delta, Chol Vossa opens the year's major festival sequence (G.D4). This is a cultural and social layer, not a compulsory lay requirement.

The distinction prevents confusion: the Vinaya requirement applies to monastics; laypeople participate according to their means through generosity, Dhamma listening, precepts, and practical support for the monastery.

5. What newcomers should know

  • This is a good time to begin a small, sustainable commitment: hear the Dhamma regularly, keep the precepts more carefully, or meditate daily for three months.
  • If a nearby monastery holds an opening service, attend as you would any temple service: arrive early, sit quietly, and join your palms when monastics enter.
  • Offering support during the retreat is encouraged but voluntary.
  • Do not ask at length how many rains a monastic has completed unless you know them well; newcomers only need to understand that it is a conventional measure of seniority.
  • For the spirit of learning without anxiety about mistakes, see G.E6.

6. Summary and next steps

  • The rains retreat, or vassa, is a three-month rainy-season residence for focused monastic practice, established in the Vinaya. It is a season of community practice, not merely a festival day.
  • In Vietnam it begins on the sixteenth day of the sixth lunar month; Thailand, Laos, and Cambodia record the same occasion as the first day of the ninth Buddhist month. It ends at the Pavāraṇā full moon.
  • Completed rains retreats measure monastic seniority; laypeople participate through offerings, Dhamma listening, and precepts according to their means.

Next: G.D5.6 introduces Pavāraṇā, the Invitation Ceremony that ends the rains retreat on the fifteenth day of the ninth lunar month in this Vietnamese notation. The kaṭhina robe-offering season follows (G.D5.7).

Glossary:

  • Rains retreat (Pali: vassa): the three-month rainy-season residence for focused monastic practice; it begins after Āsāḷha Pūjā and ends at Pavāraṇā.
  • Entering the rains: Vietnam's expression for beginning the rains retreat; “leaving the rains” means its end.
  • Theravāda Buddhism (Pali: Theravāda): a Buddhist tradition whose scriptures are preserved chiefly in Pali; in Vietnam it includes Vietnamese and Khmer Theravāda communities (G.D4).
  • Wandering: the early monastic practice of travelling from place to place to teach and collect alms, temporarily suspended during the rains retreat.
  • Sattāha (Pali): a Vinaya allowance of up to seven days away from the monastery during the rains retreat for a legitimate need.
  • Pavāraṇā: the Invitation Ceremony at the final full moon, when monastics invite others to speak of faults seen, heard, or suspected (G.D5.6).
  • Number of rains retreats: a conventional measure of monastic seniority based on completed retreats.
  • Eight precepts (Pali: aṭṭhasīla): eight training rules observed for one day and night (G.D3).
  • Khao Phansa (Thai): “entering the rains”; Laos calls the day Boun Khao Phansa and Cambodia Chol Vossa.
  • Kaṭhina (Pali): the robe-offering ceremony and one-month season after the rains retreat, available only to monastics who completed the retreat (G.D5.7).
  • Uposatha (Pali): a lunar observance day associated with monastic discipline and lay practice (G.D3).

Source notes

  • The origin of the rains-retreat rule, seasonal residence among other renunciants, and the seven-day allowance are found in the Vinaya Piṭaka, Mahāvagga - Theravāda Buddhism.
  • Monastic seniority counted by completed rains retreats is a Vinaya convention, presented here at an introductory level.
  • The country-specific calendar notations for the beginning and end of the retreat are not converted into a fixed Gregorian date, which varies by year.
  • The local names Khao Phansa, Boun Khao Phansa, and Chol Vossa, and customary practices surrounding the retreat, describe Thai, Lao, and Cambodian Buddhism; they designate the same Pali-rooted “entering the rains” marker.
  • The Vietnamese Mahāyāna rains retreat is a shared Vietnamese practice and is distinguished here as a customary and practical layer, not a Theravāda-specific doctrine.
