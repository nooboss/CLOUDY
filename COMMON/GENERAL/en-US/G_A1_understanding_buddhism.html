G.A1. Understanding Buddhism

This is the opening article in the "Buddhism Basics" section, written both for people who know nothing about Buddhism and for those who want to reorganize their foundational knowledge systematically. The six themes below answer the most basic questions almost every beginner carries: What is Buddhism?, What are the Three Jewels — Buddha, Dhamma, and Saṅgha?, How does Buddhism differ from religions that believe in a creator god?, What are the common misunderstandings?, and How should Buddhist terms in Pali, Sanskrit, and Sino-Vietnamese be read? By the end, you will have a sufficiently solid overall picture to move on to the next articles.

1. What is Buddhism?

Many people imagine Buddhism as the worship of a deity named Buddha. The first misunderstanding — and the most fundamental one — begins with the word "Buddha" itself.

1.1. "Buddha" means "the awakened one"

Buddha (Pali: buddha - the awakened one) is not the personal name of a deity, but a title: "the awakened one" — one who has awakened from ignorance (not clearly seeing the nature of things - Pali: avijjā - ignorance) and sees life as it truly is. Just as "professor" is a title for a highly qualified teacher, "Buddha" is a title for one who has attained complete awakening.

When capitalized as the Buddha, the term usually refers to Śākyamuni Buddha (Pali: Sakyamuni Buddha) — the teacher who lived in India more than 2,500 years ago, discovered the path to the ending of suffering for himself, and passed that path on to later generations. But he was not the only Buddha: according to Buddhist teachings, there have been Buddhas in the past and there will be Buddhas in the future (article G.C7).

1.2. A path of education and practice for ending suffering

In brief: Buddhism is a path of education and practice for ending suffering, taught by Śākyamuni Buddha. Its starting point is not a dogma, but a very practical observation: life contains suffering (Pali: dukkha - unsatisfactoriness) — birth, aging, illness, and death; loving and then being separated; wanting something and not obtaining it. Rather than simply lamenting, the Buddha looked at suffering as a doctor looks at an illness: diagnose the illness (suffering), find its cause (craving — Pali: taṇhā - craving), establish that the illness can be cured (suffering can cease), and prescribe the treatment (the path of practice). These four steps are the Four Noble Truths (Pali: cattāri ariyasaccāni - Four Noble Truths) — the central framework shared across Buddhist traditions (article G.C1).

Therefore, the heart of Buddhism is not about believing certain propositions, but about practicing to transform oneself: living ethically (virtue), cultivating a steady mind (concentration), and developing clear insight (wisdom) — these three together form the three trainings (Pali: tisso sikkhā - three trainings) (article G.C6). A short verse summarizes this spirit: "Avoid all evil, cultivate the good, and purify the mind — this is the teaching of the Buddhas." [Source: Dhammapada, verse 183 - Theravāda Buddhism].

1.3. What Buddhism has — and does not have

Buddhism has:

  • A body of teachings about the origins of suffering and the path to liberation, transmitted and developed for more than 2,500 years.
  • Concrete practices: ethical training, meditation, and cultivating compassion — not rituals alone.
  • A community consisting of the ordained Saṅgha and lay followers, today expressed through three major traditions: Theravāda Buddhism in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia; Mahāyāna Buddhism (Sanskrit: Mahāyāna) in East Asia, including Vietnam; and Vajrayāna Buddhism (Sanskrit: Vajrayāna) in the Himalayan region and Mongolia.
  • Art and culture: temples, Buddha images, scriptures, and festivals — a vast human heritage.

Buddhism does not have:

  • An omnipotent creator who creates everything and determines human fortune and misfortune — discussed more fully in section 3.
  • An immutable doctrinal book: the Buddha encouraged people to test teachings through experience rather than accept them simply because "the scripture says so."
  • Earthly punishment imposed by a deity: there is no "hell for people who do not believe"; results operate according to the causal law of karma (section 3.2).

2. The Three Jewels — Buddha, Dhamma, and Saṅgha

Three Jewels (Pali: ratanattaya, Sanskrit: triratna - Three Jewels) is the collective name for Buddha — Dhamma — Saṅgha, the three common refuges of Buddhist traditions. A person is traditionally regarded as entering Buddhism when they take refuge (Pali: saraṇa - refuge) in the Three Jewels.

2.1. Buddha — the teacher who has walked the path

Among the Three Jewels, the Buddha is the awakened one — someone who has walked the path and returned to point it out to others. Buddhists bow to the Buddha first of all to express reverence for this supreme teacher, much as standing before a photograph of a loved one or a statue of a hero helps us remember and emulate that person — not to ask for a supernatural power to grant wishes.

2.2. Dhamma — the teaching that guides the way

Dhamma (Pali: dhamma - the teaching) is what the Buddha taught: the causes of suffering, the path to its ending, and the truth of life. Dhamma is often compared to a map: someone who went before has drawn the route, while those who follow must walk it step by step. (In other contexts, "dhamma" can broadly mean "phenomena"; in the Three Jewels, however, Dhamma refers to the teaching directed toward liberation.)

2.3. Saṅgha — the community that walks alongside us

Saṅgha, or Saṅgha (Pali: saṅgha - community), in its original sense means the community of people who practice and learn together — not only monks and nuns. This community consists of the fourfold assembly (Pali: catasso parisā - fourfold assembly): bhikkhus (Pali: bhikkhu — ordained men), bhikkhunīs (Pali: bhikkhunī — ordained women), upāsakas (Pali: upāsaka — male lay followers), and upāsikās (Pali: upāsikā — female lay followers). Thinking of "Saṅgha" only as monks is the narrower usage; in the act of taking refuge, taking refuge in the Saṅgha means relying on the wider community of sincere practitioners.

2.4. Why are they called "Jewels"?

For a Buddhist practitioner, these are the three most precious supports on the journey: the teacher who knows the way, the path itself, and the companions who walk with us. Imagine someone lost in a forest at night: what they need is not a prayer, but someone who has escaped the forest (Buddha), a map (Dhamma), and a group walking alongside them (Saṅgha). The earliest refuge formula — "Buddhaṃ saraṇaṃ gacchāmi" (I go to the Buddha for refuge) — is recorded from the early Vinaya accounts, when the Buddha's first five companions asked to go for refuge. [Source: Vinaya Piṭaka, Mahāvagga - Theravāda Buddhism]. For the refuge ceremony in contemporary lay life, see article G.A3.

3. Buddhism and religions that believe in a creator god

Buddhism is commonly grouped under "religion", yet it differs from creator-centered religions in several fundamental ways.

3.1. No omnipotent creator

Buddhism has no concept of a supreme being who created the universe and arranged human destiny. In response to the question "Where do things come from?", Buddhism presents dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda - dependent origination): phenomena arise and continue in dependence on conditions — "when this exists, that comes to be; when this does not exist, that does not come to be" — like a seed sprouting only when there is sufficient soil, water, and light, rather than because a single creator's hand decided it would happen (article G.C3).

In the scriptures, the Buddha rejected three influential views of his time in turn: that everything is created and determined by a deity; that everything is predetermined by past karma; and that everything arises without cause or condition. All three can lead people to believe that nothing can be changed — contrary to the path of practice itself. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 3.61 (Titthāyatana Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism].

3.2. Karma is a law of causation, not reward and punishment

Karma (Pali: kamma - intentional action) is a causal law: wholesome actions lead to wholesome results, and unwholesome actions to unwholesome results. This law operates naturally — there is no "judge" behind it handing out blessings or punishments. Unlike fate, karma does not lock the future in place: every thought, word, and deed in the present continues to shape the rest of one's life. Present intention is each person's greatest "share of freedom."

The Buddha also rejected the idea that "everything that happens is caused by old karma": bodily feelings may arise from illness, weather, or lack of care — not everything is "karmic retribution". [Source: Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 36.21 (Sīvaka Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]. For more on karma and practical questions — why good people can still meet bad circumstances, and whether karma can be calculated — see article G.C4.

3.3. Devas: part of the Buddhist cosmology, but not supreme beings

Buddhist cosmology includes heavenly realms and devas (Pali: deva - celestial beings) — beings with greater merit and longer lifespans than humans, yet still within saṃsāra (Pali: saṃsāra - cycle of rebirth) — not liberated and still subject to ignorance. Therefore, even devas are not the ultimate objects of refuge. The scriptures tell that after his awakening, Brahmā (Pali: Brahmā — a deity regarded as supreme in some Indian religious traditions of the time) came to invite the Buddha to teach the world: in this image, a deity honors the awakened one — not the other way around. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 26 (Ariyapariyesanā Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism].

This helps explain why a Buddhist's attitude when bowing before the Buddha is one of reverence for the teacher and recollection of the teaching. Folk prayer rituals found around many temples are layers of custom that have blended into everyday life; they are not the doctrinal core — the principle of distinguishing between these "layers of sources" will recur throughout this section.

3.4. So, is Buddhism a religion or a philosophy?

The most precise description may be: a path of education and practice. From a philosophical perspective, Buddhism is strongly empirical in spirit: it analyzes mind, analyzes reality, and invites verification — the Buddha advised the people of the Kālāma community not to accept something merely because of tradition, custom, or scripture, but to investigate for themselves and accept what they see to be wholesome and beneficial. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 3.65 (Kālāma Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]. From the perspective of lived religion, Buddhism has all the familiar features of a religion: faith, ritual, community, and festivals. Both perspectives are valid, because Buddhism does not force a choice between "believing" and "understanding" — confidence is the beginning of the path, while wisdom is what later confirms it (article G.A3 discusses confidence and wisdom separately).

And one well-known teaching closes this section — the Buddha's instruction to his disciples before his final passing: be your own refuge, take the Dhamma as your refuge, and rely on nothing else. [Source: Dīgha Nikāya, DN 16 (Mahāparinibbāna Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism].

4. Common misunderstandings about Buddhism

Almost every beginner encounters at least one of the following statements — sometimes from family, sometimes from social media. Each may sound plausible, yet each departs from the foundational teachings.

  • "Buddhism is pessimistic; it only talks about suffering." The opposite is the case: suffering is only the first step in a four-part framework, and the third already affirms that "suffering can cease." Talking about suffering is like a doctor naming the illness clearly — so that it can be treated, not so that we can complain about it. Buddhist scriptures devote many passages to present well-being and the complete peace of nirvana (Pali: nibbāna - nirvana) — the extinguishing of greed, hatred, and delusion (articles G.C1, G.C7).
  • "Worshipping a Buddha image is idolatry." A Buddha image can function like a photograph of a loved one on a table: it turns the mind toward the person, helping us remember the teacher's appearance and teachings. A person who bows to the image does not have to believe that a deity resides inside it; the reverence is directed toward what the image represents.
  • "Going to the temple is just about asking for prosperity and safety." Prayer-for-safety customs have naturally blended into temple life in many places, but according to the foundational teachings, merit is generated by one's own actions (article G.C4). In its Buddhist sense, bowing before the Buddha expresses reverence and recollection of the teaching; for practical lay life, see article G.A3.
  • "Everything is due to karma, so there is no point in trying." This reverses the teaching: karma is action, not a sentence. Because the future is shaped by present actions, the present moment is each person's greatest "share of freedom" — see section 3.2 and article G.C4.
  • "Practice means escaping life and abandoning the family." Buddhism has two broad forms of life: full-time monastic practice and lay practice within ordinary life — most Buddhists in the world are laypeople with families, work, and responsibilities (article G.A3). Ordination is a choice to devote one's life fully to practice, learning, and service, not an escape.
  • "You must be vegetarian to be a Buddhist." Theravāda Buddhism does not require vegetarianism: monks receiving alms accept what is offered, and the texts apply the condition of "threefold-pure meat" (Pali: threefold-pure meat) — meat that one did not see, hear, or suspect was obtained by killing an animal specifically for oneself. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 55 (Jīvaka Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 55) - Theravāda Buddhism]. Mahāyāna Buddhism encourages vegetarian practice out of compassion, but vegetarianism is a practice — not a condition for being Buddhist (article G.E6).
  • "Buddha-recitation is a spell that guarantees protection." Buddha-recitation (recollecting and directing the mind toward the Buddha - buddha-recitation) is, at its root, a mind-training practice: keeping the mind from scattering, cultivating confidence, and nurturing wholesome aspirations. No scripture promises that "recitation guarantees safety unconditionally"; the practitioner must still live ethically and act well like everyone else (article G.E4).

5. Reading Buddhist terminology: Pali, Sanskrit, and Sino-Vietnamese

Open a scripture or listen to a Dharma talk, and beginners are often overwhelmed by three "kinds of words": Satipaṭṭhāna, bodhisattva, nibbāna, dependent origination... In fact, these are simply three language layers of one body of Buddhist teachings. Once the principle is understood, reading becomes much easier.

5.1. Why one concept has many names

The same concept often appears in several forms: kamma / karma (karma), nibbāna / nirvāṇa (nirvana). These are not two different concepts — they are the same word in two languages, like different languages having different words for "computer". The three main language layers are:

  • Pali: the language closest to the oral transmission of the Buddha's era, used by Theravāda Buddhism for its scriptural canon.
  • Sanskrit: the classical scholarly language of India; Mahāyāna Buddhism and Vajrayāna Buddhism use Sanskrit extensively in their scriptural traditions.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: when Buddhism spread to China, scriptures were translated into Chinese characters; Vietnamese readers pronounce many of those Chinese Buddhist terms in Vietnamese, producing familiar words such as nirvana, right view, bodhisattva, and bhikkhu.

Because the dominant Buddhist stream in Vietnam historically came through China, Sino-Vietnamese terminology is especially familiar to Vietnamese readers, while many widely used Theravāda texts retain their Pali terminology. Thus, the same practice can appear in two different "sets of clothing" in two sources — while the underlying teaching remains the same.

5.2. Scriptural languages of the three traditions

  • Theravāda Buddhism: scriptures in Pali form the Three Baskets (Pali: tipiṭaka - Three Baskets), consisting of Vinaya, Sutta, and Abhidhamma; within the Sutta Piṭaka are the major Nikāyas (Pali: Nikāya - collection of discourses) — collections of discourses arranged by length or theme (article G.D2).
  • Mahāyāna Buddhism: scriptures were composed in Sanskrit, with much of the surviving transmission taking place through Chinese and Tibetan translations (article G.E3).
  • Vajrayāna Buddhism: Sanskrit scriptures were translated into Tibetan, including tantric scriptures (tantra) (article G.F3).

5.3. Reading diacritics in Pali and Sanskrit

Words such as saṅgha and Vesākha contain diacritics — marks that indicate different pronunciations and sometimes distinguish words with different meanings:

  • Macrons over vowels (ā, ī, ū): the vowel is long and pronounced for a longer duration — a/ā, i/ī, and u/ū are distinct vowel pairs.
  • Dots and other marks: ṃ closes the syllable somewhat like a final "m"; ñ is roughly like "ny"; ṅ like "ng"; ṭ, ḍ, ṇ, and ḷ are t, d, n, and l sounds pronounced with the tongue curled back.
  • ś and ṣ: pronounced roughly like the English "sh".

You do not need to master this to begin — terms are always given with their English meaning and the original form beside them; diacritics mainly help when you need to look up a word accurately.

6. Summary and next steps

  • Buddha means the awakened one — a title, not the name of a deity; Buddhism is a path of education and practice for ending suffering taught by Śākyamuni Buddha, centered on the Four Noble Truths and the three trainings of virtue, concentration, and wisdom.
  • The Three Jewels — Buddha (teacher), Dhamma (teaching), Saṅgha (community) — are the common refuges of Buddhist traditions; in its original sense, "Saṅgha" includes the fourfold assembly of monastics and lay followers, not only monks.
  • Buddhism does not have a creator god; karma is causal law rather than reward and punishment; devas appear in Buddhist cosmology but are not supreme beings. Buddhism has both philosophical and religious dimensions — its core is a path of personal practice and verification.
  • Common misunderstandings — pessimism, idolatry, praying for prosperity, giving up because of karma, treating practice as escape from life, requiring vegetarianism, and treating Buddha-recitation as a magical charm — are all distant from the foundational teachings; each has a clear response.
  • Pali, Sanskrit, and Sino-Vietnamese terminology are three language layers of one body of Buddhist teachings; reading the English meaning alongside the original is enough to begin.

Glossary:

  • Śākyamuni Buddha (Sanskrit: Śākyamuni): the teacher traditionally regarded as the founder of Buddhism, who lived in India more than 2,500 years ago.
  • Ignorance (Pali: avijjā): not clearly seeing the nature of things; the "sleeping" state from which the Buddha — the awakened one — awakens.
  • Three Jewels (Pali: ratanattaya): Buddha – Dhamma – Saṅgha, the common refuges of Buddhist traditions.
  • Saṅgha (Pali: saṅgha): the community of practitioners; in the broad sense, the fourfold assembly — bhikkhus (bhikkhu, ordained men), bhikkhunīs (bhikkhunī, ordained women), upāsakas, and upāsikās (upāsikā, lay followers).
  • Dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda): the principle "when this exists, that exists" — phenomena arise in dependence on conditions.
  • Karma (Pali: kamma): intentional action of body, speech, and mind together with its results; it operates as causal law, not as a reward-and-punishment system administered by any being.
  • Saṃsāra (Pali: saṃsāra): the ongoing cycle of birth and death through different lives and realms.
  • Devas (Pali: deva): celestial beings with great merit, yet still within saṃsāra and not liberated.
  • Four Noble Truths (Pali: cattāri ariyasaccāni): suffering – its origin – its cessation – the path; the central framework of Buddhist teachings.
  • Three trainings (Pali: tisso sikkhā): virtue – concentration – wisdom, the threefold practice program of the Buddhist path.
  • Nirvana (Pali: nibbāna): the complete ending of suffering, compared to a fire going out because its fuel has been exhausted; the goal of Buddhist practice.
  • Three Baskets (Pali: tipiṭaka): the Buddhist canon — Vinaya Piṭaka, Sutta Piṭaka, and Abhidhamma Piṭaka.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled according to theme or length.
  • Threefold-pure meat: meat that is accepted when one did not see, hear, or suspect that the animal was killed specifically for oneself.
  • Dhammapada (Pali: Dhammapada): a collection of 423 verses preserving foundational teachings attributed to the Buddha, belonging to the Khuddaka Nikāya.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; many Buddhist terms entered Vietnamese through the Chinese Buddhist canon.
  • Theravāda Buddhism: the tradition that preserves the Pali Canon and is widespread in Sri Lanka, Thailand, Myanmar, Laos, and Cambodia; in Vietnam it is often called the Southern tradition.
  • Mahāyāna Buddhism: the tradition widespread in East Asia — China, Vietnam, Japan, and Korea; in Vietnam it is often called the Northern tradition.
  • Vajrayāna Buddhism: the tradition widespread in Tibet, the Himalayas, Bhutan, and Mongolia; often called Esoteric Buddhism.
