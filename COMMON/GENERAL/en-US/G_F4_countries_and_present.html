G.F4. Countries and the Present Day

The previous article — G.F3 — introduced the scriptural system of Vajrayāna Buddhism (Sanskrit: Vajrayāna). This article moves to the present-day landscape, in two parts: Tibet and the exile community — the historical centre and the picture today; and other countries and regions: Bhutan, Mongolia, and Nepal, communities in Russia, and Western Vajrayāna. One important note before reading: the 20th-century history of Tibet involves sensitive political questions, on which sources differ and sometimes contradict one another. This app's principle is to present those sources side by side, without taking a side — readers are given the facts and the differing views so they can form their own judgement; points where sources disagree are marked explicitly.

1. Tibet and the exile community

1.1. From 7th-century unification to the Dalai Lama system

The political starting point is the 7th century, when King Songtsen Gampo (article G.F1) united the tribes into a single empire — passing through a strong imperial phase (7th–9th centuries, coinciding with the first dissemination), then fragmentation after the Langdarma persecution, and religious revival through the second dissemination (10th–11th centuries — article G.F1).

From the 13th century, politics and religion became closely bound through the priest–patron relationship: religious heads received the patronage of political powers, and vice versa. The Mongol Empire patronised the Sakya school; from the 16th–17th centuries, the Gelug school became the central force with the Dalai Lama lineage (article G.F2 — the tulku system): the Fifth Dalai Lama (17th century) unified power across the plateau, and successive Dalai Lamas served as religious leaders and — in certain periods — as political leaders until the 20th century.

1.2. The 20th century — 1959 and the exile community

By the mid-20th century, Tibet had become the focus of a conflict between two sets of forces and two narratives. Following the principle stated at the start of this article, the sources are presented side by side:

  • The basic sequence of events (common to the sources): in 1950, Chinese forces entered eastern Tibet; in 1951, the two sides signed the Seventeen-Point Agreement (an agreement on the status of Tibet within China); in 1959, an uprising in Lhasa was suppressed, and the Fourteenth Dalai Lama — together with about 80,000 people — left Tibet for India, establishing the exile community centred at Dharamsala (a town in Himachal Pradesh — the Tibetan exile community calls it "Little Lhasa").
  • Points where sources disagree: official Chinese accounts describe the events of 1950–51 as a "peaceful liberation" and regard Tibet as long part of China; Tibetan exile sources and many historians describe them as an invasion and cite evidence that Tibet functioned as an independent state before 1950. Even the two uses of the term "Tibet" differ: the "Tibet Autonomous Region" (an administrative unit established by China, smaller in extent) and "Greater Tibet" (the broader cultural–historical region, including Kham and Amdo). Readers need to know both names when consulting sources.

What the exile community accomplished afterward is a remarkable story in its own right: the re-establishment of the great monastic universities and monasteries — the three Gelug monasteries of Lhasa (Drepung, Sera, and Ganden) were rebuilt at the resettlement camps in South India (Karnataka State); schools, libraries, and museums of Tibetan culture were founded; arts, medicine, and scriptures were preserved across a generation away from home — becoming a kind of "cultural insurance" for the whole tradition. In civic life, the exile community organised its own civil institutions; in 2011, the Fourteenth Dalai Lama relinquished his political role, transferring it to democratically elected civil leadership — while retaining spiritual leadership.

1.3. Religious life inside and outside Tibet today

Inside Tibet (under Chinese administration): religious life continues — the great monasteries remain active, devotees still go on pilgrimage and turn prayer wheels (article G.F2) — but under tight management: limits on the number of monks and nuns, controls on teaching content, and supervision of major religious events. The clearest point of disagreement today concerns the tulku system (article G.F2): the recognition of the next Dalai Lama and the Panchen Lama (the second tulku lineage of the Gelug school) involves two different claimants — one following a process organised by the Chinese authorities (including the "Golden Urn" mechanism discussed in article G.F2), and one following a process announced by the Fourteenth Dalai Lama and the religious traditions; the specific cases remain contested to this day. This app does not adjudicate here — the purpose of this section is to help readers follow sources from any side with an understanding of the context.

Outside Tibet: the exile community (in India, Nepal, Bhutan, and Western countries) continues a full monastic and study life; and a larger development has opened up: through the travels of the Fourteenth Dalai Lama and other lamas, Tibetan Buddhism has become a familiar face of global Buddhism — the Fourteenth Dalai Lama received the Nobel Peace Prize (1989), initiated Science–Buddhism dialogue forums (such as Mind & Life — a dialogue between scientists and meditation masters, from 1987), and brought themes of compassion, meditation, and the emotions into academic and public forums. This is the path by which Himalayan compassion and wisdom have entered the lives of people who have never been to Tibet (article G.B6 — modern Buddhism in the world).

2. Vajrayāna in Bhutan, Mongolia, Nepal, and elsewhere

2.1. Bhutan — the "Land of the Thunder Dragon"

Bhutan — a small kingdom in the Himalayas — is the only country to take Vajrayāna as its state religion: the Drukpa Kagyu school (Tibetan: 'brug pa bka' brgyud, the Dragon school — the thunder dragon is the symbol of the country — a Kagyu branch transmitted in the 12th–13th centuries). In the 17th century, Shabdrung Ngawang Namgyal unified the country under a religio-administrative organisation, establishing the dzong system (Tibetan: rdzong, fortress monastery — a fortress that is at once monastery and administrative centre) — an architecture still characteristic of Bhutan today. Buddhism here is closely associated with Padmasambhava (Guru Rinpoche — article G.F1): Taktsang ("Tiger's Nest" — the famous cliffside monastery) is the central sacred site. Bhutan is also known for its Gross National Happiness index — a development measure that takes happiness and culture as its standard, inspired by the country's Buddhist values.

2.2. Mongolia — two waves of rise and decline

Mongolia received Buddhism in two waves: in the 13th century, through the relationship between King Kublai and the Sakya master Phagpa; and again strongly from the 16th century, with the Gelug school becoming the dominant lineage and the Bogd Khan tulku lineage at its religious head. In the 20th century, the Soviet period brought severe destruction: in the 1930s, thousands of monasteries were destroyed and monks and nuns were forcibly disrobed or killed. After independence from the Soviet bloc (1990), Buddhism revived strongly: Gandan Monastery in the capital Ulaanbaatar — the largest surviving monastery — resumed activity and became the centre of the revival; a distinctive Mongolian feature is the blending of Buddhist practice with folk customs linked to shamanism.

2.3. Nepal — two streams on the same land

Nepal has two parallel Buddhist streams: (1) Newar Buddhism of the Newar people in the Kathmandu Valley — the only Indian Vajrayāna lineage still living "in place" since Indian Buddhism declined (article G.B5): ritual traditions, Sanskrit scriptures, and the distinctive lay–monastic institutions of the Newar people have continued unbroken for centuries; (2) Himalayan Buddhism in the northern regions — the Sherpa people (of the Mount Everest region), Mustang, and others follow the Tibetan lineages. The two great stupa monuments of Kathmandu — Boudhanath and Swayambhu (article G.E1) — are the central sacred sites where both streams make pilgrimage.

2.4. Communities in Russia

Buddhism reached the regions now in Russia in the 17th–18th centuries, in today's three autonomous republics: Buryatia (Lake Baikal), Tuva (southern Siberia), and Kalmykia — the only region in Europe (on the lower Volga) where Buddhism is a traditional religion — with the Gelug school predominant. All three passed through Soviet-era destruction (1930s) and revived after 1991; today the monasteries are active again and Buddhism is treated by local authorities as part of regional identity.

2.5. Western Vajrayāna

From the 1960s, following the post-1959 dispersal, the first lamas arrived in the West: in 1967, Kagyu Samye Ling Monastery in Scotland was founded — the first Tibetan Buddhist monastery in Europe. Since then, Tibetan meditation centres have spread across America, Europe, and Australia; Western students have both practised and translated scriptures (article G.F3 — the 84000 project); and Tibetan Buddhism — together with Japanese Zen and mindfulness — has become one of the three most familiar "faces" of Buddhism in the West (article G.B6). Today, "global Vajrayāna" means both the Himalayan heartland and the new communities formed around the world.

Glossary:

  • Vajrayāna Buddhism (Sanskrit: Vajrayāna): the esoteric Buddhist tradition of the Himalayan region and Mongolia — an esoteric current within Mahāyāna Buddhism (Sanskrit: Mahāyāna).
  • Priest–patron relationship: the model from the 13th century onward — a political power patronises a religious lineage, which in turn confers legitimacy on that power.
  • Dharamsala: a town in Himachal Pradesh (India) — the centre of the Tibetan exile community, called "Little Lhasa".
  • Seventeen-Point Agreement (1951): the agreement between the Chinese authorities and the Tibetan authorities on the status of Tibet; sources assess differently how voluntary the agreement was.
  • "Tibet Autonomous Region" / "Greater Tibet": two uses of the region's name — the administrative unit established by China (smaller) and the broader cultural–historical region (including Kham and Amdo).
  • Drepung / Sera / Ganden: the three great Gelug monasteries of Lhasa — re-established in South India after 1959.
  • Panchen Lama: the second tulku lineage of the Gelug school — connected with the recognition of the next Dalai Lama; at present there are two different claimants.
  • Mind & Life: a dialogue forum between scientists and meditation masters — initiated in 1987 on the initiative of the Fourteenth Dalai Lama.
  • Drukpa Kagyu (Tibetan: 'brug pa bka' brgyud, the Dragon school): the Kagyu branch that is Bhutan's state religion — the thunder dragon is the symbol of the country.
  • Shabdrung Ngawang Namgyal: the unifier of Bhutan in the 17th century — founder of the religio-administrative organisation and the dzong system.
  • Dzong (Tibetan: rdzong, fortress monastery): a fortress that is at once monastery and administrative centre — the characteristic architecture of Bhutan.
  • Gross National Happiness: Bhutan's development index that takes happiness, culture, and the environment as its standard.
  • Taktsang (Tibetan: stag tshang, Tiger's Nest): the cliffside monastery of Bhutan — the sacred site associated with Padmasambhava (article G.F1).
  • Phagpa (1235–1280): the Sakya master — who formed the priest–patron relationship between the Yuan court (Kublai) and Tibetan–Mongolian Buddhism.
  • Bogd Khan: the tulku lineage at the head of Mongolian Buddhism until the early 20th century.
  • Gandan (Tibetan: dga' ldan, Gandan Monastery): the largest Gelug monastery in Ulaanbaatar — the centre of the revival of Mongolian Buddhism after 1990.
  • Newar: the people of the Kathmandu Valley — preserving an Indian Vajrayāna tradition continuously to this day; Newar Buddhism — that lineage.
  • Sherpa: the people of northern Nepal near Everest — followers of the Tibetan Buddhist lineages.
  • Boudhanath / Swayambhu: the two great stupa monuments at Kathmandu (article G.E1) — the central sacred sites of both Nepalese streams.
  • Buryatia / Tuva / Kalmykia: the three Buddhist autonomous regions of Russia following Gelug Buddhism; Kalmykia — the only region in Europe where Buddhism is a traditional religion.
  • Kagyu Samye Ling: the first Tibetan Buddhist monastery in Europe (Scotland, 1967).
