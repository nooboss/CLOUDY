1. Mahāyāna daily liturgy and ritual

1.1. What is daily liturgy?

Daily liturgy is a regular sequence of chanting and practice. In monasteries it is usually held in the early morning and evening; at home, it is commonly abbreviated for morning or evening use. It is the backbone of East Asian temple life: monastics gather to chant before daily activities, and lay visitors join the same service rather than a separate rite.

1.2. A typical sequence

Arrangements differ between temples, but a Vietnamese Mahāyāna morning service commonly includes the following steps:

  • Offering incense: the officiant offers incense and begins with reverence, using its pure fragrance as a symbol of the mind directed toward goodness.
  • Chanting praise: melodic verses praise the Buddha and establish a shared direction of mind, often including homage to Śākyamuni Buddha, the Original Teacher.
  • Sutra recitation: the centre of the service. Common texts include the Heart Sutra (G.E3) and the Great Compassion Dhāraṇī, a long dhāraṇī associated with Avalokiteśvara and compassion. Other verses and sutras vary by temple and occasion.
  • Repentance: recognizing harmful actions, being sincerely sorry, and deciding to change. It is not self-hatred; it is a way to clear the mind and begin again. East Asian repentance verses express the principle that what arises in the mind can be ended through transforming the mind.
  • Dedication of merit: the service ends by dedicating its wholesome results to all beings, family members, and the departed. See G.C4.

1.3. Newcomers: there is no need to fear mistakes

  • Arrive before the service if possible. If late, enter quietly and do not walk in front of the Buddha shrine.
  • Stand with the congregation. If unsure of a local arrangement, remain still and follow those around you.
  • When others make full prostrations, newcomers may simply bow with palms together; this is fully respectful.
  • Keep palms together at the chest when appropriate, remain quiet during chanting, and silence your phone.
  • No one is judging a newcomer for standing or bowing imperfectly. A service trains the mind; it is not a performance.

The principle from G.A1 applies here: distinguish ritual form from foundational teaching. Bowing, standing, and chanting are means for arousing a wholesome mind; the heart of the practice is what occurs in that mind.

2. Vegetarianism in Buddhism

2.1. Why Mahāyāna encourages vegetarianism

The non-killing precept is shared by all three major Buddhist traditions (G.A3). Mahāyāna takes a further step: because the bodhisattva path is driven by compassion, many Mahāyāna texts encourage refraining from the flesh of any animal, rather than relying on others to kill it. The Laṅkāvatāra Sutra famously rejects the argument that one bears no responsibility simply because one did not do the killing oneself; demand for meat still supports killing.

2.2. Theravāda: not compulsory, and “threefold clean meat”

Theravāda Buddhism does not require vegetarianism. Monastics who live on alms accept what is offered, subject to the rule of threefold clean meat: they have not seen, heard, or suspected that an animal was killed specifically for them. If any of these conditions is present, it must not be accepted. [Source: Jīvaka Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya 55 - Theravāda Buddhism]

These are different applications of the shared value of compassion, not proof that one tradition is right and the other wrong. Above all, “a person is not Buddhist unless vegetarian” is false: refuge in the Three Jewels, not a food choice, defines Buddhist commitment (G.A2).

2.3. Levels in everyday life

  • Permanent vegetarianism: refraining from meat continually. This is common among East Asian monastics and some laypeople.
  • Periodic vegetarian days: eating vegetarian food on specified days. In Vietnam, the first and fifteenth lunar days and festivals such as Vesak and Vu Lan are common choices for beginners.
  • Associated custom: some Vietnamese and Chinese temple traditions avoid the five pungent herbs—garlic, onion, chives, shallots, and asafoetida—because they are said to agitate the mind. This is a customary layer and is not applied uniformly.

2.4. Health and a common misunderstanding

A balanced vegetarian diet may benefit health, but health is a secondary benefit, not the doctrinal aim. The central purpose is compassion and reducing reliance on killing. Choose a sustainable level: beginning with periodic vegetarian days is already meaningful training in compassion.

3. Buddha-recollection in lay life

3.1. What it is—and is not

Buddha-recollection (Sanskrit: buddhānusmṛti) means keeping the mind directed toward the Buddha: by reciting a name, recalling qualities, or quietly remembering inwardly. It is not a magical charm. Each recollection turns the mind toward wakefulness instead of its habitual drift. The Amitābha Sutra describes the ideal as one-pointed, undistracted mind. [Source: Smaller Sukhāvatīvyūha Sutra - Mahāyāna Buddhism]

3.2. Ways to practise

  • Vocal recitation: saying “Namo Amitābha Buddha” aloud, useful for rhythm, groups, or a tired mind.
  • Mental recitation: reciting inwardly without moving the lips, useful when the practice has become familiar.
  • Ten-recitations method: reciting in groups of ten and moving to the next bead. This simple structure helps keep attention from wandering.

Prayer beads (Sanskrit: mālā) commonly have 108 beads, traditionally associated with 108 defilements. One bead accompanies one recitation, combining counting with steady attention (G.C1).

3.3. Buddha-recollection retreats and end-of-life chanting

Laypeople may join a buddha-recollection retreat, often a seven-day period of concentrated recitation, quiet sitting, and temple life. When someone is seriously ill or near death, temples and lay groups may offer end-of-life chanting: they sit and recite beside the person to support a peaceful, unafraid mind. This is a deeply compassionate community service, not a guarantee or a substitute for care, treatment, and ethical living.

3.4. Blessing and memorial services: understanding them well

  • Blessing service: dedicating merit for people who are living—family, illness, or work. Properly understood, it initiates wholesome intention and shares merit; it is not an order for good fortune without changing one's life (G.C4).
  • Memorial service: dedicating merit to those who have died, within the practice of remembering and transferring merit.

Both lose their point if separated from practice. In the Pure Land framework of faith, vows, and practice, confidence and aspiration must lead to wholesome conduct (G.E4).

4. Summary and next steps

  • Daily liturgy commonly moves through incense offering, praise, sutra recitation, repentance, and dedication of merit. Each stage trains the mind; newcomers can participate simply and respectfully.
  • Vegetarianism is strongly encouraged in Mahāyāna as compassion, while Theravāda does not require it under the threefold-clean-meat rule. It is not a condition for being Buddhist.
  • Buddha-recollection is a widespread East Asian mind-training practice: vocal or mental recitation, beads, retreats, end-of-life chanting, and services understood through compassionate practice rather than magical expectation.
  • In all three areas, distinguish forms and customs from teaching: forms vary by temple and country; the shared core is training the mind, compassion, and wholesome living.

Glossary:

  • Mahāyāna Buddhism (Sanskrit: Mahāyāna): the “Great Vehicle” tradition, prominent in East Asia.
  • Daily liturgy: a regular sequence of temple or home chanting and practice.
  • Dhāraṇī (Sanskrit: dhāraṇī): a long formula or chant; the Great Compassion Dhāraṇī is associated with Avalokiteśvara.
  • Repentance: recognizing wrongdoing, feeling sincere regret, and deciding to change.
  • Dedication of merit: dedicating the wholesome results of an action to others, including the departed.
  • Threefold clean meat: meat acceptable to a Theravāda alms-receiving monastic only when they have not seen, heard, or suspected that the animal was killed for them.
  • Five pungent herbs: garlic, onion, chives, shallots, and asafoetida; a customary restriction in some East Asian vegetarian traditions.
  • Buddha-recollection (Sanskrit: buddhānusmṛti): recollecting and directing the mind toward the Buddha.
  • Prayer beads (Sanskrit: mālā): beads used to count recitations.
  • Buddha-recollection retreat: a concentrated period of Buddha-recitation, often seven days.
  • End-of-life chanting: compassionate chanting alongside a seriously ill or dying person to support a peaceful mind.
  • Blessing service: a service dedicating merit for those who are living; memorial service: one dedicating merit for the deceased.

Source notes

  • The sequence of daily liturgy varies by East Asian temple and region; this article presents a common Vietnamese Mahāyāna pattern.
  • The Heart Sutra, Great Compassion Dhāraṇī, and Flower Garland repentance verses are influential sources for the liturgical elements described here.
  • Mahāyāna encouragement of vegetarianism is associated especially with the Laṅkāvatāra Sutra; Theravāda's threefold-clean-meat condition is stated in MN 55.
  • The Amitābha Sutra is a foundational Pure Land text for Buddha-recollection. Bead counts, end-of-life chanting, blessing, and memorial services are customary practices whose local forms vary.
