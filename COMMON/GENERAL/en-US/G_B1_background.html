G.B1. Background

Śākyamuni Buddha did not appear in a vacuum. He was born in northeastern India around the 6th–5th centuries BCE — in a society with ancient religions, lively philosophical debate, and thousands of people already seeking a way to liberation. To understand why his teachings retained many concepts of that age — such as the causal effects of actions and the cycle of rebirth — while radically differing on key points — such as the nature of self and the path of practice — we first need to see the social and religious landscape into which he was born. This article sketches that landscape; the next enters the life of the Buddha himself.

1. Northeastern India — where and when the Buddha appeared

1.1. A land between two civilizations

Around the 6th–5th centuries BCE, northeastern India — roughly the regions that are now Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, along the Ganges (Sanskrit: Gaṅgā) and Gandak (Sanskrit: Gaṇḍakī) rivers — was a meeting point of two civilizations. To the west lay the ancient center of Vedic civilization, where religion and society were tightly organized according to tradition. To the east were tribal republics and small kingdoms that had developed later, with newer lands and societies less bound by the old order. Indian tradition called the central cultural region the Middle Country (Sanskrit: Madhya-deśa); the Buddha's birthplace — Kapilavastu — lay on the northern edge of this region, in the territory of the Śākya clan (Sanskrit: Śākya).

This was also a time of rapid social change: wet-rice agriculture gave rise to trading towns, commerce along the Ganges expanded, and smaller states were gradually absorbed into larger kingdoms. Economic change brought a shift in political power: wealthy merchant groups grew in importance, while warrior rulers — rather than priests — became powerful kings, such as King Bimbisāra of Magadha, whom the Buddha would later meet and converse with. Society was changing, and old questions about the proper order of human life were becoming more urgent than ever.

1.2. An age of great questions

At the time, the region was home to spiritual teachers of many kinds: forest ascetics, ritual specialists who sought favor from the gods, philosophers who publicly debated one another, and lay teachers as well. Buddhist scriptures preserve a memorable picture of the age: King Ajātasattu visited, one after another, the communities of six famous śramaṇa teachers — crowded gatherings where each teacher was presenting his own path. Then, on a full-moon night, the king went to the mango grove of his physician Jīvaka, where the Buddha was staying, and was struck by the complete silence of the Saṅgha. [Source: Dīgha Nikāya, DN 2 (Sāmaññaphala Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]. The scene shows that in the Buddha's time, "choosing a spiritual path" had already become a real life decision — seekers had to choose among diverse methods much as people today choose a school.

Against this background, two major forces shaped the Buddha's life: Brahmanism — the ancient religious tradition from which he inherited much of the language and many concepts — and the śramaṇa movement — a freer movement of spiritual seeking that he joined and eventually moved beyond. The next two sections look at these two forces in turn.

2. Brahmanism and the caste system

2.1. A religion founded on the Vedas

Brahmanism (the ancient Indian religion that preceded Hindu traditions) was the dominant religious tradition in this region, based on the Vedas (Sanskrit: Veda) as its sacred texts. In this tradition, the Vedas were revealed texts (Sanskrit: śruti) — divine speech heard and transmitted by seers (Sanskrit: ṛṣi), possessing absolute authority and not regarded as human compositions. Memorizing and orally transmitting these texts, with precise pronunciation of every syllable, was the most important work of the priestly class.

This religion revolved around two main axes. The first was sacrifice (Sanskrit: yajña): pouring clarified butter into ritual fires and making offerings to Vedic deities for rain, victory, and children. Rituals had to be performed according to the texts and rules; if something was done incorrectly, the sacrifice was considered ineffective — and this is why those who knew the texts and rituals held a privileged position. The second was a view of ultimate reality: behind the many gods was a single absolute reality called Brahman (Sanskrit: brahman) — the source and substance of the whole universe.

2.2. The caste system — a religiously grounded social order

Brahmanism organized society into four varṇas (Sanskrit: varṇa) — a hierarchy regarded not as a royal invention but as divinely ordained, based on the head-to-foot division of the "cosmic being" (Sanskrit: Puruṣa) in a famous hymn of the Rigveda:

  • Brahmins (Sanskrit: brāhmaṇa) — from the cosmic being's mouth: priests, scholars, keepers of sacred texts, and ritual specialists.
  • Kṣatriyas (Sanskrit: kṣatriya) — from the two arms: kings, warriors, rulers, and defenders of the realm.
  • Vaiśyas (Sanskrit: vaiśya) — from the thighs: farmers, merchants, producers, and traders.
  • Śūdras (Sanskrit: śūdra) — from the feet: laborers serving the three groups above.

This order was fully religiously sanctioned: caste was understood as something fixed from birth; being born into a caste meant living — and being reborn — within that status. Marriage and commensality with other castes could be regarded as polluting. At the bottom were groups outside the caste system — people treated as "untouchable." A human life, with all its forms of suffering — aging, illness, death, separation — was framed within the question: "Into which status was I born, and what am I supposed to do within it?"

Yet as society changed, this order was beginning to be unsettled. The eastern regions where the Buddha traveled were largely ruled by Kṣatriya kings, and many clans — including the Buddha's Śākya clan — were organized as oligarchic republics, governed by councils of elders rather than a fixed caste hierarchy. In that very period, the question "Which caste is truly noble?" became a lively topic of debate — and Buddhist texts preserve the Buddha's response in the argumentative language of his time: human beings are born alike; it is conduct, not lineage, that determines who is noble. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 98 (Vāseṭṭha Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism].

3. Upanishadic philosophy — inward inquiry within the tradition

From around the 8th–6th centuries BCE, a stream of thought arose from within Brahmanism that went beyond ritual: the Upanishads (Sanskrit: Upaniṣad), philosophical works concerned with ultimate reality and the human being. Whereas the older Vedic texts were concerned with "how to live rightly in relation to the gods," the Upanishads asked a different question: "Who is the 'I'? What lies in the depths of a human being, and where does it lead?"

The Upanishads introduced two major ideas. First, ultimate reality — Brahman, the source of the universe — was understood to be the deepest essence of the human being: what is called the true self (Sanskrit: ātman). Second, human beings were caught in saṃsāra (Sanskrit: saṃsāra), the cycle of rebirth across lives, and the way out was to realize the true self, observe ethical disciplines, and cultivate meditative absorption (Sanskrit: dhyāna) to directly realize that self. For the first time, the path to liberation was located in the human interior rather than solely in external ritual; this was a major intellectual turning point in Indian civilization.

However, the Upanishads retained the Vedic foundation: this true self was understood as an everlasting essence, present without destruction and underlying all individuals like the ocean beneath its waves. This is exactly the point on which Buddhist thought would take a very different position — as section 5 will discuss.

4. The śramaṇa movement — seekers outside the tradition

4.1. Who were the śramaṇas?

Alongside Brahmanism — associated with the western region, ritual, and the caste system — a major social movement was rising in the northeast: the śramaṇa movement (Pali: samaṇa, Sanskrit: śramaṇa), a movement of spiritual seekers who left ordinary society in search of liberation. Śramaṇas left conventional social life — family, occupation, and caste position — to live as religious renunciants: going on alms rounds, living in forests or parks, or establishing their own communities with disciples. They did not accept the Vedas as the absolute standard; liberation, if possible, had to be achieved through actual practice rather than ritual or lineage.

This was a remarkable social phenomenon: at the time, almost anyone — Brahmin or Kṣatriya, merchant or slave — could become a śramaṇa. The spiritual path was therefore open to everyone — and this was the path the Buddha chose by going forth (in the next article).

4.2. Major schools

Buddhist scriptures preserve the names and teachings of famous śramaṇa teachers of the period — the "six outside teachers" — each offering a different answer to the question: what determines human destiny, and what is liberation? [Source: Dīgha Nikāya, DN 2 (Sāmaññaphala Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]. Three representative orientations were:

  • Fatalism: human beings are completely determined by destiny (Pali: niyati); action has no meaningful effect — practice and wrongdoing alike end in the same result. A representative school was the Ājīvikas, a fatalist ascetic movement led by Makkhali Gosāla.
  • Materialism: the Cārvāka/Lokāyata school held that only the body and material world are real; death is the end, there is no afterlife or karmic causation, and the purpose of life is pleasure.
  • Ascetic purification of karma: many ascetic groups (Sanskrit: tapas) believed karma (Pali: kamma) to be an absolute law, and used self-mortification — lying on thorns, standing on one leg, prolonged fasting — to "work off" old karma in hope of attaining liberation (Sanskrit: mokṣa).

These approaches stood in sharp opposition: one said "everything is predetermined," another "death is the end," another "self-mortification cleanses karma." A person seeking a path in that age had to find a way through this maze of answers — and this confusion is part of the context in which the Buddha's Middle Way (Pali: majjhima paṭipadā) became distinctive (section 5.2).

5. What the Buddha inherited and what he transformed

5.1. What he inherited: karma, rebirth, meditation, and the aspiration for liberation

The Buddha inherited many concepts already present in his time — but what is striking is that he inherited them while repositioning them:

  • Karma: in the Buddha's time, ideas about karma and its results were already widespread among śramaṇa schools, but karma was either treated as an unchangeable destiny or understood through self-mortification to purify it. The Buddha retained causal moral law, but presented it in terms of freedom and responsibility: karma is shaped by intention, and what one does today can change what happens next (article G.C4).
  • Rebirth: both the Upanishadic tradition and many śramaṇa schools accepted the cycle of birth and death. The Buddha accepted this framework — but his explanation of "what is reborn" was very different (section 5.2), making rebirth something that needed to be understood rather than a question with an already accepted answer.
  • Meditation: methods of cultivating the mind to attain deep states of concentration had already been taught by teachers such as Āḷāra Kālāma and Uddaka Rāmaputta before the Buddha continued his own search (next article). He acknowledged these states, but regarded them as steps on the path, not its final goal.
  • The aspiration for liberation: the desire to escape the cycle of suffering shared by śramaṇas and Upanishadic thinkers was the starting point; the Buddha's path was his answer to that aspiration.

5.2. What he transformed: non-self, the Middle Way, and action instead of ritual

The points the Buddha transformed became central to his teaching:

  • Non-self (Pali: anattā): whereas the Upanishads affirmed an everlasting true self (Sanskrit: ātman) within each person, the Buddha analyzed body, feeling, perception, volitional formations, and consciousness and found no fixed essence — everything arises and ceases according to conditions (article G.C5). This was not merely an academic difference; it changed the aim of practice: not "realizing a supreme self," but letting go of attachment to what is not self.
  • The Middle Way: between "following the gods and seeking help through ritual" and "self-mortification and punishing the body," the Buddha opened a third path — training body and mind to an appropriate degree, without attachment to sensual pleasure and without self-mortification. This path was presented in his first discourse, the Turning the Wheel of Dhamma (Pali: Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta), which the next article will recount.
  • Action instead of ritual: unlike the Vedic tradition, which treated sacrificial ritual as the highest means to benefit, the Buddha taught that intentional action — of body, speech, and mind — produces happiness and suffering (article G.C4). A grand ritual cannot lead someone to liberation if the mind still contains hatred and greed; when people of that time asked the Buddha "To whom is an offering most fruitful?", he answered not a Brahmin or any particular lineage, but a person of a purified mind. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 3.57 (Verañjaka Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism].
  • Liberation is possible for everyone: instead of a "true self" as a possession waiting to be recognized, the Buddha's path is a process of transformation — from conduct, to mind, to wisdom — open to anyone regardless of lineage, caste, or past identity (article G.C7).

5.3. One integrated picture

In summary, the Buddha's age was an age of a changing society, a richly established religion being questioned, and an expanding movement of spiritual seekers. The Buddha stood at that intersection: he inherited the era's ideas of karma, rebirth, and meditation, but reversed the way key questions were approached — on caste (conduct, not lineage, determines nobility), on self (there is no fixed essence), and on liberation (not by appealing to gods, not by self-mortification, but by disciplined training). In debate with Brahmins of the time, the Buddha once asked: Which Brahmin is born without a mother and without a father? None. Therefore, all four castes are born in the same human way — they differ only in conduct. [Source: Majjhima Nikāya, MN 93 (Assalāyana Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism]. That answer — simple yet pointed — captures the broader spirit: placing human experience and conduct above tradition and lineage.

6. Summary and next steps

  • The Buddha arose in northeastern India in the 6th–5th centuries BCE — a crossroads of civilizations, a society changing rapidly, and a region alive with competing spiritual paths.
  • Brahmanism was founded on the Vedas and sacrificial ritual and organized society into four divinely sanctioned varṇas; the Upanishads turned inward, developing ideas of the true self and rebirth.
  • The śramaṇa movement opened renunciant practice to a broad range of people and included many contrasting views: fatalism, materialism, and asceticism — requiring seekers to find their own answer to suffering.
  • The Buddha inherited ideas of karma, rebirth, meditation, and the aspiration for liberation; he transformed them through non-self, the Middle Way, action instead of ritual, and a path to liberation open to everyone.

Glossary:

  • Veda (Sanskrit: Veda): the oldest sacred texts of India, regarded as revealed speech (Sanskrit: śruti); the four principal collections are Rigveda, Yajurveda, Samaveda, and Atharvaveda.
  • Brahmanism: the ancient Indian religious tradition founded on the Vedas and sacrificial ritual; a historical predecessor of later Hindu traditions.
  • Brahmin (Sanskrit: brāhmaṇa): the priestly class — ritual specialists and scholars who preserved sacred texts; the term later came to be used more broadly for members of the Vedic religious tradition.
  • Caste system (Sanskrit: varṇa): four social classes — Brahmins, Kṣatriyas, Vaiśyas, and Śūdras — traditionally regarded as divinely ordained.
  • Oligarchic republic: a form of political organization governed by a council of clan elders rather than a single absolute king; the Buddha's Śākya clan followed this model.
  • Upanishads (Sanskrit: Upaniṣad): philosophical works from roughly the 8th–6th centuries BCE concerned with ultimate reality, the true self, and liberation; a later philosophical development within the Vedic tradition.
  • Brahman (Sanskrit: brahman): ultimate reality and source of the universe in Upanishadic philosophy; not to be confused with Brahmā (Sanskrit: Brahmā), a deity associated with the heavenly realms.
  • True self (Sanskrit: ātman): in the Upanishads, the permanent, indestructible essence within a person — a view Buddhism does not accept.
  • Śramaṇa (Pali: samaṇa, Sanskrit: śramaṇa): a renunciant or spiritual seeker who leaves ordinary life in search of liberation; the śramaṇa movement was a free-ranging spiritual movement outside the Vedic tradition in the Buddha's time.
  • Materialism (Sanskrit: Cārvāka/Lokāyata): a materialist school of the period holding that only the body is real, death is the end, and there is no afterlife.
  • Asceticism (Sanskrit: tapas): forms of self-mortification — fasting, lying on thorns, standing on one leg — based on the belief that suffering can purify old karma.
  • Liberation (Sanskrit: mokṣa): the goal of many Indian traditions of the period — release from the cycle of rebirth; in Buddhism, the corresponding goal is nirvana (Pali: nibbāna).
  • Karma (Pali: kamma): intentional action of body, speech, and mind together with its results; it operates as causal law rather than punishment or reward imposed by a being.
  • Saṃsāra (Sanskrit: saṃsāra): the continuing cycle of birth and death across lives and realms.
  • Meditation (Sanskrit: dhyāna): deep states of mental concentration taught by spiritual teachers of the period and acknowledged by the Buddha as steps on the path rather than the final goal; in the Pali Canon, related states are called jhāna.
  • Non-self (Pali: anattā): the teaching that there is no fixed, independent, everlasting "self" within body and mind; a central feature of Buddhist doctrine.
  • Middle Way (Pali: majjhima paṭipadā): the path of balanced training of body and mind — without attachment to sensual pleasure and without self-mortification — presented by the Buddha in his first discourse.
  • Turning the Wheel of Dhamma (Pali: Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta): the image of setting the Dharma wheel in motion; the name of the Buddha's first discourse, recounted in the next article.
  • Dating the Buddha: traditional accounts place the Buddha in the 6th–5th centuries BCE; modern studies generally place his lifetime later, around the late 5th to early 4th centuries BCE — the issue is discussed in "The Buddha's Life."
