G.E1. Mahāyāna — Overview

The articles on Theravāda Buddhism conclude at G.D5. This group introduces the second major Buddhist tradition: Mahāyāna Buddhism, the tradition familiar to many Vietnamese people through temples, liturgy, Buddha-recitation, and observances such as Vu Lan. Its central ideal is awakening together with all sentient beings. Mahāyāna is most deeply rooted in China, Vietnam, Korea, and Japan. This article offers a portrait of the tradition and an approximately two-thousand-year historical outline.

1. Mahāyāna Buddhism — overview

1.1. The name: “Great Vehicle”

Mahāyāna combines the Sanskrit mahā, “great,” and yāna, “vehicle.” The image is of a great vehicle carrying many people together toward awakening. Its central ideal is the bodhisattva (Sanskrit: bodhisattva), who seeks awakening while undertaking to benefit all beings.

This should be understood as a different emphasis within Buddhism, not a ranking of traditions. Theravāda presents the arahant ideal as the culmination of the path; Mahāyāna makes the bodhisattva ideal central. For a comparative map, see G.G1.

Older books sometimes oppose Mahāyāna to “Hīnayāna,” or “Lesser Vehicle.” The latter term has a disparaging history in some early Mahāyāna texts and is not a neutral name. Use the proper names of traditions instead: Theravāda Buddhism, Mahāyāna Buddhism, and Vajrayāna Buddhism.

1.2. Where Mahāyāna is found

Mahāyāna's principal East Asian region is China, Vietnam, Korea, and Japan, together with East Asian Buddhist communities worldwide. It is internally very diverse: Japanese Zen, Chinese Pure Land, and Vietnamese combined Chan–Pure Land practice all have their own histories. Mahāyāna is therefore a broad movement rather than one centrally governed organisation. The country histories are introduced in G.E5.

1.3. Five identifying features

First — an expanded scriptural collection. Mahāyāna retains shared early teachings such as the Four Noble Truths, Noble Eightfold Path, and three trainings, while also recognising sutras such as the Perfection of Wisdom texts, Lotus Sutra, Flower Garland Sutra, and Pure Land sutras. These were largely composed in Sanskrit and translated into Chinese and Tibetan (G.E3). East Asian monastics nevertheless preserve an early-school Vinaya; “Mahāyāna” names an ideal and body of scriptures, not a separate Vinaya. Traditional Mahāyāna accounts and modern historical scholarship explain the origin of these texts differently; both perspectives should be kept distinct.

Second — the bodhisattva ideal. A bodhisattva arouses bodhicitta, the resolve for awakening for the benefit of self and others, and develops the six perfections: generosity, ethical conduct, patience, diligence, meditation, and wisdom. The Pali Jātaka stories already portray the Buddha's former lives on a bodhisattva path; Mahāyāna makes this path a possibility for all (G.E2).

Third — many buddhas and bodhisattvas. The framework of the three bodies of a buddha helps explain why the historical Śākyamuni Buddha is understood as one manifestation of awakening. Mahāyāna temples therefore venerate Amitābha, Bhaiṣajyaguru, and Maitreya, together with bodhisattvas such as Avalokiteśvara, Mañjuśrī, Samantabhadra, and Kṣitigarbha. Each represents a quality to cultivate, including compassion, wisdom, vows, and care for beings in suffering (G.E2).

Fourth — rich ritual and daily life. Morning and evening liturgy, sutra recitation, Buddha-recitation, repentance rites, and annual observances have a large place in East Asian temple life (G.E6, G.E7). Ritual and custom should be distinguished from the tradition's foundational teachings.

Fifth — great diversity. Mahāyāna includes Chan / Zen, Pure Land, Tiantai, Huayan, Faxiang (Yogācāra), Sanlun (Madhyamaka), and Shingon / East Asian Esoteric Buddhism. When teachings appear different, first ask which school and context they belong to (G.E4).

1.4. What “Northern Buddhism” means in Vietnam

In Vietnam, Mahāyāna is often called Northern Buddhism, because it arrived largely through China. The term can mean Mahāyāna broadly, a Chan school, a Pure Land school, the widespread combined Chan–Pure Land practice, or the Vietnamese Trúc Lâm Chan lineage founded by King Trần Nhân Tông. It must not be confused with “Northern School” and “Southern School,” terms for two historical lineages within Chinese Chan.

2. The formation and growth of the Mahāyāna

Early Mahāyāna is reconstructed from Mahāyāna sutras, inscriptions, monastic and translator records, and modern research. Texts rarely date themselves; dates below are therefore approximate, and individual texts formed over time.

2.1. Roots in early Buddhism

Mahāyāna did not emerge from nowhere. Its roots include the Jātaka stories of the Buddha's former bodhisattva lives, early belief in buddhas of past, present, and future, and possibly devotional and lay activity around stupas. These hypotheses need not be mutually exclusive. By the early centuries CE, new sutras had begun to circulate within Indian Buddhism.

2.2. Perfection of Wisdom texts

The Perfection of Wisdom in Eight Thousand Lines is usually placed by textual scholarship around the beginning of the Common Era. It develops emptiness: phenomena lack independent, fixed self-nature and arise dependently (G.C3). It also maps the bodhisattva path. Mahāyāna did not begin as a clean institutional split: followers of these sutras often lived in the same monasteries, under the same Vinaya, as other Buddhists. Inscriptions explicitly using “Mahāyāna” appear only much later, around the fifth to sixth centuries.

2.3. Major philosophical schools and Nālandā

Madhyamaka, associated with Nāgārjuna (c. 150–250), argues from dependent arising to emptiness: emptiness is not nihilism but the absence of independent self-nature. Yogācāra, systematised by Asaṅga and Vasubandhu in the fourth to fifth centuries, analyses experience through layers of consciousness. Buddha-nature thought affirms that no being is excluded from awakening. These streams are explained in G.E2.

From about the fifth century, Nālandā in eastern India became a major monastic university where such teachings were studied and debated. It helped train scholars whose influence reached Tibet and East Asia.

2.4. The movement to China and East Asia

Mahāyāna travelled by the Silk Road and maritime routes, with merchants, monastics, and translators. An Shigao began major translation work at Luoyang in the second century. Kumārajīva (344–413) produced influential Chinese translations of texts including the Lotus and Diamond sutras. Xuanzang (602–664) travelled to India, studied at Nālandā, returned with hundreds of texts, and became a major translator of Yogācāra works.

In China, Buddhist teachings interacted with an established civilisation and East Asian schools gradually formed: Tiantai, Huayan, Chan, Pure Land, and East Asian Esoteric Buddhism. Buddhism then reached Korea, Japan, and Vietnam. Saichō brought Tiantai to Japan, while Kūkai brought Shingon in the early ninth century. See G.E4 and G.E5.

2.5. Vietnam and Mahāyāna today

Buddhism reached Vietnam in the first centuries CE by Indian maritime routes and through China. Under the Lý and Trần dynasties, Mahāyāna flourished; Trúc Lâm Chan became a distinctively Vietnamese lineage. Today, combined Chan–Pure Land practice remains common in Vietnamese Mahāyāna temples. Buddhism declined sharply in India from the twelfth to thirteenth centuries, but Mahāyāna had already taken deep root in East Asia. Vajrayāna developed from Indian esoteric Buddhism and spread primarily to Tibet and the Himalayan regions (G.F).

3. Summary and next steps

  • Mahāyāna means “Great Vehicle”: it centres the aspiration that all beings reach awakening. It should not be opposed to Theravāda through the non-neutral label “Hīnayāna.”
  • Its East Asian centre is China, Vietnam, Korea, and Japan; in Vietnam it is commonly called Northern Buddhism.
  • Its five identifying features are expanded scriptures, the bodhisattva ideal, many buddhas and bodhisattvas, rich ritual life, and exceptional diversity of schools.
  • Its history moves from early roots and Perfection of Wisdom texts, through Madhyamaka and Yogācāra, Nālandā, translation into Chinese, East Asian schools, and local Vietnamese forms such as Trúc Lâm.

Glossary:

  • Mahāyāna Buddhism (Sanskrit: Mahāyāna): “Great Vehicle,” the Buddhist tradition centred on the bodhisattva ideal, prominent in East Asia.
  • Theravāda Buddhism: a Buddhist tradition whose scriptures are preserved chiefly in Pali.
  • Vajrayāna Buddhism (Sanskrit: Vajrayāna): the esoteric Buddhist tradition prominent in Tibet and Himalayan regions.
  • Bodhisattva (Sanskrit: bodhisattva): one dedicated to awakening for the benefit of all beings.
  • Bodhicitta (Sanskrit: bodhicitta): the aspiration for awakening for self and others.
  • Emptiness (Sanskrit: śūnyatā): the absence of independent self-nature in phenomena.
  • Madhyamaka: the philosophical school associated with Nāgārjuna and emptiness.
  • Yogācāra: the philosophical school that analyses experience through consciousness.
  • Buddha-nature (Sanskrit: tathāgatagarbha): language for the capacity for buddhahood in all beings.
  • Three bodies (Sanskrit: trikāya): a framework for understanding a buddha's modes of presence.
  • Hīnayāna: a historically disparaging label; do not use it as a neutral name for Theravāda Buddhism.
