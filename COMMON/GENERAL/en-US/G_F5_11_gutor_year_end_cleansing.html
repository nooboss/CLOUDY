G.F5.11. (Phugpa) Gutor — Year-End Cleansing Ritual
Twenty-ninth day of the twelfth Tibetan month

The Gutor (Tibetan: Gutor, meaning "the 29th day of the last month" — the year-end cleansing ritual) falls on the 29th day of the twelfth Tibetan month — two days before Losar New Year (article G.F5.2). It is the year-end "cleansing" occasion: families and monasteries clean houses and shrine halls, and — most importantly — perform expelling rituals to drive away the ill omens of the old year and welcome the new year in purity. This rite closes the series of 10 annual festivals of group G.F5 (G.F5.2 → G.F5.11); after Gutor, the new-year cycle begins again with Losar.

1. What Gutor includes

  • Thorough cleaning: families scrub their houses — monasteries clean the shrine halls; the spirit is to leave behind the old and bad to welcome the new and good — like the year-end general cleaning found in many cultures (Vietnam, Japan — article G.E7.31 on the Japanese year-end festival season).
  • Offerings to household — local spirits: by custom, people prepare offering trays for the house — earth spirits (the custom layer — Tibetan folk belief, to be distinguished from Buddhist doctrine).
  • Monastic rites: expelling recitations — protective offering ceremony (Sanskrit: pūjā) rites (explained in article G.F5.1); the great monasteries stage the cham dance (Tibetan: cham — explained in article G.F5.2) with fierce masks — symbolising the elimination of evil.

2. Cham dance and the expelling dough

The most striking feature of Gutor is the expelling rituals:

  • Masked cham dance: dancers wear the masks of the dharma protector (Sanskrit: dharmapāla) figures — dramatically enacting the victory of good over evil — performed in the monastery courtyard before the community.
  • The "gutor" dough piece: each family cooks a special noodle — dough dish; by custom, a piece of the dough (holding the year's ill omens) is thrown toward the door — a symbolic act of "driving evil out of the house" (the custom layer — folk practice, not doctrine).

3. Three layers for reading Gutor

  • Doctrinal — practice layer: the spirit of Repentance (recognizing faults and resolving to change) — letting go of what was unwholesome in the old year; resolving to practise well in the new year (the same spirit as article G.C5 on seeing faults clearly and correcting oneself).
  • Shared practice layer: sutra chanting, protective offering ceremonies, cleaning the places of worship — the common practice of Vajrayāna Buddhism.
  • Custom layer: cham dance, the expelling dough, offerings to household spirits — Tibetan folk belief joined to Buddhism, to be distinguished from the original doctrine.

4. Summary — closing the annual festival group

  • Gutor = the 29th day of the twelfth Tibetan month — two days before Losar; the year-end cleansing occasion — driving away evil, welcoming the new year in purity.
  • The 10 annual festivals (G.F5.2 → G.F5.11) now form a complete cycle: Losar → the Miracles (G.F5.3–G.F5.4) → the Saga Dawa month (G.F5.5–G.F5.6) → Dzamling Chisang (G.F5.7) → Chokhor Duchen (G.F5.8) → Lhabab Duchen (G.F5.9) → Ganden Ngamcho (G.F5.10) → Gutor (G.F5.11).

Glossary:

  • Gutor (Tibetan, meaning "the 29th day of the last month" — the year-end cleansing ritual): the year-end cleansing — expelling rite before Losar New Year.
  • Cham (Tibetan — cham dance): the masked ritual dance — explained in article G.F5.
  • Offering ceremony (Sanskrit: pūjā): a rite of offering with material gifts and prayers — see article G.F5.
  • Repentance (recognizing faults and resolving to change): the practice of reviewing past faults and committing to correct them — part of the shared path of practice (article G.C5).
  • Dharma protector (Sanskrit: dharmapāla): figures with the role of protecting the teaching — familiar images in Tibetan monasteries.

Source notes

  • Gutor and the expelling customs — a general account of Tibetan Buddhist culture; the folk-belief elements are clearly marked as belonging to the custom layer.
  • The festival date of the 29th day of the twelfth Tibetan month follows the standard festival calendar of this subsection (the Phugpa system); it is not converted into a fixed Gregorian date, which varies by year.
