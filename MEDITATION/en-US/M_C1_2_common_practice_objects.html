M.C1.2. Common Practice Objects

The previous article built the framework of calm abiding: calming the mind and keeping attention continuous. But attention needs somewhere to perch — that place is the meditation subject. This article introduces the four most common subjects: the breath, bodily sensations, sound, and image / object. The three general principles from M.B2 still stand: a good subject is stable, directly knowable, non-agitating; choose one subject and keep it for the whole sitting; and remember the subject is only a scaffold — what is trained is the mind, not the subject.

1. Calm abiding with the breath

1.1. The most common subject — and why

The breath is the subject most recommended by the traditions, and M.B1–M.B2 already trained exactly calm abiding with the breath. Why it is "favoured": always present (nothing to buy, carry, or remember to bring), both distinct and gentle, immediately reflecting the state of mind, and possessing a complete canonical system guiding from the first steps to deep levels. [Source: Ānāpānasati Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 118 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

1.2. How to do it — summarized in four steps

  1. Choose a touching point: rim of the nose or belly area — keep it for the whole sitting and following sittings.
  2. Count when scattered: silently count with the out-breath, one to ten — the "scaffold" for the early phase.
  3. Release counting, follow sensations: know the whole in – pause – out cycle, sensing from coarse to fine.
  4. Keep a middle measure: observe without controlling; strain half as much; each break, gently rejoin.

1.3. Characteristics to know

  • The breath is a "living" subject: it changes with the state — as the mind settles, the breath refines and fades. That is a good signal, not the subject "lost". Know the subtlest sensation still present, or return to belly movement (as discussed in M.B2).
  • Typical difficulties: at first the breath is hard to catch; or inadvertently controlled. Both pass through patience and the "unforced" principle.
  • Recommended place: for beginners, this is the default subject — use it for several months before considering other subjects. Later articles do not "replace" it; they are options for different circumstances and temperaments.

2. Calm abiding with body sensation

2.1. The idea

Instead of following the movement of the breath, choose a relatively stable region of bodily sensation as the collectedness subject: the grounding of both feet, the feeling of both hands resting on the thighs, the heaviness and warmth of the whole body, or the contact area between body and seat. This is a "static" subject: its movement is far slower than the breath, so the mind easily rests lightly — suitable for those finding the breath too fine, or those swept into straining "tracking" of the breath.

2.2. How to do it

  • Choose one region, clearly know its present feeling: contact, pressure, warmth, heaviness, tingling.
  • Place attention on that region like laying a leaf on water; each fall, place again — the same recognize – release – return loop.
  • Do not manufacture sensation: if the hand "feels nothing special", clearly know "feeling nothing special" — that empty feeling is still a genuine subject.

2.3. Distinguished from body scan

This is not a relaxing-style body scan: a body scan moves attention through many regions to increase awareness and relaxation; calm abiding with body sensation holds one region to build collectedness. One travels about, one stays put. Different goals mean different ways — mixing the two makes the continuity calm abiding needs hard to gain.

3. Calm abiding with sound

3.1. The idea

Choose one sound or the whole "sound field" (all sounds in the environment, regarded as one stream) as the subject. This subject suits circumstances without absolute silence, and "listening-sensitive" people — instead of fighting noise, turn noise into the subject. Article M.A4 already hinted: stable background sound (fan, rain) disturbs less than sudden sound — here that principle is continued.

3.2. How to do it

  • One stable sound: a fan, rain, running water — hear that stream like hearing one long sustained note; clearly know its sounding, slight shifting, fading.
  • The whole sound field: instead of sticking to one sound, let all sounds come — traffic, birds, distant voices — as a passing stream; do not name each sound, do not analyse origins: only "hearing", not "that is the neighbour's motorbike".
  • Sudden sounds cutting in: clearly know "there is sound", return attention to the subject — each such time is one round of practice, not one failure.

3.3. How it differs from relaxing music

The two listenings differ greatly in purpose. Listening to music chooses beautiful content, stimulating pleasant feelings — that is relaxation, good in its way, but the mind is carried along the melody, literally "flowing with scenes". Calm abiding with sound uses neutral sound, unchosen by taste, and trains pure hearing — clearly knowing sound coming and going, adding nothing further. The interesting paradox: the more "boring" the sound subject, the better, because it does not imprison the mind with colour.

4. Calm abiding with an image / object

4.1. The idea

Choose a visual point or object as the subject: a candle flame, a small stone, a blue dot on the wall, the pattern of a coloured clay disc. The common way: look at the object at a suitable distance, eyes softened not staring, for a few minutes — then close the eyes and hold its remembered image; when the image fades, open the eyes to look again, then close and hold again. Alternating thus, the remembered image gradually sharpens and stabilizes.

4.2. Leading to the kasiṇa system

In Theravāda Buddhism, this visual subject is systematized as kasiṇa (Pali: kasiṇa): ten kasiṇas comprising earth, water, fire, air, the four colours blue – yellow – red – white, light, and space. [Source: Visuddhimagga, ch. IV–V - the Theravāda commentarial system.] The stable remembered image of the subject is the first step on the road to training the meditation sign (Pali: nimitta) — a detailed topic with its own article in the app (article M.C1.3); here only know the road exists, without rushing.

4.3. Notes for beginners

  • Visual subjects easily cause eye strain — keep sessions short, eyes soft, blinking naturally.
  • Easily breeding expectations of strange visions: seeing special lights or figures — recall M.B7: that is not the measure; keep returning to the real object and its remembered image.
  • This is a non-required subject: if not yet needed, not needed — the breath remains the recommended main road.

5. Choosing a subject for yourself and next steps

5.1. Suggestions by circumstance

  • Default, most stable: the breath — use as the main subject.
  • Breath too fine to catch, or often straining when following the breath: body sensation (feet, hands).
  • Noisy environment, or listening-sensitive people: sound — turning noise into the subject instead of the opponent.
  • Need to train remembered imagery, or following a tradition using kasiṇa: image / object — with the cautions of section 4.3.

And the three old rules remain in force: choose one subject and keep it for the whole sitting; try long enough (several weeks) before changing — changing cuts the chain mid-weave; when changing, change for a clear reason, not a one-day mood.

5.2. Summary

  • Four common subjects: breath (default), body sensation (static, easy to rest lightly on), sound (turning noise into the subject), image / object (doorway to kasiṇa and the meditation sign).
  • The subject is a scaffold — what is trained is the continuity of attention on that subject; do not run seeking the "best subject", train well on the chosen one.
  • All four subjects share the practice loop already learned: recognize — release gently — return.

Glossary:

  • Sound field (working term): all sounds in the environment regarded as one shared stream; the practitioner hears the whole stream instead of sticking to each separate sound.
  • Kasiṇa (Pali: kasiṇa): a group of ten collectedness subjects using an object or element (earth, water, fire, air, four colours, light, space) as the object for training remembered imagery — belonging to the forty-subject system of Theravāda Buddhism.
  • Meditation sign (Pali: nimitta): the remembered image of the subject appearing in the mind as collectedness deepens; presented in detail in the app's separate article; not a required measure.
  • Body scan: the practice of moving attention through many body regions to increase awareness and relaxation; unlike calm abiding holding one fixed sensation region as the collectedness subject.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Visuddhimagga (Pali: visuddhimagga): the compendium of meditation practice in Theravāda Buddhism, composed by the Elder Buddhaghosa around the 5th century.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
