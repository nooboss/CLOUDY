M.D1.5. The Four Foundations of Mindfulness, Basic to Advanced

Group M.D1 has now traversed the full four establishments of mindfulness (Pali: satipaṭṭhāna): overview (article M.D1), then each domain: body (article M.D1.1), feeling (article M.D1.2), mind (article M.D1.3), and dhammas (article M.D1.4). This article merges them from a path viewpoint: in which sequence to traverse the four domains, how many levels from basic to advanced each domain has, and what the discourses say about no mandatory single sequence.

1. Why a path is needed

The four establishments are a map of four domains, not four separate subjects — all four share one material: mindfulness (Pali: sati). But self-learning without a path easily falls into two extremes: either throwing the whole map over one's head — today body, tomorrow trying mind, the day after reading about seven awakening factors and trying too — each touched a little, none deep enough to see anything; or sticking in one place — sitting ten years only following the breath without knowing three unopened domains remain.

A path resolves both: a starting point, a recommended order, and criteria for knowing when to "leave" a stretch — all pedagogical suggestions, not laws. An easy image: the four establishments are like four rooms of one house — enterable through many doors, but beginners should enter the main door first.

2. Recommended sequence: body → feeling → mind → dhammas

The Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta's textual order is both a listing and a reasonable recommended sequence for beginners — because each step builds the foundation for the next:

  • Body first — because body is the distinctest, coarsest, located object: pain in the legs, softness in the shoulders, breath at the nose. The material trained here — continuous clear knowing — is the shared raw material for the remaining three domains.
  • Feeling next — because feeling lies between body and mind: one must distinguish "unpleasant sensation" from "the tension wanting to expel it" — clearly knowing the feeling layer — before the mind layer stops blending into one block.
  • Mind after — because mind contemplation is mind mirroring mind, the thinnest and fastest object of the three; it needs a firm body – feeling knowing foundation to avoid slipping into imagining.
  • Dhammas last — because analytical frames (five aggregates, six internal–external bases, seven awakening factors, four noble truths) grip only when the three coarse layers are distinct; opening the analytical lens without distinctly seeing body – feeling – mind turns observation into speculation.

The Buddha's final instruction in the Mahāparinibbāna Sutta goes with this spirit: be an island, a refuge for yourself — with mindfulness established on the body. [Source: Mahāparinibbāna Sutta, Dīgha Nikāya, DN 16 - Theravāda Buddhism.] The body is the most common doorway across canonical eras — and also the most stable doorway for today's self-learners.

3. How many levels each domain has: basic to advanced

"Basic to advanced" does not mean these four domains gain other domains, but each domain has many levels — the same object, the same frame, deepening gradually:

  • Body contemplation: the basic level is clearly knowing postures, activities, coarse breath; the deep level is the sixteen steps of mindfulness of breathing leading through all four establishments (the app's advanced L4 article), mindfulness immersed in the body, and the tradition's detailed body contemplations — the body-contemplation subject group (group D3).
  • Feeling contemplation: the basic level is clearly knowing the pleasant – unpleasant – neutral tone; the deep level is seeing feeling arising – passing moment by moment and clearly seeing the moment the mind leans in / pushes away — joining straight to insight meditation.
  • Mind contemplation: the basic level is labelling states after they arise; the deep level is catching the switching moment — the mind just settled already scattered, just irritated already tense — while switching is still happening.
  • Dhamma contemplation: the basic level is using one frame at a time (for example clearly knowing the five hindrances present / absent); the deep level is viewing whole sittings through the four noble truths — seeing suffering, seeing the clinging cause, seeing settling, seeing the path being walked.

What is worth noting: the four domains' deep levels all touch one another — the sixteen breath steps lead through feelings – mind – dhammas; deep feeling contemplation leads to the three characteristics; dhamma contemplation sees through all three layers. The deeper one goes, the more the four-room map resembles an open-plan house.

4. The discourses mandate no single sequence

The body → feeling → mind → dhammas order is a recommended order, not a mandatory condition. The Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta lists in that order but never says "body must finish before feeling"; and the canon itself shows different doorways:

  • The breath leads all four: the mindfulness-of-breathing discourse presents sixteen steps in four groups corresponding to body – feeling – mind – dhammas — followers of this road touch all four domains from the very first subject. [Source: Ānāpānasati Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 118 - Theravāda Buddhism.]
  • Different objects for different circumstances: the canon records advising different subjects for each person by capacity and circumstances — the "choosing a fitting subject" principle which the traditional subject group (group D3) unfolds.
  • When crossing obstacles through another domain: mind too agitated → back to tranquillity – collectedness, that is temporarily leaving the current stretch to replenish foundation; too much pain → walking meditation, that is switching from sitting observation to posture observation. Shifting rhythm between domains mid-sitting is normal, not "abandoning the path".

The principle for self-learners: sequence is a means — if following it for several weeks shows distinct progress, continue; if one stretch blocks (for example mind contemplation never showing anything distinct), return to the previous stretch to replenish foundation, or change doorways — and record in the journal to evaluate by data rather than that day's feeling.

5. Sequences of modern systems

How "four establishments from basic to advanced" unfolds in modern meditation lineages also differs — exactly the "one name, many systems" spirit discussed in article C2.1. In common presentations:

  • The noting lineage (Mahāsi): beginning with clearly knowing coarse movements — rising-falling belly, steps — then gradually opening to feeling, mind, dhammas through noting skill; the path closely matches the textual order.
  • The concentration-first lineage (Pa-Auk): building collectedness with the breath first — until the foundation is firm — then unfolding the four establishments in detail; that is, "advanced" begins after a dense collectedness foundation.
  • The scanning lineage (Goenka): traversing body – feeling mainly by whole-body sensation scanning, on a calm-collectedness foundation; mind and dhammas appear through arising – passing experience itself.
  • The mind-centred lineage (U Tejaniya): entering by viewing mind and practice attitude — differing from the textual order but the same map.

The point for beginners: lineages differ in departure point and technique, not destination; choosing one lineage long enough before evaluating (the "one teacher at a time" principle — article M.A6), not mixing many lineages' sequences in the same phase.

6. A suggested path for self-learners

Merging the above into a practical path — adjusted by circumstances, not a rigid schedule:

  • Stretch 1 — body (reasonable start for most): breath as subject + clearly knowing postures and walking meditation. Transition criterion: knowing what the body is doing / in which posture through most sittings, and several markers outside sittings.
  • Stretch 2 — feeling: adding the tone layer to the same old objects — still breath, still aching legs, but now clearly knowing "pleasant / unpleasant / neutral" and the accompanying leaning reaction. Criterion: seeing the "wanting to keep / wanting to expel" moment appearing at least several times each sitting.
  • Stretch 3 — mind: opening to labelling mental states. Criterion: distinguishing feeling from mental states; recognizing scattering / sluggishness earlier (per the journal — article M.B7).
  • Stretch 4 — dhammas: adding analytical frames one by one — recommended beginning with five hindrances and seven awakening factors because directly usable in sittings; the four noble truths as the frame for reviewing whole sittings.
  • Throughout: keeping the never-abandoned foundation — each sitting still opening with a few minutes of breath (default subject); the path adds layers, not changes lessons. Those in special circumstances (chronic pain, high anxiety) may be advised to enter through another door — a reasonable adjustment; ask a teacher when possible (article M.A6).

7. Summary and next steps

  • The four establishments are a four-domain map; the body → feeling → mind → dhammas sequence is a reasonable recommendation because each step builds the next's foundation.
  • Each domain has many levels from basic to advanced; the deeper, the more the four domains touch one another.
  • The discourses mandate no single sequence — the breath leads all four, different subjects for different circumstances, shifting rhythm between domains mid-sitting is normal.
  • Modern systems depart differently but share the map; self-learners choose one sequence long enough, journal, keep the breath foundation.

Glossary:

  • Four establishments of mindfulness (Pali: satipaṭṭhāna): four grounds for establishing mindfulness — body, feelings, mind, dhammas.
  • Mindfulness (Pali: sati): the capacity to remember and stay with what is happening as it happens.
  • Walking meditation (Pali: caṅkama): meditation with steps' movement as object.
  • Mindfulness of breathing (Pali: ānāpānassati): the practice of keeping mindfulness on the breath — the subject the discourses place first into body contemplation.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
