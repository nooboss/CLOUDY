M.B1. Body and Breath Foundations

The foundational articles (M.A1 → M.A6) have answered what meditation is, how to prepare, and how to stay safe. As the previous article promised, the next step in the app's path is the Shared Practical Skills group, opening with this article. The three skills below are the foundation of nearly every later meditation method: releasing bodily tension so the body settles, noticing the natural breath to place the mind on an object, and counting the breath to gather attention while the mind is still scattered. The three follow one another very naturally — read in order, then practise step by step.

1. Releasing bodily tension

1.1. Why release comes first

Most of us go through the day with a body that is "braced" without knowing it: jaw slightly clenched, shoulders lifted to the ears, belly tight, brow furrowed. A tense body means a tense mind; sitting down while the body is still rigid makes it hard for the mind to settle. Relaxing the body is therefore not random "warm-up" but part of the practice: the canon lists tranquillity (Pali: passaddhi) — the lightness, freshness, and workability of body and mind — as one of the seven factors of awakening (Pali: bojjhaṅga). [Source: Bojjhaṅga Saṃyutta, Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 46 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

Note the word "tranquillity": it is not limp dullness, but a state of body and mind as fresh as a sponge wrung dry — soft yet alert. Release in order to practise, not in order to sleep.

1.2. Recognizing where tension sits

The first step is not "to relax" but to clearly see where tension sits. Scan in turn the areas that hold the most tension:

  • Face: is the forehead furrowed? The jaw clenched? The area around the eyes tight?
  • Shoulders and neck: are the shoulders "lifted up"? The neck jutting forward?
  • Chest and belly: is the chest compressed, the belly held tight — as often happens with anxiety?
  • Both hands: clenched or loosely open? The hands are a very sensitive "tension meter".

1.3. How to release each area (5–10 minutes)

  1. Sit or lie down comfortably per the "Meditation posture" section (article M.A4); close the eyes gently.
  2. Take two or three slow, extended breaths as a signal: now is time to rest.
  3. Scan from top down: face → jaw → shoulders → arms → hands → chest → belly → back → hips → legs → feet.
  4. In each area, simply clearly know the feeling present (tight, warm, numb, heavy...), then as you breathe out, let that area "slacken" a little. No need to force it soft at once — remind it again over several breaths.
  5. End with a few breaths sensing the whole body at once.

1.4. A few misunderstandings about releasing

  • Release ≠ losing alertness. A soft body with an alert mind — the two go together. If drowsiness comes, open the eyes slightly or sit a little straighter.
  • Do not force "immediate release". Tension accumulated over years; however much releases is good; forcing only breeds more tension.
  • Tension returning is normal. Mid-sitting you notice the shoulders lifted again — noticing and releasing again is the exercise, not failure.

With the body settled, the next step is placing the mind on the breath — the most familiar object across all meditation traditions.

2. Noticing the natural breath

2.1. The breath — the most familiar object

In the discourses, placing mindfulness (Pali: sati) on the breath is called mindfulness of breathing (Pali: ānāpānassati). The breath is always present, needs no equipment, has a rhythm distinct enough to hold the mind, and immediately reflects the state of mind — which is why it is the most used meditation subject (Pali: kammaṭṭhāna), from Theravāda Buddhism to Zen.

The Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta — the foundational discourse on the four establishments of mindfulness (Pali: satipaṭṭhāna) — opens the body-contemplation section with exactly the breath: "breathing in long, one clearly knows 'breathing in long'; breathing in short, one clearly knows 'breathing in short'". [Source: Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 10 - Theravāda Buddhism.] Another dedicated discourse — the Ānāpānasati Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 118 — presents the entire path of training mindfulness of breathing from coarse breath to deeper states; here we need only its very simple beginning.

2.2. The number-one principle: observe, do not control

As soon as attention lands on the breath, the breath usually changes by itself — longer, deeper, or harsher. That is a natural response, not your fault. All that is needed is to let the breath happen as it is: short, know short; long, know long; uneven, know uneven. Remember the image: you are one sitting watching traffic pass, not one standing directing traffic.

Noticing the natural breath thus differs from deep-breathing exercises: breathing exercises change the rhythm for a physiological goal; here we only observe the rhythm present — the less interference the better.

2.3. Choose one place to feel it

  • Nostrils (rim of the nose, nostrils): the feeling of air coming in and out is sharpest here; it refines attention, often used in tranquillity meditation (Pali: samatha).
  • Belly (navel area, belly rising – falling): large movement, easy to follow, comfortable for the mind; often used in insight meditation (Pali: vipassanā).
  • Whole body: sensing the breath nourishing the whole body.

Beginners should choose one place and keep it for the whole sitting; switching places continuously keeps the mind from gathering. The chosen place is called the touching point.

2.4. Common obstacles

  • Breath faint, "cannot find it": shift sensing to where movement is clearest (usually the belly), or notice the coolness on breathing in and warmth on breathing out.
  • Inadvertently controlling: when you notice you are "leading" the breath, gently let go; you may silently say: "let it breathe by itself".
  • Bored with the breath, finding it monotonous: observe more finely — no breath is like another: some long, some short, some deep, some light. This fineness is where the interest of practice lies.

3. Counting the breath

3.1. Why counting is needed

At the noticing step, a wandering mind is only natural. While still unaccustomed, you need an extra "scaffold" to hold attention — that is counting the breath. Counting turns the breath into an exercise with a clear count, and reveals at once when the mind leaves: wherever you forget the count, you have just been carried away.

3.2. How to count

  1. After breathing out, silently count "one". Breathe in – breathe out, count "two"... up to "ten".
  2. At "ten", return to "one". Do not continue to eleven, twelve.
  3. Forgetting the count, or accidentally passing ten — no problem; gently return to "one".
  4. Placing the number at the end of the out-breath is usually easiest to hold.

Several variants are also used: counting one number per full "in – out" breath; or counting each phase ("in – one, out – two"...); or counting down from ten to one. Any way works — the point is to keep one way for the whole sitting, not switch continuously.

3.3. Counting the breath across traditions

Counting the breath is no improvised trick — it appears on both the Southern and Northern sides of Buddhism:

  • Theravāda Buddhism: the Visuddhimagga (Pali: visuddhimagga) lists counting (Pali: gaṇanā) as the first stage in training mindfulness of breathing, before the stages of following, touching, and settling. [Source: Visuddhimagga, ch. VIII - the Theravāda commentarial system.]
  • East Asian Zen: there is a well-known method called breath-counting meditation (Japanese: sūsokukan), proposed by the Tiantai master Zhiyi and widely spread in Japanese Zen. [Source: The Six Subtle Dharma Gates by Zhiyi - the Tiantai Zen school, Mahāyāna Buddhism.]

The traditions use breath counting for the same purpose: remedying a scattered mind at the early stage — counting is a tool, not the final goal.

3.4. When to drop counting

Counting is like training wheels on a bicycle: needed at first, released once balanced. Signs you may drop counting: you can sit without straining to remember the number; the breath is naturally light; the mind follows the breath more smoothly. Then shift entirely to simply clearly knowing the breath coming in and out — the content of the app's next article: "Following the sensations of the breath" (article M.B2). Conversely, when the mind scatters again (tired, stressed, weighed down) — returning to counting is entirely reasonable, even for the experienced.

4. Summary and next steps

  • Releasing bodily tension: scan areas that often tense (face, jaw, shoulders, neck, chest, belly), release gently with each out-breath — tranquillity is a factor of awakening, not a perfunctory "physical warm-up".
  • Noticing the natural breath: observe as it is, do not control; choose one touching point and keep it for the whole sitting.
  • Counting the breath: a scaffold for a scattered mind — silently count with the out-breath from one to ten and repeat; forgetting means returning to "one", without blaming yourself; when the mind steadies, release the numbers.
  • The repeated "recognize — release gently — return" loop is itself the core of every later meditation method.

Glossary:

  • Tranquillity (Pali: passaddhi): the lightness, freshness, and workability of body and mind; one of the seven factors of awakening. Tranquillity differs from dullness: the body is "soft" yet the mind stays alert.
  • Seven factors of awakening (Pali: bojjhaṅga): the seven conditions the canon points to as leading to awakening: mindfulness, investigation of dhammas, effort, joy, tranquillity, concentration, equanimity.
  • Meditation subject (Pali: kammaṭṭhāna): the object on which the meditator relies to gather and develop the mind, for example the breath, bodily sensations, loving-kindness.
  • Four establishments of mindfulness (Pali: satipaṭṭhāna): the four domains of mindfulness listed in the canon: body, feelings, mind, dhammas. The breath is the first part of body contemplation.
  • Touching point: the place on the body chosen to sense the breath (nostrils or belly), kept fixed for the whole sitting so attention is not pulled away.
  • Breath-counting meditation (Japanese: sūsokukan): the breath-counting method of East Asian Zen; "sū" means counting, "soku" means breath, "kan" means contemplation.
  • Visuddhimagga (Pali: visuddhimagga): the compendium of meditation practice in Theravāda Buddhism, composed by the Elder Buddhaghosa around the 5th century.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
