M.D3.3. Contemplation of Foulness (Asubha)

Within the forty-subject map, the group needing most careful presentation is the ten foulness meditations — corpse contemplations reducing infatuation with the body. This article presents exactly per the taxonomy's editorial note: historical–monastic level — explaining what this subject group is, why it once existed, what the canon says — and no practice guidance; with distinct safety warnings for those who should not approach. 1. What is foulness — and why does it exist?

Contemplation of foulness (Pali: asubha; asubha meaning "not beautiful") is a subject group aiming to see the body's true nature without overlaying beautifying perceptions — cooling attachment to the body (Pali: rūpa-rāga): the pull binding the mind to beauty, youth, and bodily attractiveness. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 1.2 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

This practice's logic is very straight: bodily infatuation rests on one part of the image — the part chosen to view; foulness supplies the image's remaining part: the body is also a changing material block, aging, unable to keep form. Seeing both parts fully, one-sided attraction loses footing — the mind rebalances. This is not hating the body — this group's central principle is balancing a perception (Pali: saññā): compensating the already rampant "beautiful" perception, not replacing it with an oppositely rampant "ugly" perception — in detail in section 5.

2. Canonical origins and historical context

This subject group has distinct canonical roots: in the four establishments, the discourses present body contemplation at the level of reflection on repulsiveness (Pali: paṭikūlamanasikāra) — "when this body has head-hairs, body-hairs, nails, teeth, skin, then from their presence in the body, there is impurity; and this body is likewise...". [Source: Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 10 - Theravāda Buddhism.] At the commentary level, corpse subjects were developed into a system in the Visuddhimagga.

On historical context: monastic life in the Buddha's time had conditions to meet corpses in charnel-ground forests — where bhikkhus walked meditation and dwelled; foulness subjects joined that reality: viewing corpses in decomposition stages, taking those images as subjects recalled when sitting. Only in modern times — with medicine, law, and hygiene standards — this original practice condition is no longer available, and modern meditation lineages almost never transmit this subject group in original form; the modern approach only keeps the idea part (seeing the body as it truly is) through other practices (articles M.D3.4, M.D3.5).

3. The ten foulness subjects

The Visuddhimagga (ch. VI) lists ten subjects — exactly a corpse's decomposition stages, from beginning to end: [Source: Visuddhimagga, ch. VI - Theravāda Buddhism.]

  • 1. Swollen;
  • 2. Discoloured — skin turning blue-purple;
  • 3. Festering — pus flowing out;
  • 4. Fissured — the body beginning to break;
  • 5. Gnawed in part — forest animals tearing one part;
  • 6. Scattered in parts — torn scattered;
  • 7. Hacked and scattered — the body hacked into pieces;
  • 8. Bloody — the body oozing blood;
  • 9. Worm-infested — the body full of worms;
  • 10. Bones — only bones remaining.

Two points worth noting about this list. First, it is not a horror list for play — each subject joins one observation moment of a natural reality; second, the final subject (bones) is the most stable subject — favoured by the commentaries for tidiness and repeatability, directly joining death recollection (article M.D3.7): "as this body will be so — my body will also be so".

4. Place in the 40 system and relation to mindfulness immersed in the body

  • Within the 40 system: the ten foulness meditations lead to the first jhāna — because aversion-tinged objects joined to countering minds cannot carry bliss deeper; this is the group's "limited in collectedness depth" — exactly the commentaries' division, not criticism.
  • Relation to mindfulness immersed in the body: the 32-body-parts contemplation (article M.D3.4) — the "reflection on repulsiveness" part of mindfulness immersed in the body — uses the same view as foulness but on one's own living body: no corpses needed, only listing and contemplating parts — this way remains modernly transmitted because far more available and safer.
  • Relation to death recollection: foulness's ending is bones; bones' ending is "my body will also be so" — the natural road to death recollection (article M.D3.7) — a subject with practice history continuous to today in many lineages.

5. Safety warnings — who should not approach

This is the article's main part — exactly the taxonomy's editorial note:

  • No practice guidance in the app. This group is presented at historical–monastic level: understanding what it is, knowing it exists in the 40 system — not for self-training along.
  • Unsuitable for those currently having: body-image problems (seeing one's own body ugly, hating one's body), death anxiety, depression, or psychological trauma related to death – corpses. With these states, foulness subjects are not "difficult exercises" but risk catalysts — potentially worsening self-criticism, fear, or estrangement from the body (article M.A3 already presented this signal group).
  • Even the healthy should not self-try: strong countering contemplations (foulness, death recollection, food — article M.D3.6) need a collectedness foundation and guidance; self-trying easily skews to two extremes — either horror obsession, or self-hatred — both skewing the practice's essence.
  • The perception-balancing principle (repeated): foulness's purpose is compensating one perception — not replacing one perception with its opposite. Right practice is a more balanced mind after contemplating, not a heavier, self-hating, or fearful mind. Seeing skew — stopping, back to the breath, to loving-kindness (article M.D2.1), telling the experienced (article M.A6).
  • Safe substitutes for the "reducing bodily infatuation" intention: mindfulness immersed in the body with 32 parts (article M.D3.4), four-elements defining (article M.D3.5) — both viewing "the body as it truly is" without frightening images; and impermanence contemplation on the body — seeing the body changing daily, a longer-enduring way.

6. Summary and next steps

  • Contemplation of foulness (asubha) is a ten-corpse-subject group balancing bodily infatuation — logic: supplying the image's skipped part, cooling one-sided attraction.
  • Canonical roots in reflection on repulsiveness (MN 10); the ten-stage system systematized by commentaries (Visuddhimagga ch. VI) from ancient monastic reality; modernity no longer has original practice conditions.
  • Within the 40 system: leading to the first jhāna; joining 32 parts (article M.D3.4) and death recollection (article M.D3.7).
  • Safety warnings are this article's main part: no practice guidance; unsuitable for those with body-image problems, death anxiety, depression; the healthy also should not self-try — safe substitutes are 32 parts, four elements, bodily impermanence contemplation.

Glossary:

  • Contemplation of foulness (Pali: asubha): a ten-subject group on corpses' decomposition stages — balancing attachment to the body.
  • Reflection on repulsiveness (Pali: paṭikūlamanasikāra): contemplating the body with non-infatuation — the canonical part of the same view.
  • Attachment to the body (Pali: rūpa-rāga): the pull binding the mind to beauty and bodily attractiveness.
  • Mindfulness immersed in the body (Pali: kāyagatāsati): the practice of keeping mindfulness on the body (article M.D1.1) — including the reflection-on-repulsiveness part per the Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
