M.C1.4. Levels of Concentration and Absorption

A question appears very early for those who have sat for a few months: "without attaining jhāna, can it be called concentration?" The tradition's answer is very reassuring: concentration is a spectrum of many levels, not an on / off switch. This article presents the map of that spectrum, from low to high: the three levels of concentration distinguished by the commentarial system, then jhāna — deep absorptive states — with the four form-sphere jhānas and the four formless attainments. This content is advanced: read for the map, not for self-grading — the "reference map, not scoreboard" principle will be repeated at the article's end.

1. Three levels of concentration

Concentration (Pali: samādhi) is not something "present or absent". The Theravāda commentarial system distinguishes three levels by depth and durability: [Source: Visuddhimagga, ch. III - the Theravāda commentarial system.]

  • Momentary concentration (Pali: khaṇika-samādhi): attention stable moment by moment — three consecutive clearly known breaths, one unbroken chain of steps, ten minutes un-swept by the mind. This is the level most serious practitioners have already reached without knowing its name.
  • Access concentration (Pali: upacāra-samādhi): collectedness deep enough that distraction stops obstructing — the mind still knows surrounding business but is not pulled away; on visual-subject paths, this is the threshold where the counterpart sign (article M.C1.3) usually arises.
  • Absorption concentration (Pali: appanā-samādhi): the mind enters absorptive states — jhāna (Pali: jhāna, Sanskrit: dhyāna) — complete collectedness on the object, the deepest level addressed in the following sections.

Picture the three levels as water: momentary concentration is evenly falling drops; access concentration is a seamless stream (the "oil poured out in a stream" image — article M.C1.1); absorption concentration is a brimming lake surface, no more bank between water and shore. To the article's opening question: momentary concentration is genuine concentration — not yet attaining jhāna does not mean no concentration yet.

2. Deep concentration and jhāna — overview

Jhāna (Pali: jhāna) is the deepest part of the concentration spectrum. Points to grasp at overview level:

  • The canon presents jhāna as the core of right concentration — the four jhāna levels are placed exactly at the position of right concentration in the Noble Eightfold Path. [Source: Magga Saṃyutta, Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 45.8 - Theravāda Buddhism.]
  • True to the name "absorption": in jhāna, the senses separate from outer scenes; the mind lies wholly in the subject — as one lies wholly in deep sleep, though fully awake and lucid.
  • Terminological differences between traditions: some lineages treat jhāna as the principal training goal of calm abiding; some use it as the basis for insight; some do not set it as a goal for lay practitioners. Traditions share the same word, but the word's "tension" differs — read literature with attention to source level.
  • Not a required goal for everyone: the canon records many liberated through the "scenic route" (from deep collectedness into observation) and also many through the "express route" (straight into observation with momentary concentration) — the map in section 5 will clarify.
  • On etymology: jhāna transcribed into Chinese becomes "Thiền-na", thence the word "Thiền"; dhyāna into Chinese reads "chán", into Japanese "Zen" — meaning Zen and Thiền both originate here. Same root, two very different lands.

3. The four form-sphere jhānas and the five jhāna factors

3.1. The five jhāna factors

The jhāna levels are described through jhāna factors (Pali: jhānaṅga), five in number:

  • Initial application (Pali: vitakka) — like an arm bringing the bell near.
  • Sustained application (Pali: vicāra) — like a hand keeping the bell evenly ringing.
  • Rapture (Pali: pīti) — like one thirsty meeting cool water, trembling with joy.
  • Bliss (Pali: sukha) — like having drunk, body and mind cooled through.
  • One-pointedness (Pali: ekaggatā) — already met in article M.B2; here it is the base quality of the whole state.

3.2. The four form-sphere jhānas

Form-sphere jhānas (Pali: rūpajjhāna) — presented as the canon lists them: [Source: Magga Saṃyutta, Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 45.8 and Sāmaññaphala Sutta, Dīgha Nikāya, DN 2 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

  • Level 1: the mind secluded from sense desires, with initial – sustained application – rapture – bliss – one-pointedness still present. Directing and sustaining activity remains, so still "doing"; but already separated from thirst-producing scenes.
  • Level 2: initial and sustained application subside; the mind inwardly stilled, one-pointed; rapture – bliss born of that very stillness. The discourse likens it to water welling up by itself in a lake — no more stirring needed.
  • Level 3: rapture fades, only bliss remains with equanimity and clear comprehension; a soft, full feeling, no more trembling excitement. The discourses describe: "the body touched by bliss without infatuation".
  • Level 4: bliss and pain both cease — pleasant and unpleasant alike; tranquillity already purified the body, now only transparent equanimity and mindfulness remain. Exactly as learned: equanimity (Pali: upekkhā) is the peak of these four levels — not indifference, but the finest balance.

3.3. A note on descriptions

The canon lists as above; the commentarial system analyses more finely (for instance: exactly which jhāna factors are present or absent at each level, and how the breath changes at deep levels); modern meditation teachers describe "the feeling of entering jhāna" differently by lineage. This is a place where sources differ in presentation, not in phenomena — readers should hold the canonical outline as root, viewing other analyses as commentary layers. [Source: comparing the canonical basket with Visuddhimagga, ch. IV - Theravāda Buddhism.]

4. The four formless attainments

Above the 4th form-sphere jhāna, the path of collectedness has a range passing beyond material objects called the formless attainments (Pali: arūpa-samāpatti), in four levels: [Source: Sāmaññaphala Sutta, Dīgha Nikāya, DN 2; Visuddhimagga, ch. X - Theravāda Buddhism.]

  • Boundless space (Pali: ākāsānañcāyatana): passing beyond material objects, the mind dwells on boundless space.
  • Boundless consciousness (Pali: viññāṇañcāyatana): dwelling on that very boundless knowing.
  • Nothingness (Pali: ākiñcaññāyatana): dwelling on there being no object at all to hold.
  • Neither-perception-nor-non-perception (Pali: nevasaññānāsaññāyatana): the subtlest level worldly collectedness reaches.

Two things to understand rightly: first, this is a conceptual sequence for having a map — each level trained by taking the previous as basis, not a game-like sequential "unlocking" machine. Second, these levels require a very firm collectedness foundation and an experienced guide — the app presents them only at educational, overview level, per the principle for advanced content; actual training must follow a teacher and a specific tradition.

5. What this map means for practitioners

  • Momentary concentration is what beginners need and already have. Every skill from group B until now builds exactly this level; and this level is enough to begin insight meditation — many traditional paths go straight from momentary concentration into observing body and mind.
  • Access concentration is the "ordinary experience" level of the settled: sittings when the mind runs smooth, distraction unable to obstruct — if met, only know it, do not startle and lose it.
  • Jhāna and formless attainments are the deep part — not required, not raced. Those with the conditions to train jhāna systematically should have a direct teacher and an already steady regular foundation; those on the straight-to-observation road lack nothing at all.
  • Do not grade yourself on the map. The "which level am I at" zone is where misreading likes to dwell — exactly article M.B7: pleasant states and special visions are not the measure; the map orients, it does not roll-call.

6. Summary and next steps

  • Three levels of concentration: momentary (moment by moment — beginners already have it), access (near the threshold, distraction stops obstructing), absorption (absorption — jhāna); not yet attaining jhāna is still having concentration.
  • Jhāna (jhāna): the core of right concentration in the discourses; four form-sphere levels through five jhāna factors (initial, sustained, rapture, bliss, one-pointedness) — rapture settling into bliss, bliss settling into tranquillity – equanimity; sources differ in fineness, not in phenomena.
  • Formless attainments: four levels passing beyond material objects, the peak of worldly collectedness — presented at map level; actual training needs a firm foundation and a teacher.
  • The map orients; no level is required; do not grade yourself on it.

Glossary:

  • Momentary concentration (Pali: khaṇika-samādhi): the level of collectedness sustained moment by moment on the subject; the minimum foundation enough to begin insight meditation.
  • Access concentration (Pali: upacāra-samādhi): the level near the threshold of absorption — distraction stops obstructing; usually where the counterpart sign arises.
  • Absorption concentration (Pali: appanā-samādhi): deep abiding collectedness — the mind wholly absorbed in the subject, that is, dwelling in jhāna.
  • Jhāna (Pali: jhāna, Sanskrit: dhyāna): deep states of collectedness in which the mind is wholly absorbed in the subject; the words "Thiền" and "Zen" both originate here.
  • Jhāna factors (Pali: jhānaṅga): the factors constituting jhāna — initial application, sustained application, rapture, bliss, one-pointedness.
  • Form-sphere jhānas (Pali: rūpajjhāna): the four jhāna levels still joined to material objects.
  • Formless attainments (Pali: arūpa-samāpatti): the four levels of collectedness passing beyond material objects, each trained by taking the previous level as basis.
  • Equanimity (Pali: upekkhā): a balanced mind, neither pushing nor pulling before gain – loss, pleasant – unpleasant; here the peak of the four form-sphere jhānas.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Visuddhimagga (Pali: visuddhimagga): the compendium of meditation practice in Theravāda Buddhism, composed by the Elder Buddhaghosa around the 5th century.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
