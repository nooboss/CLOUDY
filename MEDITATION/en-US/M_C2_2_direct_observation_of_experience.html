M.C2.2. Direct Observation of Experience

The insight-opening article oriented: seeing experience in many ways to see its nature clearly. This article enters four foundational observation skills, ordered from coarse to fine: seeing the mind's reaction chain, separating data from story, observing thought, and observing strong emotion safely. These four skills share one already-available material: the clear knowing trained from group B — now directed at the mind's subtlest places.

1. Observing the mind's reactions

1.1. The reaction chain — happening daily yet rarely seen

Between two quarrels, between bad news and action, between noise and irritation — a reaction chain runs very fast, so familiar it looks "suddenly so". Insight meditation's first skill is making that chain slow down in knowing:

object → feeling → liking / aversion → reaction → consequence

  • Object: a sound, a remark, a message, a sensation in the body.
  • Feeling: pleasant — unpleasant — neutral, appearing at contact.
  • Liking / aversion: the mind leaning toward — wanting to pull the pleasant near, push the unpleasant far. This is the hinge: the "to me" meaning is attached here.
  • Reaction: words, deeds, or only a frowning face — mind and body already acted along the leaning.
  • Consequence: a new mental state — one layer thicker rather than softer.

This chain the canon describes tightly in the dependent-origination frame: contact → feeling → craving → clinging (Pali: phassa – vedanā – taṇhā – upādāna — presented in detail in article M.C2.4). For beginners, only the everyday version above is needed: the point mindfulness can "wedge in" lies between feeling and liking / aversion — when the rising leaning is clearly seen, a gap opens, and reaction is no longer the only one. Exactly the spirit learned in article M.B3: the value of recognition is the pause between seeing and following.

1.2. How to train

  • Begin with small events: noise, an undone reminder, an unpleasant message — not beginning with life's biggest battle.
  • Trace a reaction backward: after seeing you were irritated — step back stage by stage: irritation (reaction) ← aversive leaning ← unpleasant feeling ← sound. A few rounds, the chain shows clearly and next time is seen earlier.
  • In the sitting hour: when a phenomenon arrives (itch, sound, thought), notice two separate events: the phenomenon, and the leaning after it. The phenomenon is the guest; the leaning is ours.

2. Direct experience and the mind's story

2.1. Two layers present at once

In the same moment, two different things are happening: direct data — what the senses and mind are receiving right now (feet touching the floor, traffic, cool breath, an anxiety trembling in the chest); and story — the interpretation, judgment, and prediction the mind adds ("why so noisy all week", "this anxiety must be about money", "I must be calmer"). Data is present; story is past and future invited in.

The canon has a word for story-weaving activity: papañca (Pali: papañca): a single thought multiplied into a train of stories about me – others – past – future. The discourses record the Buddha himself observing: what one deliberates manifoldly, that is what "proliferation" governs — the more thinking, the more stories. The Madhupiṇḍika Sutta records the Venerable Mahākaccāna explaining to a deity: according to what one deliberates manifoldly, those phenomena "invade" one — the more clinging to namings and expressions, the more trúng their trick. [Source: Madhupiṇḍika Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 18 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

2.2. How to separate the two layers

  • The test question: "is this happening right now, or about another time?" — feet currently numb: direct; "feet numb probably because little sitting this week": story.
  • Use a two-word label: "storytelling" — lightly naming when the story is running; then back to data.
  • Do not kill the storyteller: the goal is not absolute silence of interpretation — interpreting is the mind's natural function; the goal is knowing which is which, and when focus is needed, standing in data.

This skill has a long-term side: when "story" is clearly seen as a layer added by the mind, practitioners begin seeing the nature of story itself — a thought-stream like all other phenomena: arising – passing, impermanent. That is the bridge to observing thought in the next section.

3. Mindfulness of thought

3.1. Thought as a sixth sense

At this point, thought can be seen in its right place: as a sixth sense — a stream of phenomena arriving and leaving by itself, like sound or sights. Previously, thought was usually taken as "I am thinking" — tightly joined to a subject; now try seeing: "there is thinking" — an event in the mind, coming and going by its own laws. The canon places thought in the "mind" domain of the four establishments of mindfulness: mental states present clearly known — with or without greed, scattered or not, noble or base. [Source: Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 10 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

3.2. Three attitudes to a thought — neither believing nor suppressing needed

  • Believing: the default — a thought arrives, I am the thought: swept into the plot, feelings following, deeds following.
  • Chasing away: trying to extinguish it — already known to backfire: the command "don't think" is itself a thought.
  • Clearly seeing: clearly knowing "there is a thought", labelling "thinking", neither believing nor chasing — the thought runs out of fuel by itself, like an echo fading when no one calls back.

A few practical notes: thoughts about meditation itself ("today going well", "some calm present") are also only thoughts — know them like all other thoughts. Thoughts need not be beautiful to be observed: fragmentary, unclean, circling thoughts — all are data, not evidence of level. And observing thought is not the goal of filling gaps: no thought is also only a moment like all other moments.

4. Mindfulness of strong emotion

4.1. The number-one principle: step by step, with an exit

Strong emotions — surging anger, panic, flooding sorrow — are the hardest "gym". The foremost principle: observing strong emotion is a step-by-step skill, not a courage test. The safety rule of article M.A3 remains in force: always an exit; if overloaded, retreat to a safe object (the breath, both feet on the ground, grounding with five visible things); and if emotion is tied to old trauma — follow the app's trauma-sensitive practice principles: open eyes, outer objects, short sessions, supported persons.

4.2. Finding emotion in both places: feeling and mind

Strong emotion always presents in two places, and knowing both prevents being swept:

  • In the body (feeling): where is anger? — hot chest, trembling hands, clenched jaw. Where is anxiety? — twisted belly, shallow breathing. Direct attention to physical sensations — distinct, bounded, and changing second by second.
  • In the mind: clearly knowing "there is anger in the mind" — correctly naming, without further storytelling.

The largest wave usually passes in a few minutes when the body is clearly known place by place. What turns the situation: emotion studied instead of served or chased away — the same wave, one surfs long along it, another is taught about the sea by it.

4.3. "Observing" must not become "suppressing" — the important boundary

  • Suppressing: not letting emotion be present — snapping "must not be sad", a forced smile, rigid body, short panting breath.
  • Observing: allowing presence, seeing clearly, and not following — soft body, slow extended out-breath, correctly placed naming.

The practical challenge: choose a fitting level — emotions too large, begin with part of them (a little of the anxiety), or wait for the wave to settle then observe its trace (after anger passes, what does the body leave?). If after sittings anxiety rises prolonged, insomnia, or old emotions surface strongly — article M.A3 has a detailed adjustment table; and if conditions relate to trauma or mental health — article M.A3 and the app's medical-boundary article are where to return first.

5. Summary and next steps

  • Observing the mind's reactions: the chain object → feeling → liking / aversion → reaction → consequence; the wedge-in gap lies between feeling and leaning; train from small events, tracing reactions backward.
  • Two layers of experience: direct data and the mind-woven story (papañca); the test question "right now or another time"; clearly knowing story is also an arising – passing stream.
  • Thought as a sixth sense: three attitudes — believing, chasing away (backfiring), clearly seeing; even thoughts about meditation are only thoughts.
  • Strong emotion: step by step, with an exit; finding emotion in both feeling and mind; the boundary between observing and suppressing: allowing + not following + soft body.

Glossary:

  • Reaction chain (working term): the sequence object → feeling → liking / aversion → reaction → consequence; the everyday version of the dependent-origination chain presented in the discourses.
  • Papañca (Pali: papañca): the mind's weaving, proliferating activity — one thought giving birth to a train of interpretations, judgments, and stories.
  • Direct data (working term): what the senses and mind receive at the present, before interpretations; not a canonical term.
  • Feeling (Pali: vedanā): the pleasant – unpleasant – neutral tone appearing at contact; one establishment of mindfulness, presented separately in group D1.
  • Trauma-sensitive practice: safety principles for those with trauma history — open eyes, outer objects, short sessions, always an exit; presented in the app.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
