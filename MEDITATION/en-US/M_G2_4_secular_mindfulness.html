M.G2.4. Secular Mindfulness

The two previous articles presented practices; this article presents the basis concept standing behind them: mindfulness — especially how this concept is used in secular health programmes, where it is often written in the original English word mindfulness. This article's focus includes: mindfulness as present attention and recognition skills in non-religious contexts — and one important distinction: why it is not fully identical with canonical satipaṭṭhāna.

1. Mindfulness — from roots to current meanings

Mindfulness is the English translation of Pali sati (Pali: sati) — one factor in the Noble Eightfold Path. In recent decades, this word "left temples" becoming common words: health programmes (MBSR, MBCT — article M.G2.5), scientific research, education, enterprises... all using it as a psychology – medicine technical word, separated from religious frames.

Leaving canonical frames, definitions are also rewritten to fit new contexts. The most-cited definition is Jon Kabat-Zinn's — MBSR's initiator: mindfulness is deliberate, purposeful attention placed in the present moment, non-judgmentally. This definition is not wrong against canonical roots — purposeful attention and non-judgment are two genuine marks of mindfulness — but it narrows concepts: choosing exactly measurable and teachable parts within medical – psychological frames.

2. Mindfulness as a skill: three components

In secular contexts, mindfulness is divided into three trainable components — readers will recognize all three already met in the collection:

  • Present attention: the capacity to place attention on happening things — breath (article M.G2.2), bodies (M.G2.3), sounds (article M.C1.2), current activities (articles M.G1.1, M.G1.2) — instead of letting minds run to past or future. Trained through group B's anchor articles.
  • Recognizing present experience: knowing what one thinks, feels, and how bodies are — while those tasks are happening, not after passing. This component is trained through the "recognize — release gently — return" loop (article M.B3) and emotion observation (article M.G1.3).
  • Non-judgmental attitudes: when unpleasant things appear — drowsiness, itches, irritation — no blaming oneself, no evaluating "good – bad"; only noting. This attitude is exactly the "holding right attitudes" part already stated in meditation's very definition, and beginners' most-skipped point.

What is worth noting: the above three components demand no beliefs — anyone triable, results measurable through daily-life experience. Hence secular mindfulness is taught in hospitals, schools, and companies — places unable (and unshould) to preach sūtras.

3. Not identical with satipaṭṭhāna — why distinguishing?

Theravāda canonical Buddhism presents the four establishments of mindfulness (Pali: satipaṭṭhāna) — the four-domain observation frame of body – feeling – mind – dhammas ("Four establishments" already presented in article group M.D1). The distinction needing distinctness:

  • Meeting points: modern mindfulness originates from satipaṭṭhāna — especially through breath-mindfulness and body observation; Kabat-Zinn also plainly said his programme was inspired by Buddhist practice. The skill part (attention, recognition, non-judgment) is the shared part.
  • Differences — frames and goals: satipaṭṭhāna is a complete observation frame — four domains with sequences, with deep analytical texts (article group M.D1), aiming to see three characteristics (impermanence, suffering, not-self) leading to liberation. Secular mindfulness is an attention-skill set — not asking "who is attending", not aiming at liberation, aiming at health and daily life.
  • Differences — the "right" dimension: in Buddhism, mindfulness always stands in the Noble Eightfold Path, oriented by right view — meaning joined to right views of life and ethics. Separated from that frame, mindfulness becomes neutral skills.

This distinction is not for demoting either side: each frame has its own goals and right roles. Health-arriving persons using secular mindfulness is reasonable; persons wanting depth along the Buddhist road will need satipaṭṭhāna with its full frame (article group M.D1). The only practical issue to avoid is confusing two frames when speaking — saying "mindfulness" while listeners understand four establishments (or vice versa) causing misunderstandings about both goals and practice ways.

4. How to rightly use this concept?

Four practical guidance when meeting "mindfulness / secular mindfulness" words in daily life:

  • Reading the word's context: in medical – psychological materials, "mindfulness" means the three-component skill set in section 2; in Buddhist materials, "mindfulness" means the Noble Eightfold Path's factor. The same word, two frames — checking frames before understanding sentences.
  • Reasonable expectations: secular mindfulness programmes have evidence for some results (article M.G2.1 already listed), but are not "panacea prescriptions"; results vary by persons, and regular practice is needed to see — "life-changing" promises do not belong to this frame.
  • No need to choose sides: persons may both use secular mindfulness apps for sleep, and practise in one Buddhist tradition for depth — the two tasks do not exclude each other, provided one distinctly knows which frame is used for which purposes (the same body-mindfulness – body-scan distinguishing spirit in article M.G2.3).
  • Recognizing misuses: (1) turning mindfulness into tools "suppressing" others' or one's own feelings ("meditate, be mindful" — speaking when someone is in pain is misusing); (2) using mindfulness to avoid necessary tasks; (3) media selling "mindfulness" as miracle products — checking sources and evidence (article M.G2.11).

5. Summary and next steps

  • Mindfulness is sati's translation, "leaving religious frames" in recent decades; the common definition: deliberate, purposeful, present, non-judgmental attention (Kabat-Zinn).
  • Three trainable components: present attention, recognizing experience, non-judgmental attitudes — demanding no beliefs, measurable through daily life.
  • Not identical with satipaṭṭhāna: meeting in skill parts; differing in frames (skill sets vs. complete observation frames), goals (health vs. liberation), and the "right" dimension (right-view-oriented vs. neutral skills).
  • Rightly using: reading frames before understanding words; reasonable expectations; no need to choose sides; avoiding three widespread misuses.

Glossary:

  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
  • Mindfulness (Pali: sati): remembering and staying with what is happening right now; in Buddhist frames a Noble Eightfold Path factor, in secular frames an attention – recognition skill set.
  • Four establishments of mindfulness (Pali: satipaṭṭhāna): the four-domain observation frame — body, feeling, mind, dhammas — presented in group M.D1 articles.
  • Noble Eightfold Path (Pali: ariya aṭṭhaṅgika magga): the Buddhist road's eight factors, in which mindfulness is one factor — presented in article A1.
  • Right view (Pali: sammā diṭṭhi): right views — the Noble Eightfold Path's guiding factor; mindfulness is oriented by right view.
  • MBSR (Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction): the mindfulness-based stress-reduction programme, eight weeks — presented in article G2.5.
  • MBCT (Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy): mindfulness-based cognitive therapy — presented in article G2.5.
  • Three characteristics (Pali: tilakkhaṇa): impermanence, suffering, not-self — the insight-meditation road's observation goals — presented in article C2.3.
  • Three trainings (Pali: tisso sikkhā): ethics – concentration – wisdom — presented in article A1.
