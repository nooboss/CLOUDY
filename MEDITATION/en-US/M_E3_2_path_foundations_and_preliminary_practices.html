M.E3.2. Path Foundations and Preliminary Practices

This article merges the whole cluster M.E3.2 — Vajrayāna Buddhism's path foundations and preliminary practices, that is "block 1" in the four-block map erected in article E3.1. The cluster has three sub-articles at three different levels: meditations developing bodhicitta (M077 — L3), stage-graded analytical meditations (M144 — L4), and preliminary practices — ngöndro (M143 — L5, presented only at educational level). These three pieces are the part shared with Mahāyāna — conditions for entering the Tantric practice floor.

1. Meditations developing bodhicitta

Bodhicitta (Sanskrit: bodhicitta — "the mind directed toward awakening for all beings") was already introduced in article M.E2.1 and trained through lojong in article E2.6. In Vajrayāna, it keeps the role of the whole path's motive — Tantric-floor methods are not regarded as "buying quick results" for oneself, but erected on the vow: attaining awakening to gain capacity to help all beings.

Treatises divide bodhicitta into two levels:

  • Aspirational bodhicitta — the strong wish "I want to attain awakening for beings"; the seed, not yet with deeds.
  • Engaged bodhicitta — bringing wishes into genuine deeds: keeping precepts, practising six perfections, helping others through concrete work; the seed already sprouted into a tree.

Meditations developing bodhicitta have two parts: vowing (using reasons to kindle and nourish wishes) and contemplating (examining life situations so wishes are no longer hollow sentences). There are two classical methods, whose names readers will meet when reading books:

  • Sevenfold cause-and-effect instruction: the contemplation chain beginning with "all beings were once my relatives" → remembering kindness → wishing to repay kindness → giving birth to loving-kindness – compassion → giving birth to bodhicitta.
  • Exchanging self and others: reversing the "prioritizing for myself" habit — systematized in Śāntideva's Entering Bodhisattva Conduct, chapter VIII.

This practice directly interfaces with the four divine abodes — loving-kindness, compassion, joy, equanimity (group M.D2) — and learned lojong (article M.E2.6): the same material, differing in frame — here placed as the Vajrayāna path's official opening step. Because L3 level, this is the cluster's accessible part: readers can understand and train at the shared level (radiating loving-kindness — article M.D2.1 — the nearest basis).

2. Stage-graded analytical meditations (Lamrim)

Lamrim (Tibetan: lam rim — "stages of the path") is the contemplation system through ordered topics, designed for the mind to gradually shift in exactly the order — like stairs with steps, no skipping. Origins: Atiśa's Lamp for the Path to Awakening (Sanskrit: Bodhipathapradīpa) — already met in article M.E2.6 with lojong — later expanded into Tsongkhapa's (1357–1419) massive teaching corpus — the Gelug lineage.

The representative sequence:

  • Precious human rebirth: viewing one's available conditions — a human body, meeting the teaching — to give birth to cherishing and non-wasting;
  • Impermanence (Sanskrit: anitya): human life short, death hour unscheduled — giving birth to rightly urgent minds;
  • Karma (Sanskrit: karma): deeds have consequences — giving birth to careful minds toward what one does;
  • Saṃsāra's suffering (Sanskrit: saṃsāra): viewing the suffering round's whole — giving birth to minds wanting to exit entirely, not merely patching;
  • Bodhicitta: on that basis, opening wishes out to all beings — joining section 1.

Many teaching versions add one opening step: right confidence in guiding teachers — because the whole sequence is transmitted through teachers (article M.A6 already spoke of choosing and working with teachers). Lamrim's large organizing structure is the "three scopes": lower scope — caring for future fruition; middle scope — wishing to exit saṃsāra entirely; higher scope — acting for all beings; the five topics above spread across these three motive levels.

M144's most important technical point is distinguishing two meditation kinds lamrim alternately uses:

  • Analytical meditation: using argument to examine one topic — asking, weighing, taking examples — until the mind gives birth to a genuinely convinced feeling (for example analysing impermanence until genuinely urgent, not only knowing theoretically);
  • Placement meditation: after convinced feelings appear, placing the mind resting calmly on that feeling — no further analysis, letting it permeate.

Like baking cakes: analysing is kneading dough, placement is letting cakes rise. Those already learning wise attention (article M.B4) will find it familiar: the same using intellect to redirect the mind, differing in that lamrim is a complete ordered programme with fixed topics and order. Analysis – placement is also the organizing pair of insight meditation in Vajrayāna — met again in article E3.3.

3. Preliminary practices (Ngöndro)

Ngöndro (Tibetan: sngon 'gro; Vietnamese: preliminary practices — "deeds before entering the principal") is a set of five preparatory practice methods, standing before Tantric-floor methods. The standard structure (presented at educational level):

  • Refuge – prostrations: clarifying direction — confidence in Buddha, Dhamma, Saṅgha — with bodily prostration forms;
  • Bodhicitta: exactly section 1's method — establishing motive;
  • Vajrasattva (Sanskrit: vajrasattva): the purifying method — clearing accumulated bad traces, like washing jars before holding clear water;
  • Maṇḍala offerings (Sanskrit: maṇḍala): the merit-accumulating method — pictorially offering the whole universe, training to release holding greed;
  • Guru yoga (meditation contemplating the teacher): the transmission-blessing-receiving method — joining the lineage through teachers; detailed contents belong to article M.E3.4 (M076).

Ngöndro's characteristic point is distinct quantification: each method usually performed to fixed counts — commonly around one hundred thousand times each, varying by tradition — like a "construction project" with milestones. Hence the structure: when deep practice appears, ngöndro-completers have three things ready in hand — partly purified obstacles, accumulated merit, and opened lineage connections — hence the later road has fewer barriers.

Two mandatory notes (L5 level):

  • The app only presents structure for understanding — no ngöndro self-training guidance; the method is learned and done through teachers of specific traditions (article M.A6), because each lineage has different texts, counting ways, and details.
  • Physical safety: large-number prostrations are genuine burdens for backs, knees, and cardiovascular systems — those with health problems need discussing with teachers and doctors first (the shared spirit of article M.A3 and posture — article M.A4).

4. Three pieces forming one foundation — and what readers carry home

Placing the three pieces side by side shows un-overlapping roles:

  • Bodhicitta answers "why go" — outward-to-beings motive;
  • Lamrim answers "in which order to go" — the topic map and mind-transforming sequence;
  • Ngöndro answers "what to prepare before departing" — quantified practice construction.

Why this foundation is genuinely needed rather than prolonged ritual? Because Tantric-floor methods (M.E3.4, M.E3.5) all assume these three already available: great motive (bodhicitta), minds passed through shifting steps (lamrim), and clean – firm bases (ngöndro). Lacking the basis, upper floors are not only difficult but skewed — exactly the "tighter entry conditions" logic discussed in article E3.1.

For Vietnamese readers following no tradition, the carry-home level is very practical:

  • Training motive through group M.D2 (loving-kindness — article M.D2.1; compassion — article M.D2.2) and lojong (article M.E2.6) — the same material as bodhicitta, needing no Tantric frame;
  • Analysing impermanence and karma in section 2's spirit — near wise attention (article M.B4) and arising – passing observation (article M.C2.3) previous articles already have;
  • Not self-doing ngöndro per book descriptions — if genuinely interested, the right step is finding teachers and traditions (article M.A6), and reading the "warning signs" part to choose healthy places.

5. Summary and next steps

Summary:

  • Bodhicitta — the path's motive; two levels aspirational and engaged; two classical methods: seven causes – effects, exchanging self and others; interfacing with group M.D2 and lojong.
  • Lamrim — stage-ordered contemplation: precious human rebirth → impermanence → karma → saṃsāra suffering → bodhicitta, organized through "three scopes"; the core technique: analytical meditation then placement meditation.
  • Ngöndro — five preliminary methods (refuge – prostrations, bodhicitta, Vajrasattva, maṇḍala, guru yoga) with large quantification; purposes: purifying – merit-gathering – lineage-joining; educational level — no self-training.
  • The three pieces are "block 1" of article M.E3.1's map: shared conditions for entering the Tantric floor — genuine basis, not ritual.

Suggested next reading: the next cluster per the map is M.E3.3 — Calm Abiding and Insight in Vajrayāna: calm abiding (Sanskrit: śamatha — M072) and insight (Sanskrit: vipaśyanā — M073) — two familiar methods of groups C–D, seeing how they integrate into the Vajrayāna path. See you there.

Glossary:

  • Bodhicitta (Sanskrit): awakened mind — the mind directed toward awakening for all beings; two levels: aspirational and engaged.
  • Lamrim (Tibetan: lam rim): "stages of the path" — the ordered contemplation system, originating with Atiśa, expanded by Tsongkhapa.
  • Three scopes: three motive levels organizing lamrim — lower (good fruition), middle (exiting saṃsāra), higher (for beings).
  • Ngöndro (Tibetan: sngon 'gro): preliminary practices — five preparatory methods before Tantric-floor methods.
  • Vajrasattva (Sanskrit): the purifying method in ngöndro.
  • Maṇḍala (Sanskrit): the universe image used in offering methods; also the name of diagrams used as meditation subjects in some traditions.
  • Guru yoga: "meditation uniting with the teacher" — lineage joining; in detail in article E3.4.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
