M.G1.7. Group and Community Practice

The previous article widened practice to families; this article further widens to groups and communities: sitting together with a few friends, joining temple meditation classes, or keeping rhythms with online groups. This article's focus has four parts: benefits of sitting together, how to organize small groups, minimal rituals enough for use, and how to join online groups while keeping practice quality. An L2-level article — directly using learned basis articles, adding no new concepts.

1. Why is sitting together different from sitting alone?

Practising with others is not only "more crowded fun" — it substantially changes sittings through three mechanisms:

  • Shared rhythms raising private rhythms: when whole groups follow one bell, one guidance, each person's rhythms are pulled back to the shared axis. Habitual abandoners keep longer; jerky sitters sit more fully. Hence retreats are so effective: practice quantity and evenness increase through shared contexts.
  • Someone awaiting oneself: appointments with oneself easily abandoned; appointments with groups harder to abandon. For those already learning habit-keeping principles, groups are natural "anti-missing mechanisms" — all self-reminders lose to one vacant place awaiting in rooms.
  • Misunderstandings spoken out and dismantled: post-sitting sharing intervals are where "only I suffer this" questions hear "me too" answers. Seeing one's own difficulties as shared difficulties — by itself reducing one stress layer; and groups' predecessors often have rightly leveled practical suggestions (exactly the good-friend spirit — fellow travellers on the path in article M.A6).

The canon early records companions' roles: when one has good friends, good companions, and good communities, one may expect the Noble Eightfold Path cultivated and fulfilled. [Source: Meghiya Sutta, Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 9.3 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

2. Organizing small groups

Small groups (three to ten persons) are the easiest forms to organize for lay practitioners — no centres, no costs needed. Common experiences when beginning:

  • Even rhythms more precious than sizes: four-person groups meeting evenly weekly endure more than twenty-person groups meeting when free or not. Choosing one fixed hour (for example Sunday mornings) and keeping that rhythm like personal "fixed hour" rules.
  • Choosing simple places: tidied living rooms, backyard corners, companies' or residential areas' shared rooms. Minimum conditions: enough seats for everyone, relatively calm, and uninterrupted mid-hours.
  • Rotating roles: each week one person "keeping sessions" — opening bells, reading short guidance, closing bells — so groups do not depend on one single person; session-keepers are also most trained. Groups need no "classroom experts": small groups self-run through this very collection's articles.
  • Choosing articles beforehand, not during hours: each session should have distinct topics from week starts (for example "this week doing group B's releasing article and article M.G1.4") — groups choosing articles during meetings waste time and easily run after momentary opinions.
  • Accepting small and level-skewed groups: beginners sitting beside the familiar — normal business benefiting both; not "awaiting same-level persons" endlessly delaying beginnings.

3. Minimal rituals for group sessions

"Rituals" here are not school rites — only fixed sequences helping group sessions run smoothly and everyone knowing where they are. One minimal forty-five-minute template:

  • Opening sessions (2 minutes): session-keepers striking three bells — mindfulness bells; everyone stopping, hearing until bell sounds end. Three shared breaths for "entering hours".
  • Principal articles (20–25 minutes): guided short practices or silent sitting through one preselected article (one group B article, or article M.G1.4). Beginners may open eyes doing hearing articles — no sitting-how requirements.
  • Short walking meditation or moving (5 minutes): with permitting spaces, one slow knowing round — alternating movements helping drowsy-prone persons keep alert.
  • Short sharing (10 minutes): each person one to two sentences: "today doing which article, seeing how". The important rule: sharing experience, no advising – no arguing – no judging; those unwilling to speak saying "not sharing today" — also fine.
  • Closing sessions (2 minutes): one bell, one session-keeper summary sentence for next sessions (which articles, where), finished.

The whole above sequence depends on no schools; if group members follow one particular lineage (nearby temples, one tradition already learned in groups E or F), chanting – rite parts of that lineage may be added — provided everyone agrees together and added parts do not push sessions too long.

4. Online groups — usable, with four conditions

Online groups (joint-sitting calls, schedule-reminding groups, sharing forums) are the easiest approach for those without nearby groups. Online practice quality differs from direct sessions yet remains genuine — if four conditions are kept:

  • Fixed hours and camera-on (or off) commitments: online groups most easily disband because "everyone free" — the countermeasure keeps the fixed-hour principle intact; groups may choose camera-on commitments to create shared presence, or no-camera groups also fine — provided distinct from the start, whoever wanting which kinds entering which groups.
  • Distinct, same weekly guidance articles: online groups lack shared spaces, hence guidance-article accuracy replaces spaces: the same recordings or written guidance articles — everyone doing exactly one article together.
  • Sharing keeping right rules: post-sitting chat sessions easily turning into health discussions, method judgments, or course advertisements — healthy groups keep exactly section 3's three no's (no advising, no arguing, no judging) on chat platforms too.
  • Privacy and private boundaries: not sharing what one does not want stored as words; groups recording sharing sessions must ask opinions first; if groups gradually shift to inviting paid retreats – courses, forcing purchases, or self-appointing teachers – proclaiming attainments — those are warning signs already listed in article M.A6: withdrawing.

One straight observation: online groups supplement, not fully replacing direct meetings; when possible, occasionally meeting in person (monthly) or joining one shared retreat for groups to have genuine "anchors".

5. Summary and next steps

  • Sitting together has three genuine mechanisms: shared rhythms raising private rhythms, someone awaiting oneself, and misunderstandings spoken out — the canonical basis is good friendship.
  • Small three-to-ten-person groups: even rhythms more precious than sizes, simple places, rotating roles, choosing articles beforehand, accepting level skews.
  • Forty-five-minute minimal rituals: opening bells → principal articles → short walking meditation → short sharing under "no advising – no arguing – no judging" rules → closing bells.
  • Online groups usable with four conditions: fixed hours, distinct guidance articles, rightly ruled sharing, privacy and boundaries — meeting warning signs, withdrawing; online supplementing, not replacing direct meetings.

Glossary:

  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
  • Good friends (fellow travellers helping one grow wholesome - Pali: kalyāṇamitta): good companions on the path — fellow practitioners, guiding teachers, co-practising communities.
  • Mindfulness bells: bells used as signals to stop and breathe mindfully — opening and closing group sessions is a widespread use.
  • Walking meditation (Pali: caṅkama): walking meditation — practising mindfulness while walking slowly, on par with sitting meditation.
  • Group sessions: shared practice sessions with fixed sequences — unlike retreats (multi-day) and classes (with lectures).
  • Claims of attainment: proclaiming high practice fruitions attained; persons or organizations using such claims to attract and keep people are warning signs (article M.A6).
