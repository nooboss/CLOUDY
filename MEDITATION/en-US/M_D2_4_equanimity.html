M.D2.4. Equanimity (Upekkhā / Upekṣā)

The group's three previous articles traversed loving-kindness – compassion – appreciative joy: three warm movements of mind toward others. The fourth quality — equanimity — keeps the other three from burning, and is simultaneously the basis of all: balance before gain – loss, praise – blame. This is also the most easily misread word of the four: equanimity is not indifference, not giving up. This article covers: what equanimity is, what it is not, two meanings of equanimity in the canon, how to practise, equanimity in daily life, and summary.

1. What is equanimity?

Equanimity (Pali: upekkhā, Sanskrit: upekṣā) is the mind's balance before life's rising – falling pairs. The canon summarizes those pairs as the eight worldly conditions — the discourse image is eight winds blowing back and forth asking no one's permission: gain – loss, fame – disrepute, praise – blame, pleasure – pain. [Source: Lokadhamma Sutta, Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 8.5 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

Untrained in equanimity, the mind is like a rudderless boat — every wind pushes: gain, elated; loss, collapsed; praise, flying; blame, burning. Equanimity is the rudder: the mind still distinctly knows each wind (not unseen!), but is not pulled tilting. Within the four-quality system, equanimity counteracts attachment (Pali: rāga): equanimity practitioners stand firm amid gain and loss without hearts burning along either side. [Source: Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 46.54 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

A quick formula: equanimity = full clear knowing + not leaning along. Dropping one side skews: full knowing yet leaning along — the ordinary mind; not leaning yet unclearly knowing — indifference (section 2).

2. What equanimity is not

  • Not indifference. Equanimity's near enemy — most resembling disguise — is unwise indifference: a cold face, dark eyes, "whatever". The Theravāda commentaries separate the two very distinctly: equanimity sees fully then balances; indifference does not look — equanimity is clear knowing's fruit, indifference haziness's verdict. The commentary image: a mother of four — a mother loving four children of four ages (nursing, toddling, running, grown) — loving none more than others, bored with none; her "even contentment" shifts with each circumstance, still loving fully — that is equanimity, not coldness. [Source: Visuddhimagga, ch. IX - Theravāda Buddhism.]
  • Not giving up. Giving up abandons one's part; equanimity does one's full part then releases the fruit part — the two words differ in that equanimity abandons no one. Equanimity practitioners still heal, still teach, still stand up to do what must be done — only not flipping to check "praised yet, succeeded yet" every five minutes.
  • Not avoidance. Avoiding leaves the playing table to spare shame; equanimity sits at the table while ups and downs cannot uproot it. Avoiding is fear's condition; equanimity courage's capacity.
  • Not suppressing feelings. Suppressing is forced smiling while boiling inside; equanimity is genuinely wholesome — feelings may still arise (pain still pain, irritation still irritation); equanimity's trainable part is recovery time and the mind's leaning degree.

3. Two meanings of equanimity (Upekkhā)

Upekkhā in canon and commentaries is used for two different phenomena, very easily mixed by meditation readers. The app's taxonomy requires this article to clarify both:

  • Equanimity as a quality toward others — a divine abode: the mind's balance before situations and persons — gain and loss, praise and blame, loved and hated persons. The commentaries call this equanimity with "beings as objects": the attitude's balance viewing persons and life.
  • Equanimity as a factor of mind — a jhāna and awakening factor: the same word pointing to the ripened state of mind itself in meditation: (a) one of five jhāna factors (Pali: jhānaṅga) at the fourth jhāna level, when both rapture and bliss recede leaving pure balance, mindfulness thereby purified; (b) one of the seven awakening factors — standing last, the basis of fully nourished mindfulness. [Source: Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 46 - Theravāda Buddhism; the fourth jhāna presented per the Dīgha Nikāya.]

How they differ, briefly: divine-abode equanimity is a balanced view outward toward others — in which meditators' feelings may still at times be glad or sad; jhāna / awakening-factor equanimity is the balanced state of sensing and knowing itself — not only viewing but the inner "tone" also purely even. The two relate (the second deeper, nourished by the first) but are not identical. Hence beginners should not, seeing in books "equanimity leading to the fourth jhāna", think they must "train feelings flat" — confusing the two meanings (deeper warnings in articles M.C1.4 and M.C2.5).

4. Equanimity meditation — how to practise

The method shares loving-kindness – compassion – joy meditation's structure, differing in objects and accompanying reflection. The Theravāda commentaries note distinctly: equanimity meditation is hard to begin with loved persons (the near enemy of affection easily rises first) — recommending starting from neutral living persons. [Source: Visuddhimagga, ch. IX - Theravāda Buddhism.]

  • Objects: one neutral person — neither loved nor hated, whose life's ups and downs you roughly know.
  • Foundational reflection: the commentary formula — "beings are owners of their actions" (Pali: kammassakatā): each person bears and enjoys the fruits of what they do — however much you love them you cannot replace that part for them, however much you release them you cannot erase the part they themselves made. This reflection does not cool you — it makes you hold less of what is not yours, leaving your part (goodwill, right-measure action) intact.
  • Sample phrases: "May you keep a balanced mind before gain – loss. May you not be pulled along by praise – blame. May you stand firm before pleasure – pain."
  • Sequence: neutral persons → oneself → loved persons (retrying with the reflection foundation) → difficult persons → all beings. The commentaries note: equanimity meditation at a collected level leads to the fourth jhāna (not the first three) — because its objects demand a deeply levelled mind — details left to article C1.4.

5. Equanimity in daily life

  • Recounting the eight winds in one's own life. A practical exercise: listing in turn gain – loss (money, jobs, opportunities), fame – disrepute (reputation, image), praise – blame (others' words), pleasure – pain (experiences) — in the past week which "wind" pushed strongest? Not for evaluating, but for knowing one's principal wind — sensitive people differ; training equanimity at the most-skewed place.
  • Doing one's part, releasing fruits. The formula placed into work and children: preparing fully, fulfilling responsibility, dedicating rightly — then results belong to all conditions combined, not oneself alone. This is not fatalism; this is how to sustain long work — those tightly gripping fruits exhaust first (articles M.A6, M.C2.6).
  • Equanimity with metrics. Likes, views, rankings — modern forms of praise – blame and gain – loss. No need to abandon (if work needs), but choosing when to view instead of viewing every beat — the same environment-design principle of articles M.A6 and M.D2.3 section 5.
  • Equanimity with loved ones. Equanimity's hardest level is those one loves — because affection is greatest, equanimity hardest. The distinction helps breathing: equanimity is not loving less, but holding less of what is not yours (section 4) — grown children must bump themselves, friends must choose themselves, aging parents must bear aging's part; one's own right part remains: presence, right-measure help, unchanged even love.
  • Self-check: after bad news, is the mind's recovery time to steadiness shorter than last month? The mind's leaning degree (a five-minute storm or a two-day storm) is equanimity's progress sign — recordable in the journal, not a "perhaps felt" thing.

6. Summary and next steps

  • Equanimity is balance before the eight worldly winds — fully clearly knowing and not leaning along; counteracting attachment and clinging.
  • Equanimity is not unwise indifference (near enemy), not giving up, not avoidance, not suppressing feelings — the commentaries' mother-of-four image summarizes the spirit.
  • Two meanings of upekkhā: divine-abode equanimity — the balanced view outward toward others; jhāna / awakening-factor equanimity — the ripened state of mind itself at the fourth jhāna and among the seven awakening factors. Related but not identical.
  • Equanimity meditation begins with neutral persons, the foundational reflection "beings are owners of their actions"; in daily life: knowing one's principal wind, doing one's part and releasing fruits, holding less of what is not yours with loved ones.

Glossary:

  • Equanimity (Pali: upekkhā, Sanskrit: upekṣā): the mind's balance before gain – loss, praise – blame, pleasure – pain.
  • Eight worldly conditions (Pali: aṭṭha lokadhammā): eight gain-and-loss pairs — gain, loss, fame, disrepute, praise, blame, pleasure, pain — the discourses' image is eight winds.
  • Attachment (Pali: rāga): the pull drawing the mind to cling to what it wants; what equanimity counteracts.
  • Unwise indifference: a cold face from not seeing — equanimity's near enemy; entirely unlike equanimity as balance after clearly seeing.
  • Beings are owners of their actions (Pali: kammassakatā): the reflection formula — each person bears and enjoys the fruits of their own deeds; equanimity meditation's foundation.
  • Jhāna factors (Pali: jhānaṅga): components constituting jhāna; equanimity is a factor of the fourth jhāna (article M.C1.4).
  • Awakening factors (Pali: bojjhaṅga): seven factors of awakening; equanimity is the seventh awakening factor (article M.D1.4).
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
