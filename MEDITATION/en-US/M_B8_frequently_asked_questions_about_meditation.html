1. On posture and duration

How long is enough to sit?

Short but regular beats long but erratic. A reasonable start: 10–15 minutes a day; keeping the thread matters more than lengthening each sitting. On the busiest day: the minimum of "sitting down and breathing five counted breaths" — keeping the thread is the goal (articles M.A1, M.A6). 1–5 minute practices through the day are supplements, not replacements for the main sitting (article M.G1.4).

Eyes closed or open?

Either is fine — choose by condition: prone to drowsiness → half-open or open, gaze lowered to the floor; mind often running outward → closing helps gather inward. Those with a trauma background: fully open eyes are entirely valid and usually safer (articles M.A4, M.G2.10). There is no mandatory "standard".

What if the legs hurt?

Distinguish two kinds: ordinary postural pain (tingling, aching — slowly changing posture with awareness is a legitimate exercise; alternative postures include half-lotus, sitting on a chair — article M.A4) and alarm pain (prolonged numbness, sharply increasing pain, pain persisting after rising — change posture at once, review the sitting posture; article M.A3). No need to "battle" pain to prove anything; sitting on a chair is still complete sitting meditation.

What if I keep nodding off?

Drowsiness while sitting is very common. Three responses in order: check whether you sleep enough (meditation does not replace sleep); shift sittings to your most alert hour (usually early morning); and change posture — sit straighter, open / half-open the eyes, or switch to walking meditation (Pali: caṅkama) (articles M.A4, M.B5). If drowsiness appears only when sitting while daytime stays alert — review food intake before sitting and room ventilation.

2. On what happens while sitting

May I listen to music?

Distinguish three cases: background music for comfort — not meditation; usable for relaxation but do not call it practice; recorded guidance — helpful when newly learning, but gradually shift to unguided sitting (article M.G1.8); sound as subject — a genuine meditation: hearing all sounds present, without sorting or grading (article M.C1.2). Long-term, silence is the deepest training environment.

The more I sit the more scattered the mind — what am I doing wrong?

The opposite — you are beginning to see clearly. The mind was scattered all day; previously unknown, now known — that is mindfulness increasing, not the mind breaking. Meeting many thoughts in the early phase is the most normal thing for everyone in every tradition (articles M.A2, M.B3). The single rule of handling: see — release gently — return to the subject; do not blame yourself (article M.B3).

Seeing nothing at all — no lights, no strange feelings — is that wrong?

Not wrong — that is the most normal thing. Lights and visions (Pali: nimitta) are only phenomena that may occur, not a measure of progress; hunting them is one of the common misconceptions warned about from the start (articles M.A2, M.C1.3). The only true measure lies in daily life: less impulsiveness, earlier recognition of distraction, faster emotional recovery (article M.B7).

Some sittings are bad — have I lost the rhythm?

"Bad" sittings (scattered, mind everywhere, finishing feeling nothing gained) are half of practice — everyone has them, including long-term practitioners. Your chain of sittings is the large picture; one bad square does not break the picture. Sitting again tomorrow in a small amount is enough — the familiar rule: never miss two days in a row (articles M.A6, M.B7).

3. On choices, confidence, and next steps

Does meditation conflict with my faith?

It depends which part you use: secular mindfulness — breathing exercises, body scans, relaxation — is presented independently of religion and demands no belief (articles M.G2.1, M.G2.4). The Buddhist meditation part of this collection is presented neutrally for study — followers of any religion can take the skills part; whichever part belongs to belief, each article states clearly so you can decide yourself. This collection does not encourage anyone's religious conversion.

Does the app replace a teacher?

No. The app teaches foundations, keeps rhythm, looks things up — its true role (articles M.G1.8, M.G2.5); but every in-depth system needs a direct guide — why and how to choose a trustworthy one was presented in articles M.A6 and G2.12. The app is designed to lead you to where a person is needed — not to replace that place.

Small children at home, a packed schedule — can I begin?

Yes — a path for each circumstance already has its article: the very busy attach practice to "anchors" (article M.G1.5); families with children have age-graded articles and the principle of never forcing (article M.G1.6); small groups or online groups if you want companionship (article M.G1.7).

Where to start if newly arrived?

Suggested sequence: group A (understanding and safety) → group B (foundational skills) → practice along each later group's path. No rigid order required — every article is always retrievable through the app's reference section (articles M.A1, M.A3 are the two to read first).

Glossary:

  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
  • Walking meditation (Pali: caṅkama): walking meditation — practising mindfulness while walking slowly, on par with sitting meditation.
  • Nimitta (Pali: nimitta): images or lights that may appear when the mind settles deeply — phenomena that may occur, not a measure of progress.
  • Secular mindfulness: the skills of attention and recognition presented independently of religion — distinguished from mindfulness in the Buddhist framework in article G2.4.
  • Half-lotus: the sitting posture with one foot placed on the opposite thigh — one of the complete alternative postures (article M.A4).
  • Meditation subject (Pali: kammaṭṭhāna): the chosen object on which the mind is placed in practice.
