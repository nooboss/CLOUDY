M.D3.2. Kasiṇa Devices — The Ten Kasiṇas

Within the forty-subject map, the first and "deepest-collectedness" group is the ten kasiṇas — visual-imagery subjects presented in the Theravāda commentarial system. This is the 40 system's most purely technical group: little moral or emotional meaning like recollections or divine abodes, but a coarse collectedness-training machine — simple, stable objects demanding persistent mind-holding. Contents: what kasiṇa is, what the ten subjects include, basic use, place in the 40 system and the collectedness road, and what to know before further study.

1. What is kasiṇa?

Kasiṇa (Pali: kasiṇa) is coloured circles or natural materials used for developing deep collectedness — meditators make a simple object (a clay earth circle, a water dish, a fire area...), viewing it as the eyes hold the mind, until that image reappears distinctly even with eyes closed. Kasiṇa per the commentaries means "totality, whole" — the subject covering the entire visual field, with no other part intervening; hence it trains collectedness to "only one single object remaining".

Within the 40-subject system (article M.D3.1), the ten kasiṇas stand first and all lead to all four form-sphere jhānas — the collectedness road's highest level; eight of ten (except fire and light — varying by source) are also the basis for opening four formless bases. If the breath is a "soft" subject — joined to life, changing with mood — kasiṇa is a "hard" subject: entirely unchanging, unemotional, demanding high patience. [Source: Visuddhimagga, ch. IV–V - Theravāda Buddhism.]

2. The ten kasiṇa subjects

Ten subjects divide into two groups — four elements and six colours:

  • 1. Earth kasiṇa (Pali: paṭhavīkasiṇa): a tray of brown earth, flat surface — the "standard" subject for learning kasiṇa use.
  • 2. Water kasiṇa (Pali: āpokasiṇa): a bowl of clear water or a still water dish.
  • 3. Fire kasiṇa (Pali: tejokasiṇa): a fire area or oil-lamp flame — the canon records fire kasiṇas once used to dispel cold and sensual desire.
  • 4. Air kasiṇa (Pali: vāyokasiṇa): a distinctive subject — viewing not objects but wind's effects (bending rice, rippling water, flickering candle).
  • 5–8. Colour kasiṇas (Pali: nīla-pīta-lohita-odāta kasiṇa): blue, yellow, red, white — pure colour circles, unjoined to concrete objects; these colour discs are also subjects for hating-natured persons (article overview section 4).
  • 9. Light kasiṇa (Pali: ālokakasiṇa): light gaps, even light areas — near the white kasiṇa.
  • 10. Space kasiṇa (Pali: ākāsakasiṇa): unobstructed sky regions — "empty space" as subject.

The four elemental kasiṇas (earth, water, fire, air) later also join four-elements contemplation: working with the same four basic qualities of body – world, differing in goal — kasiṇa aiming at collectedness, four elements aiming at wisdom.

3. Basic use per the commentarial system

The presentation below is an overview-level description for understanding essence; practising kasiṇa per the commentaries demands distinct guidance sources — see section 5.

  • Step 1 — making the subject: making or choosing objects per commentarial standards (flat surface, pure colour, fitting size — like a handspan clay disc); placed in moderate light, about one-and-a-half handspans away.
  • Step 2 — gazing hold: sitting steadily, eyes open viewing the subject wholly — not examining details — several minutes; mind wandering, gently returning (the recognize – release – return loop).
  • Step 3 — eyes-closed hold: eyes closed, holding the subject's image in mind. Initially hazy, distorted, fleeting images — normal; eyes open to view again then eyes closed to hold again — alternating many rounds.
  • Step 4 — sign appearing: through the commentaries' three sign stages: preparatory sign — the seen image; acquired sign — the image appearing distinctly even with eyes closed; counterpart sign — the pure, firm image appearing when the mind has deeply settled. The counterpart sign is access concentration and jhāna's basis.
  • Step 5 — expanding: at a deep level, practitioners "widen" the sign from disc-size to cover all directions — the technique leading to formless levels with space kasiṇa and fitting kasiṇas.

4. Kasiṇa on the collectedness road: from sign to formless bases

Kasiṇa is the 40 system's sole branch walking the entire long road: material subject → sign → access concentration → four form-sphere jhānas → (with 8/10 subjects) four formless bases. Hence in commentarial-system monasteries (for example the Pa-Auk lineage — article M.E1.3), kasiṇa is the typical tool for developing deep collectedness as the basis for insight meditation in the "concentration as vehicle" model.

What is worth noting from the canon: kasiṇa almost never appears in the canonical basket's meditation sections — discourses speak much of breath, four establishments, divine abodes, but kasiṇa is a system systematized by commentaries (Visuddhimagga); using these subjects is therefore tightly joined to the commentaries' transmission sources. This is also why the taxonomy reminds: detailed guidance needs distinct traditional sources — the above list and step numbers only convey essence, insufficient for deep self-training.

5. Who should study — and who need not yet

  • Not yet hurried: beginners, lay practitioners not heading along the jhāna road — breath, bodily sensations, sounds, loving-kindness already supply the basis for all beginner needs; kasiṇa adds nothing to this phase.
  • Should know through: whoever reading Theravāda meditation books continuously meets the word kasiṇa — this article is the needed map; and those interested in the deep-collectedness road in the concentration-as-vehicle model.
  • Needing a teacher: whoever genuinely wants to train kasiṇa — entering retreats with direct guidance following a distinct transmission lineage (articles M.A6, M.A3); self-training by books easily errs at basic places (making subjects, handling signs) and wastes unnecessary time.
  • Not required: the 40 system is the commentaries' map, not every Buddhist's required list; many virtuous monastics and modern meditation lineages never follow kasiṇa yet still go very far on the establishments – insight road.

6. Summary and next steps

  • Kasiṇa is coloured-circle / natural-material subjects training deep collectedness — kasiṇa meaning "totality": subjects covering the visual field, leaving the mind only one object.
  • Ten subjects: earth, water, fire, air + blue, yellow, red, white + light, space — all leading to all four jhāna levels; 8/10 opening the formless-base road.
  • Use through the chain: making subjects → gazing hold → eyes-closed hold → three sign stages → expanding — but detailed guidance needs distinct traditional sources; kasiṇa is the commentaries' system, not the canonical basket's central meditation part.
  • For beginners and lay practitioners: knowing through is enough; the beginner meditation basis needs no kasiṇa.

Glossary:

  • Kasiṇa (Pali: kasiṇa): coloured-circle / natural-material subjects training deep collectedness; original meaning "totality, wholeness".
  • Preparatory / acquired / counterpart sign (Pali: parikamma / uggaha / paṭibhāga nimitta): three stages of the subject's image per the commentaries — in detail in article C1.3.
  • Formless bases (Pali: āruppa): four imageless collectedness levels developed from kasiṇa — in detail in article C1.4.
  • Physical subject: real objects made / chosen per commentarial standards as gazing-hold subjects.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
