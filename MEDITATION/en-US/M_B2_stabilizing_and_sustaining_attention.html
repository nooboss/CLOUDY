M.B2. Stabilizing and Sustaining Attention

The previous article taught the three starting skills: releasing bodily tension, noticing the natural breath, and counting the breath. Counting is like training wheels on a bicycle — there comes a time to let go. This article answers the next question: once the counting is released, how does attention stay on the object without being pulled away? It contains two successive skills: following the sensations of the breath — moving from counting to directly feeling each breath, and anchoring the mind on an object — principles for choosing a stable object and how to bring attention back each time it is pulled away. Together these two form the single capacity the canon calls concentration (Pali: samādhi).

1. Following the sensations of the breath

1.1. From counting to direct sensing

Counting is only a temporary scaffold. Once the mind follows the breath to some extent — you still remember the numbers, the breath is not "beautified" by the counting — release the counting and place mindfulness (Pali: sati) directly on the sensations of the breath: now breathing in; now breathing out. This operation is generally called mindfulness of breathing (Pali: ānāpānassati), and the sequence of "knowing long and short → feeling the whole → calming" is exactly the opening steps presented in the discourse devoted to the breath. [Source: Ānāpānasati Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 118 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

1.2. Following one complete breath cycle

At a finer level, follow one whole breath rather than merely labelling "in – out":

  • In-breath: beginning (the breath starts entering), middle, end (the breath gradually exhausts).
  • The small pause between out- and in-breath — if naturally present, know it; no need to create a pause.
  • Out-breath: beginning – middle – end, with the feeling of the body softening along the out-breath.

Following the whole cycle fills the "gaps" — where the mind usually slips out unnoticed. If sensations are unclear part by part, simply knowing generally "coming in – going out" is enough; the word "whole body" in the discourse text is understood by commentators in two ways (the whole breath, or the whole physical body) — for beginners, closely following one complete breath is the right direction.

1.3. Coarse and subtle sensations

Coarse sensations are what you see from the start: belly rising – falling, the stream of air through the nose. Subtle sensations appear only when the mind settles more: the in-breath carrying a touch of coolness, the out-breath a touch of warmth; the contact point at the rim of the nostrils shifting slightly breath by breath; small "waves" inside the breath. Two things to remember:

  • Subtle sensations are not to be manufactured — they are already there; previously there was just not enough stillness to see them. They appear by themselves when attention is fine enough.
  • When they appear, do not "gape to receive them": simply note additionally "a touch of cool", "a touch of warm", while keeping attention on the chosen spot. Trying to probe deeper easily turns into strain.

1.4. "Bare knowing, just enough"

The Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta — the root discourse of the four establishments of mindfulness (Pali: satipaṭṭhāna) — repeats one very fine detail: mindfulness is established "to the extent of bare knowing and mindfulness, just enough", without further clinging. [Source: Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 10 - Theravāda Buddhism.] Applied to practice: when "today's breath feels so pleasant" appears — that is a thought arisen; when "the breath is too faint, why not clearer" appears — that too is a thought. Know both, then return to the sensation. Knowing continues, but without exaggeration, evaluation, or complaint.

1.5. Common difficulties

  • Breath growing faint: normal as the mind settles — the breath lightens because the body is resting. Shift to knowing the subtlest sensation still present, or back to the belly where movement is clearer. Do not breathe deeply to "create" sensation — that is returning to controlling the breath.
  • Feeling bored, monotonous: follow each breath as one tasting tea for the first time; no breath fully repeats another.
  • Missing mid-breath: following the whole cycle at first often drops in pieces — normal; resume from where you are, do not hunt back for the breath just lost.

2. Anchoring the mind on an object

2.1. The anchor image

A boat without anchor drifts with the current; a mind without object drifts with thought. Anchoring the mind means choosing one object for attention always to return to — it is the meditation subject (Pali: kammaṭṭhāna) met in the previous article. The canon has a close image: one whose mindfulness is established on the body has a mind like a fish tied to a firm post — though pulled this way and that by agreeable and disagreeable scenes, the mind only circles the post without being dragged far; by contrast, a mind without post is like a fish fallen into a net, the more it struggles the more tired it grows. [Source: Alagaddūpama Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 22 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

2.2. Principles for choosing an object

A good object for anchoring the mind usually has four qualities:

  • Stable: always present, not vanishing unpredictably — the breath and bodily sensations are typical examples.
  • Directly knowable: seen directly, needing no inference or analysis.
  • Non-agitating: the object itself does not by itself generate strong emotion.
  • Suitable for the learner: matching the purpose of practice and the guidance of the tradition followed.

The place on the body where the object is felt most clearly is called the touching point — the previous article chose one place and kept it; this article continues that principle: choose once, keep for the whole sitting. Changing points continuously forces attention to "repack" after every move.

2.3. The main object and the "background"

Between sessions, the world keeps happening: traffic humming, a television behind the wall, food smells, a slight ache somewhere, all kinds of thoughts surfacing. No need for absolute silence — not needed, and not to be expected. The right way is to distinguish two layers:

  • Main object — where attention "perches": it plays the central role; attention returns here.
  • Background — everything else: when only faintly flickering, leave it alone, no handling needed; when it rises strongly and pulls attention away, note it and return to the main object.

Picture it: standing on a platform watching one specific train. Other trains pass — know enough, but do not jump aboard.

2.4. The loop: recognize — release gently — return

This is the core of this whole skill group, and the "exercise" repeated most in a sitting:

  1. Recognize: "ah, the mind has gone". This very moment is vivid mindfulness — you have just won, not just lost.
  2. Release gently: do not analyse the thought content, do not blame yourself; drop what you were thinking like opening your hand from an object held.
  3. Return: place attention back on the anchor — gently yet distinctly, like laying a leaf lightly on water.

One such loop is one complete round of exercise. A sitting with dozens of "recognize – release – return" rounds is worth far more than a sitting "drifting" without knowing where you went. This steady repetition, accumulated over many sittings, creates the "one-place-only" quality of attention the discourses call one-pointedness (Pali: ekaggatā); the Visuddhimagga (Pali: visuddhimagga) defines concentration from the outset with exactly this quality: concentration is one-pointedness of mind on the subject. [Source: Visuddhimagga, ch. III - the Theravāda commentarial system.] At a deeper level, this continuously stable attention is the foundation of tranquillity meditation (Pali: samatha) — group C1 in the app will treat it separately.

2.5. When may the object be changed

Do not change merely because of "boredom" — the boredom passes, while whatever part of the mind was trained remains. But changing is reasonable when:

  • The object by itself generates too strong an emotion (sharp pain, frightening sound) — temporarily switch to a more neutral object, or open the eyes slightly.
  • Bodily conditions change greatly: tired, ill, sleepless — choose an object fitting the state (lying down, following the belly breath...).
  • The teacher or the method followed directs a change.

The general rule: try one object long enough (several weeks) before deciding; change for a clear reason, not a passing mood. Article M.D3.1 "Choosing a subject that suits you" treats this more deeply.

3. Summary and next steps

  • Following the sensations of the breath: release counting, sense directly; follow one whole in – pause – out cycle; recognize coarse sensations, then subtle ones appearing by themselves; keep the spirit of "bare knowing, just enough" — knowing much without embellishment.
  • Anchoring the mind on an object: choose an object that is stable, directly knowable, non-agitating; keep one touching point; distinguish the main object from the background; and repeat the "recognize – release gently – return" loop — this very loop, not perfection, creates concentration.
  • These two skills have shared value for every later method: tranquillity, the four establishments of mindfulness, and insight all begin where attention is held and sustained.

Glossary:

  • Concentration (Pali: samādhi): the stability, collectedness, and workability of the mind; the second part of the three trainings (ethics – concentration – wisdom), and the accumulated result of sustaining attention on one object.
  • One-pointedness (Pali: ekaggatā): the "one-place-only" quality of attention — the mind standing firm on one object; a jhāna factor in deep states of collectedness.
  • Meditation subject (Pali: kammaṭṭhāna): the object on which the meditator relies to gather and develop the mind, for example the breath, bodily sensations, loving-kindness.
  • Touching point: the place on the body chosen to sense the object most clearly (nostrils or belly when using the breath), kept fixed for the whole sitting.
  • Four establishments of mindfulness (Pali: satipaṭṭhāna): the four domains of mindfulness listed in the canon: body, feelings, mind, dhammas.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Visuddhimagga (Pali: visuddhimagga): the compendium of meditation practice in Theravāda Buddhism, composed by the Elder Buddhaghosa around the 5th century.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
