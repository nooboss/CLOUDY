M.C2.3. Observing Arising and Passing Away and the Three Characteristics

The previous article's four skills taught how to look at experience. This article enters what that looking heads toward: arising – passing — the arising and dissolving of all phenomena — and the three characteristics the canon points to in all experience: impermanence, suffering (unsatisfactoriness), not-self. This is the core of insight meditation: not learning three concepts to believe, but seeing for yourself those three in each breath, each sensation, each thought. Contents: observing arising – passing, an overview of the three characteristics, then three sections in turn with each characteristic — each going from concept down to what is observable on the cushion.

1. Observing arising–passing

1.1. From "knowing present" to "seeing arising–passing"

At this level, practitioners already know what is present: breath, sounds, sensations, thoughts. The finer next step: seeing each such phenomenon how it is present — arising, lingering a moment, changing, then dissolving. This seeing is named in insight systems as wisdom of arising and passing away (Pali: udayabbaya), and the canon lists it as a principal step in the progress of wisdom (the progress map in detail in article M.C2.5). [Source: Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 10 - Theravāda Buddhism: the "contemplating arising – passing" frame repeated across the four establishments of mindfulness.]

The difference between the two levels: "knowing an itch is present" — knowing landing on the phenomenon; "seeing the itch arising – passing" — seeing the itch not as a standing block, but as hundreds of sensation-fragments popping up, flaring out, continuously replacing. The phenomenon remains the phenomenon; its definition changes: no longer a thing, but a process.

1.2. How to train

  • Collectedness foundation first: a few minutes of collectedness on the subject — continuity of knowing is the condition for seeing small fragments.
  • Choose "high-density" phenomena: belly breath, foot sensations, or aching regions — where experience is dense and distinct.
  • Listen with the mind's eye: like hearing raindrops on a lake surface — each sensation-fragment: popping up — flaring out. No measuring needed; only seeing the continuity of change.
  • Spread across other streams: sounds (each sound arriving then switching off), thoughts (popping then dissolving), mental states (excitement arriving then leaving). The same structure in every stream.

A progress token (note in the journal): previously seeing "an itch present", now seeing "an itch-stream flowing" — and seeing the mind reacting to that stream too.

2. The three characteristics — overview

The three characteristics (Pali: tilakkhaṇa) are three observations the canon makes about all phenomena: impermanence (Pali: anicca), suffering or unsatisfactoriness (Pali: dukkha), not-self (Pali: anattā). [Source: Khandha Saṃyutta, Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 22 - Theravāda Buddhism.] These three characteristics are three complementary viewing angles of the same experience, not three separate lessons: seeing one characteristic deeply enough, the other two reveal themselves along. The three sections below are therefore a pedagogical sequence — helping newcomers step by step — not three exercises to do separately.

One thing to say beforehand: the three characteristics are easily reduced to slogans. Insight meditation's goal is not saying "everything is impermanent", but directly seeing impermanence to the point it changes how the mind reacts. The difference between those two levels — one knowledge, one wisdom — is the whole matter.

3. Contemplating impermanence

3.1. Not a concept, but direct change

Everyone knows "everything is impermanent" — at concept level, impermanence is the youngest lesson. The observation level is entirely different: impermanence is direct change happening before knowing's eyes — this breath not that breath, this pain different each second, yesterday's self already gone. Contemplating impermanence (Pali: anicca) is training the eye to see that change in the smallest phenomena, not in large events (birth – aging – illness – death) everyone already knows.

3.2. How to train

  • Going from coarse to fine: first seeing changes of bodily weather (hot – cool), of feelings within one sitting; later seeing change within one breath, within one second of sensation.
  • Noticing ceasing: two eyes usually have one "seeing arising" fast and one "seeing ceasing" slow; training the ceasing eye — seeing phenomena switch off — is this article's fine part, because the human mind habitually grasps the arising part and neglects the ceasing.
  • Noticing reactions: when seeing something dissolving, does the mind want to keep it? Seeing and clinging placed side by side — exactly the doorway to section 4.

4. Contemplating suffering / unsatisfactoriness

4.1. What "suffering" means here

Suffering in the three-characteristics context is not "tragic pain" — so understood, Buddhism becomes a pessimistic doctrine. It is unsatisfactoriness: because everything is changing, nothing when grasped brings lasting satisfaction — like grasping water. The canon distinguishes three layers of meaning: [Source: Khandha Saṃyutta, Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 22 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

  • Suffering as suffering (Pali: dukkha-dukkha): pain and sorrow — the layer everyone already knows.
  • Suffering in change (Pali: vipariṇāma-dukkha): the pleasant changing away — joy switching off, loved ones changing — unsatisfactoriness arising right from change.
  • Suffering in formations (Pali: saṅkhāra-dukkha): the subtlest layer — the very "unfinished, never-standing-still" nature of all phenomena is itself already unsatisfactory.

4.2. How to train — observing clinging, not seeking sorrow

Contemplating suffering (Pali: dukkha) is not going to find sad feelings. The work is eyeing the focus of clinging:

  • Seeing a pleasant sensation — noticing the wanting to prolong rising along.
  • Seeing an unpleasant sensation — noticing the wanting to expel rising along.
  • Seeing the interval between two urges — the mind with nothing to hold, and also unable to bear that "nothing to hold" for long.

Observing those three thrusts without needing a single word "suffering" — unsatisfactoriness shows itself at exactly those leanings. This is where this article separates from negative thinking: the goal is not world-weariness, but seeing rightly where is truly unsatisfactory — clinging — to release at exactly that place.

5. Contemplating not-self

5.1. What is examined is the default "self", not "nothing exists"

Not-self (Pali: anattā, Sanskrit: anātman) is the subtlest characteristic, and also most easily misread. Two common misreadings:

  • "Not-self = nothing exists at all, so nothing matters" — wrong; the canon regards this as straying to an entirely different side of the path.
  • "Not-self = no personal responsibility" — wrong in the opposite direction; not-self practice goes with higher responsibility, because deeds still have consequences and consequences still belong to the causal stream.

More rightly: not-self is examining the default "self" hidden in each experience — "I am thinking", "I am in pain", "I am progressing". The observation question is not "do I exist" (a distant philosophical question), but: "what is seen here — who is its master?" [Source: Cūḷasuññata Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 121 and the root discourse on not-self — Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta, Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 22.59 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

5.2. How to train — going outward from sensation

  • Begin with observable reality: "there is a painful sensation" — seeing the sensation not asking permission first: it arises by causes, not by wish. Feelings and thoughts likewise.
  • Seeing everything "happening" — breath happening, itch happening, thought happening — as results of many conditions, not as orders of a subject standing behind.
  • Looking at knowing itself: what knows those phenomena also exists by itself, arising – passing along the phenomena it knows — no observer standing outside the flow.

This section's level is initial sensing; deep understanding of not-self is the path's long part, continued by advanced articles (M.C2.4, M.D1). At this stage, only hold the right attitude: examining evidence, not hasty concluding — and not turning "not-self" into a denial slogan for telling others.

6. Summary and next steps

  • Arising – passing: from "knowing what is present" up to "seeing phenomena as process" — all streams (body, sounds, thoughts) the same arising – passing structure; train with a collectedness foundation and "high-density" phenomena.
  • The three characteristics are three viewing angles of one experience: impermanence (direct change, training the ceasing eye); suffering (unsatisfactoriness — observing the want-to-keep / want-to-expel leanings, not seeking sorrow); not-self (examining the default "self" — "what is seen here — who is its master?").
  • All three are self-seen, not believed along; and all lead to the same place: releasing the clinging place, not denying life.

Glossary:

  • Arising and passing away (Pali: udayabbaya): the arising, changing, and dissolving of phenomena moment by moment; when clearly and continuously seen, the discourses call it a wisdom in the progress of wisdom.
  • Three characteristics (Pali: tilakkhaṇa): impermanence (anicca), suffering / unsatisfactoriness (dukkha), not-self (anattā) — three observations the canon makes about all phenomena.
  • Impermanence (Pali: anicca): instability; here understood as directly observable change, unlike empty concept.
  • Suffering as suffering / suffering in change / suffering in formations (Pali: dukkha-dukkha / vipariṇāma-dukkha / saṅkhāra-dukkha): three layers of the word suffering — pain as worldlings understand; suffering through change; suffering through ceaseless arising – passing itself.
  • Not-self (Pali: anattā, Sanskrit: anātman): no fixed, independent "self" mastering experience; not synonymous with "nothing exists" or "no responsibility".
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).

Source notes

  • The inner – outer "contemplating arising – passing" frame, repeated across the four establishments of mindfulness — Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 10 - Theravāda Buddhism.
  • Three layers of suffering (suffering as suffering, in change, in formations) per canonical analysis — Khandha Saṃyutta, Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 22 - Theravāda Buddhism.
  • Not-self: the five aggregates are not-self — Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta, Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 22.59 - Theravāda Buddhism; with the "who is its master" examination in modern practice lineages.
