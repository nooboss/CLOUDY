M.E2.4. Combined Zen–Pure Land Practice

The two previous clusters presented Zen (article M.E2.2) and Buddha-Recitation – Pure Land (article M.E2.3) as two separate roads. But in China and Vietnam, for many centuries, they have been practised together in the same person, temple, and sitting — called combined Zen–Pure Land practice. This article presents that combined tradition per exactly M141's content: dual-practice forms, explanations, and practical meaning for lay practitioners.

1. What is dual practice — why does this phenomenon exist?

Combined Zen–Pure Land practice is one person practising both Zen investigation and buddha-recitation on the same path. This phenomenon arose strongly in China from the Ming – Qing eras (great masters like Yunqi Zhuhong and Hanshan Deqing all encouraged dual practice), becoming the hallmark of early-modern Chinese Buddhism — transmitted to Vietnam through northern and central temples, where seated-meditation sessions and recitation sessions coexist in the same temple.

Why this phenomenon? Historically: East Asia's two largest roads are not good at excluding each other — Zen strong among dedicated monastics, Pure Land widespread among the populace; large temples serving both naturally produced combining. Practically: the two methods have complementary characters — Zen digs deep, Pure Land keeps rhythm; one cannot always "dig" (lacking sustaining calm), nor always only "keep" (lacking depth) — in detail in section 3.

2. Dual-practice forms

Dual practice is not a single formula — the tradition has many combining levels:

  • Time-shared dual practice: dividing hours — mornings investigating huàtóu or just sitting (Zen), evenings holding name recitation (Pure Land); or Zen-practice days – Pure-Land-practice days. This is the most widespread form in Vietnamese temples.
  • Dual practice within one sitting: opening with buddha-recitation to settle the mind (Pure Land as basis), shifting to observing — "who is reciting the Buddha?" (Zen); closing with recitation dedication. The question "who is reciting the Buddha?" is exactly huàtóu (article M.E2.5) taking buddha-recitation material — the tightest combining form.
  • Dual practice within one method: reciting the name while each syllable is one question — reciting to where, examining to there; Chinese masters called this "reciting the Buddha into Zen".

How to read: three levels from loose (parallel, separated hours) to tight (merged into one) — practitioners choosing levels by basis; the tight level demands understanding both methods, belonging to the grounded under guidance (article M.A6).

3. Explanations of the combination

The tradition offers many explanations why these two roads combine — each one viewpoint (neutrally presented per the taxonomy):

  • The "same destination" explanation: Zen aims at seeing nature, Pure Land aims at rebirth in the Pure Land — but the Pure Land at a deep level is also explained as the purified mind-ground ("when the mind is pure, the land is pure" — the Platform Sūtra spirit) — two roads ultimately touching the same settled – luminous mind; differing in means and style, not destination.
  • The "complementary" explanation: Zen gives depth — directly viewing essence; Pure Land gives durability — a mind-keeping method available at all times, ages, and states (article M.E2.3); lacking either side, the path skews — deep without durable stalls, durable without deep stops.
  • The "remedying" explanation: when huàtóu investigation tenses too much — buddha-recitation softens the mind; when name-holding easily turns mechanical — Zen questions revive recitation; the two remedy each other — a very common experience in Vietnamese practice.
  • The "conditional affinity" explanation: each person suits different weightings — quiet taciturn persons suiting more Pure Land; those needing living questions suiting more Zen; dual practice allows self-calibrating weightings by one's own phase.

4. Practical meaning for lay practitioners — and summary

  • Lay practitioners need not choose "sides". Vietnamese Buddhist life was already born in a dual-practice environment: temple rites have chanting-recitation (Pure Land), meditation courses have Zen — practising both at home is natural, needing no "must choose one" feeling.
  • A simple daily template: 10 morning sitting minutes — a few minutes of buddha-recitation as basis, a few minutes of clearly knowing breath and body (group B), a few minutes of the light question "who is sitting?"; evenings — 108 recitations with beads before sleep (joining loving-kindness's calming benefits — article M.D2.1). This is one template, not a mould — each adjusting by hours and strength.
  • Foundation principles still stand: shared skills (releasing — M.B1, anchoring the mind — M.B2, handling distraction — M.B3, journaling — M.B7) apply to both methods; dual practice is not "two techniques doubled" but two materials on one skill set.
  • One teacher – one direction remains the golden principle: dual practice is not free mixing — those combining two roads should follow one temple – one teacher – one sequence (article M.A6); self-mixing many complex methods at once (especially huàtóu, kōans — M.E2.5) is an easy-to-err road.

Summary:

  • Combined Zen–Pure Land practice is the tradition combining Zen investigation and buddha-recitation — naturally formed in China (Ming – Qing), early-modern Chinese Buddhism's hallmark and very present in Vietnam.
  • Three form levels: separated hours (most widespread) → within one sitting (buddha-recitation as basis, the "who is reciting the Buddha?" question) → merged in one method (the tight level, needing the grounded).
  • Four explanations: the same destination — depth × durability complementing — mutually remedying — calibrating by conditional affinity; neutrally presented, unranked.
  • For lay practitioners: no need to choose "sides"; a simple daily template possible; group B's skill set shared; and the "one teacher – one sequence" principle (article M.A6) remains the guarantee.

Glossary:

  • Dual practice (Chinese: shuāngxiū): practising two methods on the same path — here: Zen and Pure Land.
  • Huàtóu (Chinese: huàtóu): living questions used as meditation objects in the Línjì lineage — in detail in article E2.5.
  • When the mind is pure, the land is pure: the sūtra-meaning summary — the land is mind when the mind is purified; used to explain two roads touching one destination.
  • Rosaries (Sanskrit: mālā): recitation-counting bead strings — usually 108 beads; already introduced in article E2.3.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
