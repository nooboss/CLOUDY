M.E2.1. Mahāyāna — A Map and the Calming–Contemplation Framework

After group E1 — the Southern tradition — group E2 turns to Mahāyāna Buddhism: the tradition widespread in China, Vietnam, Japan, and Korea — also the most present basis in Vietnamese Buddhist life. 1. What is Mahāyāna — one tradition, many systems

Mahāyāna (Sanskrit: mahāyāna — literally "the great vehicle": a practice road open to all beings, not only monastics) is the tradition developed from around the beginning of the Common Era and spread across East Asia. Unlike Theravāda preserving the Pali canon, Mahāyāna uses Sanskrit for most scriptures — later translated into Chinese, and from Chinese most meditation terms entered Vietnamese (article M.A1).

The most important point for reading the whole E2 group — exactly the taxonomy's main requirement: not using "Mahāyāna" as a single meditation system. Within Mahāyāna exist many very different practices:

  • Zen / Chan (Chinese: Chán; Japanese: Zen) — "the school living by meditation": sitting meditation, kōans, huàtóu (M.E2.2, M.E2.5);
  • Pure Land — buddha-name recitation and Pure Land visualization (M.E2.3);
  • The Tiantai school — the structured Calming–Contemplation system (sections 3–4 of this article);
  • Consciousness-Only — the cognition-analysing lineage (M.E2.6);
  • And mind-training and giving-and-taking directions (M.E2.6; deeper Tibetan parts belong to group E3).

These systems share one common basis — the join with learned articles: bodhicitta (Sanskrit: bodhicitta — the mind resolved on awakening for beings; article M.D2), the six perfections (Sanskrit: pāramitā; article M.A1), emptiness (Sanskrit: śūnyatā — Mahāyāna's central concept), and the four divine abodes — among the clearest meeting points between traditions (article M.D2.5).

2. Tranquillity and insight (Śamatha–Vipaśyanā) in Mahāyāna

The tranquillity – insight pair already fully presented in groups C1 – C2 with Pali names (samatha – vipassanā); in the Mahāyāna context, the pair appears in Sanskrit form: Śamatha–Vipaśyanā (calming - Sanskrit: śamatha; insight - Sanskrit: vipaśyanā). Mahāyāna canonical literature keeps exactly these two directions: the most familiar discourse speaking of them is the Samādhirāja Sūtra — the sūtra naming two things to be cultivated for going to wisdom: tranquillity and insight. [Source: Samādhirāja Sūtra - Mahāyāna Buddhism.]

The characteristic mark of Mahāyāna's use of the tranquillity – insight pair — differing in the surrounding frame rather than technical essence:

  • Oriented by bodhicitta: practitioners vow before sitting — collectedness and wisdom placed in wishing benefit for all beings, not only one's own calm;
  • Placed among perfections: meditation is one of six perfections (dhyāna pāramitā) — sitting meditation joined to remaining virtues: generosity, ethics, patience, effort, wisdom;
  • Observation objects bearing Mahāyāna marks: contemplating the emptiness of phenomena — seeing all phenomena without fixed separate essences (article M.E2.6);
  • Hence: the tranquillity – insight pair in Mahāyāna is usually not divided into two independent "methods" but presented as one combined movement — the basis of the East Asian Calming–Contemplation frame below.

3. Calming–Contemplation (Zhǐguān) — the East Asian frame

Calming–Contemplation (Chinese: zhǐguān — Vietnamese: chỉ quán) is the East Asian name of the tranquillity – insight pair: calming means stopping, abiding — corresponding to śamatha; contemplation means observing, examining — corresponding to vipaśyanā. This frame characterizes Chinese and East Asian Buddhism, with one essential point: the two directions presented as one combined whole — one sitting has both, shifting by rhythm ("mind drifting, use calming; mind sluggish, use contemplation") instead of choosing one and discarding one.

The East Asian understanding is very practical, using a familiar image: calming like stopping turbid water to settle, contemplation like clearly seeing the moon in settled water — stopping and seeing are not two opposing tasks, but two beats of the same seeing. The Calming–Contemplation frame is the basis structure later East Asian lineages developed in their own directions: the Tiantai school systematizing into a curriculum (section 4); Zen later unfolding in the "directly pointing at the mind-ground" way (M.E2.2); Pure Land using name-recitation as calming and visualization as contemplation (M.E2.3).

4. The Great Calming and Contemplation and Tiantai Zen

The Great Calming and Contemplation (Chinese: Móhē Zhǐguān — "Great Calming–Contemplation") is the large-scale meditation curriculum of the Tiantai school (the school named for Mount Tiantai, China), compiled by Meditation Master Zhiyi (538–597) — regarded as the founding patriarch. This is regarded as one of East Asian Buddhism's most structured meditation systems: the whole path arranged into a distinct programme — from living conditions and sitting ways to contemplation dimensions.

At map level (this article teaches no techniques — exactly the taxonomy's note), three prominent structural marks of the Great Calming and Contemplation:

  • 25 preparatory conditions: before tranquillity – wisdom practice, learners must arrange life — dwelling, precept-keeping, health, relations — seen from there: the "preparing a sitting" basis of articles M.A4 – M.A6 has a long traditional depth;
  • Three contemplation dimensions (contemplating emptiness – conventional – middle): three views on the same phenomenon — unfixed, un-cancelled, non-dual — Tiantai's practical presentation of emptiness;
  • Three practice forms (gradual – indeterminate – perfect-sudden): from step-by-step paths for beginners to roads of "complete in one mind at once" for other capacities — this very school plainly affirms: no single walking rhythm for everyone.

This system's influence spreads very far: Chinese Zen (and Japanese Zen and Vietnamese Zen lineages) grew on the Calming–Contemplation cultural basis; Tiantai in Japan (Tendai) became the basis of many later lineages; and current Vietnamese Mahāyāna meditation curricula still use Calming–Contemplation vocabulary — meaning the two words Vietnamese practitioners meet most often are exactly this article's frame.

Summary:

  • Mahāyāna is not a single meditation system — Zen, Pure Land, Tiantai, Consciousness-Only... sharing the common basis: bodhicitta, perfections, emptiness, four divine abodes.
  • Tranquillity – insight (Śamatha–Vipaśyanā) is kept by Mahāyāna with the same two technical directions but placed in its own frame: bodhicitta-oriented, placed among perfections, observation objects bearing emptiness marks (citing the Samādhirāja Sūtra).
  • Calming–Contemplation (Zhǐguān) is the East Asian name and distinct presentation: two directions combined as one whole, not divided into two methods — the settled-turbid-water and appearing-moon image.
  • The Great Calming and Contemplation by Meditation Master Zhiyi (Tiantai) is East Asia's most structured system: 25 preparations, three contemplation dimensions, three practice forms — influencing the entire East Asian meditation culture, including Vietnam.

Glossary:

  • Mahāyāna (Sanskrit: mahāyāna): "the great vehicle" — the Buddhist tradition opening the practice road for all beings, widespread in East Asia.
  • Calming–Contemplation (Chinese: zhǐguān): the East Asian two-direction meditation frame — calming = stopping, abiding; contemplation = observing, examining.
  • Great Calming and Contemplation (Chinese: Móhē Zhǐguān): Master Zhiyi's meditation curriculum.
  • Zhiyi (Chinese: Zhiyi, 538–597): the founding patriarch of the Chinese Tiantai school.
  • Tiantai school (Chinese: Tiantai): the school named for Mount Tiantai; in Japan called Tendai.
  • Emptiness (Sanskrit: śūnyatā): Mahāyāna's central concept — all phenomena without fixed separate essences; deep in article E2.6.
  • Samādhi (Sanskrit: samādhi): collectedness states — a component of many Mahāyāna sūtra names.
  • Bodhicitta (Sanskrit: bodhicitta): the mind resolved on awakening for beings (article M.D2).
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
