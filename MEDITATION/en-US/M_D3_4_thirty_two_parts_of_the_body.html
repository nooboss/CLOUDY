M.D3.4. The Thirty-Two Parts of the Body

Among the 40 system's body-contemplation subjects, the thirty-two parts — contemplating the body through 32 parts — is a practice still fully available today from canonical origins: no corpses needed like foulness contemplation (article M.D3.3), only one's own living body. This is part of mindfulness immersed in the body, transmitted by modern lineages at various levels. Contents: what the thirty-two parts are, the 32-part list per the discourses, differing presentations between sources, basic-level practice, and safety warnings.

1. What are the thirty-two parts?

The thirty-two parts (Pali: dvattiṃsākāra) are the canon's list of 32 parts composing the body — from head-hairs to brain — for contemplating through reflection on repulsiveness (Pali: paṭikūlamanasikāra) — the same view introduced in article M.D3.3, but performed on the living body and through a book list, needing no frightening images. The canon calls this practice one of the body-contemplation ways; the commentaries place it in mindfulness immersed in the body (kāyagatāsati) and the 40-subject system.

The purpose — exactly as the previous article distinguished: compensating one perception. The body is overlaid with "beautiful" perceptions through bathing, fine clothes, adornment; the 32-part list is an inventory of the body's genuine materials — the discourses use the grain-bag image: opening the bag, laying out each grain kind from the bag — viewers distinctly seeing each kind, no longer the bag's overall image obscuring. [Source: Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 10 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

2. The 32-part list per the discourses

The Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta lists in clusters — 20 parts then 12 watery substances:

  • Series 1 — head parts: head-hairs (kesā), body-hairs (lomā), nails (nakhā), teeth (dantā), skin (taco);
  • Series 2 — flesh – membranes: flesh (maṃsaṃ), sinews (nahārū), bones (aṭṭhi), marrow (aṭṭhimiñjaṃ), kidneys (vakkaṃ);
  • Series 3 — inner organs: heart (hadayaṃ), liver (yakanaṃ), diaphragm (kilomakaṃ), spleen (papphāsaṃ), lungs (papphāsa-pavāṭaka);
  • Series 4 — intestines – substances: intestines (antaṃ), mesentery (antaguṇṭhaṃ), undigested food (udariyaṃ), excrement (karīsaṃ);
  • Series 5 — twelve watery substances: bile (pittaṃ), phlegm (semhaṃ), pus (pubbo), blood (lohitaṃ), sweat (sedo), fat (medo), tears (assu), grease (vasā), saliva (kheḷo), nasal mucus (siṅghāṇikā), joint oil (lasikā), urine (muttaṃ).

Important note: translations and systems cluster differently — some sources divide 20 / 12, some group by bodily location (head, upper body, belly, legs); the 32 list is consistent, grouping belongs to translators and transmitters — in detail in section 3 below.

3. Differing presentations between sources

This point — exactly the assigned article content — needs stating plainly, and reality is so:

  • In the canonical basket: 32 parts appear in body contemplation (MN 10, DN 22) and the Kāyagatāsati Sutta MN 119 — the practice purpose is contemplating: each part in turn, clearly seen, uninfatuated; the discourses attach no deep-collectedness technique on each part.
  • In the commentarial system (Visuddhimagga): each part is assigned colour, shape, direction, location, delimitation — usable as collectedness subjects: choosing one part, holding the mind on it, developing the sign — the road to jhāna like other subjects; hence 32 parts are placed in the 40 system.
  • In modern lineages: most use 32 parts as a contentful body-scanning way — scanning through parts, clearly knowing, uninfatuated contemplating; some lineages (Pa-Auk — article M.E1.3) keep the commentarial collectedness use; secular health programmes (group G2) only borrow the scanning form with relaxing goals — differing in foundation from establishments-frame body contemplation.

How to read: the same list, three different use levels — canon: contemplating; commentaries: contemplating + collectedness; modern: contentful scanning (or relaxing outside the Buddhist frame). Knowing which level one reads prevents goal confusion.

4. Basic-level practice

Below is the contemplating – body-scanning level in the canonical spirit, suitable for those with a practice foundation:

  • Step 1 — foundation: a few minutes of breath for the mind moderately settled.
  • Step 2 — scanning by series: from head-hairs downward, each part: clearly knowing its location on one's own body (sensed — not imagining medical images), silently labelling the part's name, and one short contemplating beat — "this material is present, not 'self'".
  • Step 3 — rhythm: 10–20 seconds per part is enough; through series 1–4 then the 12 watery substances — or on short-time days, only one series.
  • Step 4 — closing: back to the breath a few minutes, clearly knowing the mind's state — then ending sittings as usual.
  • Frequency: no need daily; suitable as one sitting weekly alternating with the main subject — keeping the 5-minute four-elements rhythm (article M.D3.5) or relaxing body scan (group G2) if wanting lighter.

This practice's measure is not "more disgusted" — but a more balanced mind toward the body: less outraged when ill, less showy when beautiful, daring to view one's own X-ray without trembling — progress recorded in the journal as always.

5. Safety warnings

  • Unsuitable for those currently having body-image problems, hating one's own body, or depression — the same warnings as foulness contemplation (article M.D3.3): this practice may worsen self-criticism; when steadied, retrying under experienced guidance is possible.
  • Contemplating ≠ hating oneself. Seeing oneself disparaging the body after sittings — a skew signal: the purpose is a balanced mind (neither infatuated – nor hating), both sides needed; back to the breath, to loving-kindness (article M.D2.1), and telling the experienced (article M.A6).
  • Do not turn into medical analysis. Remembering 32 parts' standard medical names is not yet meditation; the goal is sensing and contemplating on one's own body, not anatomy exams.

6. Summary and next steps

  • The thirty-two parts (dvattiṃsākāra) are the canon's 32-body-part list — for contemplating through reflection on repulsiveness on the living body, needing no corpses.
  • The list is consistently 32, but grouping differs between sources; three use levels: canon — contemplating; commentaries — contemplating + collectedness subjects; modern — contentful scanning (or relaxing outside the Buddhist frame).
  • Basic level: scanning by series, short labels, contemplating "this material is present, not 'self'"; the measure = a more balanced mind toward the body.
  • Safety: unsuitable for those with body-image problems; contemplating ≠ hating oneself — both neither-infatuated – nor-hating sides needed.

Suggested next reading: the 40 system's second body-viewing needing no frightening images: Analysis of the Four Elements (Catudhātuvavatthāna) — observing the body through four qualities: hard / soft, cohesion, hot / cold, movement (article M.D3.5). See you there.

Glossary:

  • Thirty-two parts (Pali: dvattiṃsākāra): the 32-body-part list used in contemplating through reflection on repulsiveness.
  • Reflection on repulsiveness (Pali: paṭikūlamanasikāra): contemplating the body with non-infatuation (article M.D3.3).
  • Mindfulness immersed in the body (Pali: kāyagatāsati): the practice of keeping mindfulness on the body (article M.D1.1) — 32 parts are part of it.
  • Grain bag: the discourse image (MN 10) for viewing 32 parts — opening the bag laying out each kind, seeing genuine materials instead of the overall image.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
