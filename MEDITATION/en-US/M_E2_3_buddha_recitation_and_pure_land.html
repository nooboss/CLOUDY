M.E2.3. Buddha-Recitation and Pure Land

After Zen (article M.E2.2), the next cluster of East Asian Mahāyāna is the most widespread form nearest Vietnamese Buddhist life: Buddha-Recitation and Pure Land. 1. What is buddha-recitation — one word, many levels

Buddha-recitation (Chinese: niànfó; Japanese: nembutsu) is the practice of holding the mind on the Buddha — through names, images, or qualities. "Recollection" means remembering, thinking of — as article M.D3.7 distinguished: this recollection shares roots with Theravāda recollection (anussati); differing in scope of unfolding:

  • Theravāda (article M.D3.7): virtue recollection — belonging to the ten-recollection system, leading to access concentration, nourishing confidence and calm;
  • East Asian Mahāyāna (this article): name recitation — developed into a complete road in the Pure Land tradition, with its own doctrine, destination, and practice life.

The two presentations are not contradictory but two floors: the same holding the mind on the Buddha — one side a subject in the 40 system, one side an independent practice road; article M.D3.7 already advised readers not to mix the two systems. This article presents the second side.

2. Three recitation ways: name-holding, visualization, true-mark

The Pure Land tradition divides recitation ways by depth — the familiar classification in Pure Land books:

  • Name-holding recitation: reciting "Namo Amitābha Buddha" — mouth reciting, ears hearing, mind knowing; the most widespread way, available to everyone, at all times (walking, light work, before sleep). Technique: keeping slow – distinct – distinctly heard rhythms; using rhythm bells or rosaries (mālā — counting beads) when needed; mind wandering, clearly knowing then back to the name — the "recognize – release – return" loop wholly applies.
  • Visualization recitation: holding the mind on the Buddha's image — either picturing qualities, or structured visualization (section 4); needing a firmer collectedness basis than name-holding.
  • True-mark recitation: reciting the Buddha as the mind's unborn-undying nature — holding no images, stopping at no names; the deepest level, needing collectedness – wisdom basis and guides; Pure Land books place this as buddha-recitation merging with emptiness-nature (article M.E2.6).

How to read the three levels: one ladder from coarse to fine, not three different meditations — name-holding is the recommended doorway; higher levels for those grounded.

3. What is Pure Land — doctrine at map level

Pure Land (Chinese: Jìngtǔ — "pure land") is the East Asian Mahāyāna tradition taking Amitābha Buddha's realm (Sanskrit: Amitābha — "boundless light – boundless life") as destination: practitioners reciting names with vowing minds — vowing rebirth in that realm, where practice conditions are complete, nothing obstructing until fruition. The tradition's three basis sūtras (collectively called the Three Pure Land Sūtras): the Amitābha Sūtra (shortest, describing the Pure Land and guiding name-holding), the Contemplation Sūtra (presenting visualization ways — section 4), and the Larger Sukhāvatīvyūha Sūtra (long, fully presenting doctrine) — with Vasubandhu's Rebirth Treatise systematizing the road.

The doctrinal mark to know (map level): Pure Land stresses "other-power" (Chinese: tālì — the Buddha Amitābha's aiding power) — beside personal self-power effort; this point makes Pure Land unlike self-power-stressing roads (like Zen). Do not understand "other-power" as passive: the tradition still demands faith – vows – practice (deep faith, vowing rebirth, reciting practice) — three conditions practitioners themselves supply; "other-power" is the means' breadth, not a place to abandon effort.

Pure Land's social place: the widest practice form in East Asian countries — demanding neither youth, health, nor long time; suitable for the elderly, ill, and busy; hence nearer Vietnamese Buddhist families' lives than all other meditation forms (Pure Land in Vietnam met in group F1).

4. Pure Land visualization — The Contemplation Sūtra

The representative source of structured visualizations is the Contemplation Sūtra — the sūtra presenting sixteen contemplations, in three groups: (1) thirteen contemplations on the Pure Land (setting sun, water surfaces, grounds, jewel trees, lakes, adornments...); (2) three contemplations on the Buddha and two Bodhisattvas (Amitābha Buddha, Avalokiteśvara, Mahāsthāmaprāpta); (3) nine contemplations on rebirth grades (nine levels of those reborn by conduct).

Two things to understand rightly:

  • This is canonical structured visualization — each step sourced, sequenced, doctrinally purposed (seeing the Pure Land to strengthen vowing minds) — entirely unlike modern "positive visualization" (imagining a peaceful place for relaxing): the latter has no canonical source, no doctrinal sequence, aiming at relaxing rather than vowing minds; the two do not replace each other and should not be mixed.
  • Visualization needs a basis and guides: the sūtra's detailed contemplations demand a firm collectedness basis; beginners start with name-holding — enough and safe; detailed contemplations reserved for the grounded under guidance (article M.A6).

5. Buddha-recitation as meditation: techniques and benefits — and summary

The "buddha-recitation as one meditation" viewpoint (joining groups B–C's skill articles):

  • A complete attention subject: the name is a stable sound – semantic object — like a variant of calm abiding with sound and calm abiding with one recitation phrase; anchoring and distraction-handling skills wholly apply (group B);
  • Mindfulness training in daily life: name-holding available at all times — walking, waiting, light work — a "mindfulness marker" form fully culturalized (article M.B6);
  • Rosaries (mālā): familiar counting tools — one bead per recitation; unrequired, but evening rhythms and creating familiar bodily signals (compare breath counting — article M.B1);
  • Progress criteria: like all subjects — less wavering minds outside hours, recitation appearing naturally when the mind is uneasy; not "sacred feelings" (exactly the reliable-progress-signs spirit — article M.B7).

Summary:

  • Buddha-recitation (niànfó / nembutsu) is holding the mind on the Buddha — sharing "recollection" roots with Theravāda recollection (article M.D3.7) but developed by East Asian Mahāyāna into the Pure Land tradition's complete road.
  • Three recitation ways by depth: name-holding (doorway, everyone) → visualization (needing basis) → true-mark (needing collectedness – wisdom and guides).
  • Pure Land takes Amitābha Buddha's realm as destination, basis scriptures are the Three Pure Land Sūtras + Rebirth Treatise; doctrine stressing other-power beside self-power — yet still demanding faith – vows – practice; East Asia's widest practice form, nearest Vietnamese Buddhist life.
  • Pure Land visualization per the Contemplation Sūtra: sixteen contemplations (Pure Land – Buddha and Bodhisattvas – nine rebirth grades); entirely unlike secular "positive visualization"; beginners starting with name-holding.
  • Buddha-recitation as meditation: stable sound – semantic subjects, group B skills wholly applying; progress criteria measured in the mind outside hours.

Glossary:

  • Buddha-recitation (Chinese: niànfó; Japanese: nembutsu): holding the mind on the Buddha through names, images, or qualities.
  • Name-holding (Chinese: holding the name): reciting the Buddha's name — the most widespread recitation way.
  • True-mark buddha-recitation (Chinese: true-mark): reciting the Buddha through the unborn-undying nature — the deepest of three recitation ways.
  • Pure Land (Chinese: Jìngtǔ): the tradition taking Amitābha Buddha's realm as destination; Three Pure Land Sūtras = Amitābha Sūtra, Contemplation Sūtra, Larger Sukhāvatīvyūha Sūtra.
  • Amitābha (Sanskrit: Amitābha): the Pure Land's Buddha — "boundless light, boundless life".
  • Other-power (Chinese: tālì): the Buddha's aiding power — the dimension against self-power (personal effort).
  • Rosaries (Sanskrit: mālā): recitation-counting bead strings — 108 beads the common form.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
