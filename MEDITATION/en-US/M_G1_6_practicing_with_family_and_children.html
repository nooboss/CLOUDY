M.G1.6. Practicing with Family and Children

The previous article helps meditation living in packed schedules; this article resolves the circumstance most accompanying busyness: having families and small children. Many think children are practice "barriers" — this article proposes the reverse view: turning household members into practice parts. Contents are exactly this article's three focuses: introducing short practices for children by ages, two easiest application moments (before meals and before sleep), and the most important principle — no forcing.

1. The first principle — beginning with adults

The most important thing to understand before wanting to "teach meditation to children": children learn from models, not lectures. One adult in homes sitting two minutes daily with light minds teaches more than all mindfulness explanations; conversely, tense adults demanding children "sit still to calm down" only teach children meditation is a punishment. Hence this article begins with one straight question to oneself: have I practised for myself — regularly and happily?

The canon rightly locates adults' roles: parents have duties to prevent children from evil, lead children into good — and the bringing way is through lifestyle, not orders. [Source: Sigālovāda Sutta, Dīgha Nikāya, DN 31 - Theravāda Buddhism.] Plum Village verses, mindfulness bells, and slow walking meditation (article M.F1.4) are famously "whole-family-usable" toolkits — including very small children — exactly this article's form source.

2. Introducing to children by ages

Children differ from adults in that: not bearing long sittings, hating forcing, and learning through games. Below articles are chosen exactly those characters — all "child-ized" versions of group B articles:

  • Ages 3 to 6 — playing with breath and bells: at these ages, concepts are useless, experience is everything. Three fittest forms: placing hands on bellies and sensing hands bobbing when breathing ("breathing making animals swell"), hearing bells — striking one bell (or glass cups), children hearing until bell sounds completely end then raising hands (this game excellently trains hearing attention), and deeply breathing in like smelling flowers, breathing out really long like blowing candles — teaching deep-breathing rhythms through images.
  • Ages 7 to 11 — short numbered articles: these ages can do procedural articles: counting ten breaths (article M.G1.4), hearing sounds one minute, "looking – smelling – slowly chewing" with first bites (article M.G1.1). Coarse concepts teachable: "paying attention is bringing minds to one place, like directing antennas". One-to-three-minute durations suffice; small children still learn a few genuine sitting minutes.
  • Ages 12 and above — introducing as with adults, but voluntary: teenagers can directly use group B articles (releasing, breath counting, observing thoughts) — and many arrive at practice because of study pressure, sleep, and feelings themselves. What to avoid: turning practice into children's "error-fixing ways" ("scattered, go sitting meditation") — those sentences make meditation disciplinary tools children immediately hate. Sharing one's own genuine experience, inviting not demanding.

One safety boundary to state plainly: these articles are attention-training and gentle articles for normally developing children — not therapies. Children with hyperactivity – attention difficulties, anxiety, prolonged sleep disorders, or other psychological issues need professional evaluation and guidance; at-home meditation exercises do not replace and should not be used as homemade cures (the shared safety spirit already presented in article M.A3; health scope – limits presented in group G2).

3. Two easiest application moments — before meals and before sleep

In family days, two "naturally easiest application" moments for whole families to do together — because they repeat regularly with ready stopping reasons:

  • Before meals — one shared stopping minute: before eating, whole families stopping seconds: one shared extended breath, or one short gratitude sentence — with food, cooks, and whoever made rice. Under-one-minute durations, no tense silence demanded, small children immediately followable. This is exactly article M.G1.1's "one first bite exercise" placed in family frames — meals becoming daily ready mindfulness (Pali: sati) moments.
  • Before sleep — lying breathing and light scanning: children lying on beds, placing hands on bellies, a few slow breaths — possibly reading with one simple sleep verse ("today finished, allowing myself rest"). With older children, possibly adding light scanning from feet to heads — the abridged version of body-scan techniques: noticing each bodily part and releasing it down to mattresses. These two forms help switching nervous machines from "running" to "resting" — many families find sleep hours far easier merely through two such minutes (comparing with lying postures in four postures already presented in article M.A4).

4. The no-forcing principle

This is the whole article's most important principle, sealed in four points:

  • Forcing is immediately counterproductive. Forcing children to sit "to train patience" teaches children hating both things: sitting and patience. Children missing exercises once then forced — afterwards viewing all "sitting still" forms as punishments. Practice children want to do is practice children can do for many years — long-term goals matter more than one week's results.
  • Allowing stopping, no re-asking. "You may stop" must always be true. Children stopping mid-articles is fine; tomorrow wanting to redo arrives by itself. The same spirit as adults' rules: not turning exercises into new blame conditions.
  • Praising through attention, not rewards. Short, concrete praise, right then ("just now you heard bells until completely ended") — no candies, scores, or "conversions". Mindfulness is its own reward; outer rewards turn experiences into transactions, and children will do when candies exist — stopping when candies end.
  • Non-participating is also fine. One of two spouses practising, children unfollowing — just letting things naturally. One person's practice in homes still leaves whole homes' atmospheres: adults knowing self-balancing will be calmer when children cry, and that — not lectures — is the greatest lesson children receive.

5. Summary and next steps

  • Beginning with adults: children learning from models, not lectures; parents' roles leading through lifestyle (Sigālovāda Sutta, DN 31).
  • By ages: 3–6 playing with breath and bells; 7–11 short numbered articles; 12+ using adult articles but voluntary — avoiding turning meditation into "error-fixing ways".
  • Two easiest application moments: before meals (one shared stopping minute) and before sleep (lying breathing, light scanning) — regularly repeating, followable by anyone.
  • The no-forcing principle: allowing stopping; praising through attention; non-participating also fine; and the safety boundary — attention-training articles ≠ therapies, children's psychological issues belong to professionals.

Glossary:

  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
  • Verses (Sanskrit: gāthā): short verses going with activities to remind mindfulness — already presented in article F1.4.
  • Mindfulness bells: bells used as signals to stop and breathe mindfully; with children, usually the game of hearing bells until completely ended.
  • Body scan: the practice of successively noticing each bodily part from feet to heads (or reverse) — the full version will be presented in group G2; in this article only the abridged version before sleep is used.
  • Postures (Pali: iriyāpatha): four postures — walking, standing, sitting, lying down; lying postures are the basis of pre-sleep breathing articles.
  • Mindfulness (Pali: sati): remembering and staying with what is happening right now — the basis capacity of all articles in the collection.
  • Scattered speech (everyday speech): pointing to hyperactive, hard-to-hold-attention child states; if these states distinctly affect study and daily activities, professional medical – psychological evaluation is needed, not home-exercise self-handling.
