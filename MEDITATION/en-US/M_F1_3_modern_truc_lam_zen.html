M.F1.3. Modern Trúc Lâm Zen — "Knowing Delusion, Not Following It"

The previous article presented Trúc Lâm's spirit through history; this article presents the modern revived Trúc Lâm lineage's practice approach, summarized in four words: "knowing delusion, not following it" — recognizing wandering thoughts without running along. This article is written at a general introductory level, true to the group's neutral spirit: this is one lineage's presentation, not meditation's shared definition.

1. From decline to revival — the context of the present approach

The Trúc Lâm Zen school flourished in the Trần era, but like many traditional lineages, it passed through declining periods: scattered texts, interrupted dharma lineages, practice merging into shared temple activities. The lineage's organized revival occurred strongly in the second half of the 20th century — notably through Master Thích Thanh Từ (1924–2022), who turned toward the Trần-era Trúc Lâm model to rebuild the school's style, establishing meditation monasteries (organized meditation-practice places) and re-presenting the practice road for today's Vietnamese. Today, Trúc Lâm monasteries, temples, and practice groups are widespread — most learners approaching through meditation classes and weekend retreats.

What is worth noting: this modern approach invents no strange techniques, but returns and summarizes — taking Trần-era Trúc Lâm texts as roots, then re-presenting in simple, practicable language for beginners. The "knowing delusion, not following it" formula is exactly such a summary.

2. Decoding the "knowing delusion, not following it" formula

Dividing the formula into three parts, each one training step:

  • "Knowing" — distinctly recognizing: when a thought arises, the first task is not doing something with that thought, but recognizing it is present. This is exactly mindfulness (Pali: sati) — the "remembering and staying with" capacity for what is happening in mind (trained in group B). Without "knowing", all following steps are absent.
  • "Delusion" — wandering thoughts: automatically drifting thoughts — worrying yesterday's business, calculating tomorrow's, tormenting over someone's remark. The scriptures collectively call them wandering thoughts (Chinese: wàngxiǎng). The important point: thoughts arising is the mind's normal business — not faults, not failures.
  • "Not following" — neither clinging into, nor chasing away: this part is subtlest. "Not following" is neither erasing thoughts, nor forcefully chasing them away — because the more chasing, the stronger they grow (all methods' shared experience). "Not following" means: knowing it is present, not further receiving, not denying — the mind simply left naturally. Thoughts unfed with "food" for prolonging settle by themselves.

Merged, the formula describes one very small act repeated throughout sittings: knowing → not following → the mind settling by itself → knowing again. Readers will recognize this is the same "recognize — release gently — return" loop learned in group B, expressed in Trúc Lâm language. The difference lies in that: here the loop aims straight at the mind's very activity (wandering thoughts), not necessarily needing an outer "anchor" like breath — breath and walking meditation (Pali: caṅkama) are often used as four-posture combinations in each sitting.

One noteworthy consequence: because the goal is not following rather than thoughts absent, this approach pre-blocks two common errors from both sides — one side "forcing empty minds", the other "worrying when minds wave". That is also why this formula is regarded as easily grasped for beginners: it demands only one task, doable from the very first sitting.

3. Comparing with two learned mind-observation ways

Readers already met two handlings of thoughts; comparing shows Trúc Lâm approach's distinct points:

With "Mindfulness of thought" (article M.C2.2): the observing way places thoughts as objects: viewing them arising — existing — dissolving, sometimes labelling "thinking", seeing them un-self-mastered.
Resembling Trúc Lâm: neither believing all thoughts, nor forcing empty minds.
Differing from Trúc Lâm: "Mindfulness of thought" observes in detail (analysing thoughts as mental phenomena), while the Trúc Lâm approach goes straight to deciding not following — less analysing, more sealing: "knowing suffices, no need to examine closely". For beginners, both are usable; Trúc Lâm here resembles an easily remembered abridgement.

With "Just sitting" (article M.E2.5): the Japanese Sōtō lineage's shikantaza is a ready-open attitude: sitting, alert to all happening, choosing nor rejecting nothing, aiming to fix nothing. Resembling Trúc Lâm: neither chasing thoughts away. Differing: shikantaza does not separately isolate "wandering thoughts" for handling — everything equally received as one; while the Trúc Lâm approach has one named step ("knowing delusion"), that is, still distinctly distinguishing objects for acting. Colloquially: shikantaza is opening doors, while "knowing delusion, not following it" is half-releasing hands — distinctly knowing yet not grasping.

The three ways ("Mindfulness of thought", shikantaza, Trúc Lâm) do not exclude one another: standing together on the not-fighting-the-mind basis. Choosing which is choosing a fitting guidance language — and the "one teacher – one lineage – one time" principle remains the guiding advice.

4. One meditation session in this approach — conceptual level

The conceptual level of one practice sitting in modern Trúc Lâm monasteries usually goes (full details transmitted directly through guiding teachers):

  • Preparation: sitting steadily, the back naturally straight, the body released; gently closed or half-open eyes; possibly alternating with walking meditation between sitting stretches.
  • Beginning: letting the breath be natural; no counting, no controlling — the mind settling along the settling body.
  • While sitting: wandering thoughts arising → knowing at once they are present → not following — not prolonging, not chasing away → the mind by itself returning to settledness. When the mind settles, settled states arise ("scenes" — what arises before mind): also knowing those scenes without clinging — "knowing scenes without clinging to scenes" is the same formula's advanced part.
  • In daily activities: the same method applied outside hours: doing whatever, knowing one is doing; thoughts arising while washing dishes, walking, meeting people — also knowing then releasing, exactly as when sitting.

On progress measures, this approach follows group B's "Practice Skills" shared principle: measured through outer-hours minds — less impulsiveness, earlier thought recognition, faster releasing — not measured by in-sitting pleasant feelings or visions. Learners wanting depth will need guides following the lineage.

5. Summary and next steps

  • The Trúc Lâm modern approach is a returning and summarizing: taking Trần-era texts as roots, re-presenting for today's Vietnamese — the "knowing delusion, not following it" formula.
  • The formula has three parts: knowing (mindfulness), delusion (drifting thoughts — the mind's normal business), not following (neither receiving, nor chasing) — exactly the "recognize — release gently — return" loop in Trúc Lâm language.
  • Comparing: sharing roots with thought observation (article M.C2.2) but more abridged; sharing the non-chasing spirit with shikantaza (article M.E2.5) but with a distincter acting step.
  • Progress measures lie in outer-hours minds, not in-sitting experiences; going deep needs guiding teachers.

Glossary:

  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
  • Trúc Lâm Zen school: the Zen lineage founded by Trần Nhân Tông around 1299, revived from the second half of the 20th century (article M.F1.2).
  • Meditation monasteries: organized meditation-practice places, with their own daily schedules — in the modern Trúc Lâm lineage usually joined to the revived school system.
  • Wandering thoughts (Chinese: wàngxiǎng): automatically drifting thoughts arising in mind — the "knowing delusion, not following it" formula's object.
  • Scenes (Chinese: jìng): in East Asian Zen language, what arises before mind — sounds, states, images...; "settled scenes" are settled states arising when minds settle.
  • Seated meditation: sitting meditation — balanced sitting postures, straight backs, released bodies (sitting postures already presented in article M.A4).
  • Four postures (Pali: iriyāpatha): walking, standing, sitting, and lying down — four postures of mindfulness practice.
  • Walking meditation (Pali: caṅkama): walking meditation — practising mindfulness while walking slowly, on par with sitting meditation.
  • Mindfulness (Pali: sati): remembering and staying with present objects — the basis capacity of all methods (group B).
  • Just sitting (Japanese: shikantaza): the Sōtō Zen lineage's "only sitting" practice — already presented in article E2.5.
  • Dharma lineage: teacher-student transmission lines — the concept already met in article F1.1.
  • Dharma methods: practice ways, mind-training roads belonging to particular traditions.
