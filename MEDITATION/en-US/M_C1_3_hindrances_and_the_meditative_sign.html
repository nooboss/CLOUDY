M.C1.3. Hindrances and the Meditative Sign

Once a subject is in hand, two kinds of phenomena begin to show more distinctly. The first kind is hindrances: familiar mental states — wanting, aversion, dullness, restlessness, doubt — each appearance scattering collectedness. The second kind is the meditative sign: the image of the subject appearing in the mind as collectedness deepens — a phenomenon that may be met and needs correct understanding, neither to miss nor to misread. This article handles both: the first part is the five hindrances and their antidotes; the second is the meditative sign and its three stages.

1. The five hindrances — obstacles to collectedness

1.1. The concept

Hindrances are states of mind that wrap around the mind and obscure calm and clarity. The canon lists the five hindrances (Pali: pañca nīvaraṇa); previous articles already named them collectively (articles M.B3, M.B4); now each is entered in turn.

The discourses use a homely image: like a deer coming to a pond to drink — the pond is the subject, where the mind must "drink" to grow calm — but around the pond, hunters have already scattered five kinds of bait. Whichever deer takes the bait abandons the pond, and the hunter masters it. The discourse concludes: one "swallowed" by the five loses collectedness and wisdom, whether practising or not. [Source: Nivāpa Sutta, Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 46.55 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

1.2. Each one — signs and antidotes

All five share one first step thoroughly learned in M.B3–M.B4: correct naming — "ah, this is present" — instead of sinking into it. After that, each has the tradition's familiar antidote:

  • Sense desire (Pali: kāmacchanda): signs — memories of liked things, daydreams of pleasure, seductive images crowding in. Antidote: clearly seeing the arising–passing nature of what attracts ("this too is impermanent"), or shifting attention to the neutral part of the subject; in the commentarial system, contemplating the instability of sense objects is the long-term remedy.
  • Ill will (Pali: byāpāda): signs — someone's irritating affair replaying, wanting to argue back, a hot feeling. Antidote: radiating loving-kindness (Pali: mettā) for a few minutes toward yourself, toward that person, or toward all; heat gradually dissolves where goodwill has room.
  • Sloth and torpor (Pali: thīna-middha): signs — hazy mind, nodding head, the idea that "a nap is also meditation". Antidote: straightening the back, opening the eyes slightly, a few minutes of walking meditation, several distinct extended breaths — exactly the principle learned in M.B5: a sluggish mind, nurture the warming factors (investigation, effort, joy). [Source: Aggi Sutta, Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 46.53 - Theravāda Buddhism.]
  • Restlessness and remorse (Pali: uddhacca-kukkucca): signs — mind job-hopping, fluttering in the chest, the mouth silently talking; or the mouth circling what was done wrong. Antidote: nurturing the settling factors (tranquillity, concentration, equanimity) — lengthening the out-breath; for remorse specifically, the good remedy is recalling wholesome deeds done and the ethical conduct kept — a mind with standing ground leaves regret no foundation.
  • Doubt (Pali: vicikicchā): signs — "is meditation for me?", "is this method right?", "months already and still the same, how can I progress?". Antidote: hindrance-doubt is not intelligent inquiry but weary doubting — the remedy is data: re-reading the journal to see how far you have actually come, asking someone experienced directly, and committing to try long enough. When concrete evidence is seen, doubt loses ground.

1.3. The right view of hindrances

  • They are weather, not mortal enemies. The discourses view them as "food" — with nourishing causes they strengthen, with causes removed they weaken. [Source: Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 46.2 - Theravāda Buddhism.] The handling is therefore not "fight to the death", but not feeding: not letting attention land where it strengthens them.
  • Hindrances and awakening factors are two sides. The same sluggish mind can be seen as "sloth" (hindrance) or transformed by "clear comprehension, effort, joy" (awakening factors). Nourishing the corresponding awakening factor dissolves the hindrance by itself — the discourses teach exactly in that direction. [Source: Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 46.51 - Theravāda Buddhism.]
  • Do not waste effort fining yourself. Beginners commonly meet all five in a week — normal; long-term practitioners also meet them, only seeing earlier and dissolving faster. The difference lies in speed of recognition and gentleness of antidote.

2. The meditative sign (Nimitta)

2.1. What the sign is

When collectedness on one subject grows deep enough, the subject is no longer an outer thing — an image of it forms in the mind. That image is called the meditative sign (Pali: nimitta). A simple picture: the real subject is the lamp; at first you look at the lamp; after a time, with the lamp out, the lamp's after-image still appears clearly in the mind's eye — that is where "sign" begins.

2.2. Three stages of the sign

The Theravāda commentarial system describes the sign in three stages, in the deepening order of collectedness: [Source: Visuddhimagga, ch. III–IV - the Theravāda commentarial system.]

  • Preliminary sign (Pali: parikamma-nimitta): the subject being trained itself — the candle being looked at, the breath being followed; nothing special yet, only the "material" of focusing.
  • Learning sign (Pali: uggaha-nimitta): after several seeings and rememberings, the subject appears by itself with eyes closed — still hazy, elusive, unstable, possibly still inaccurate against the real object.
  • Counterpart sign (Pali: paṭibhāga-nimitta): when collectedness enters access concentration, the remembered image turns into a clean, still, stable light — the discourses use it alone as the support for stepping deeper. This is the threshold that section M.C1.4 (levels of collectedness) will address.

2.3. Four things to know to avoid misreading

  • The sign is not the goal. The sign is an accompanying phenomenon when collectedness is deep enough — like foam at the edge of boiling water: a sign the water is boiling, not the meal. Seeking the sign instead of working rightly with the subject reverses the road.
  • Not everyone meets it; not every path uses it. Many quiet breath-training approaches and other meditation lineages never establish the sign concept at all — and still arrive fully. This is the description of one particular commentarial system, not a common standard of all traditions.
  • Do not pluck the sign. The state of "something about to happen" easily excites the mind — excitement is waves, waves dissolve the image. The right way: keep doing your part rightly on the subject; if the sign forms by itself, that is a token of deep collectedness; continue as before.
  • If joyful excitement comes with strange light: recall article M.B7 — delight in special experience is one of the practitioner's most misread places. At this stage, the simplest remedy is returning to the subject and letting the state pass by itself.

3. Summary and next steps

  • The five hindrances — sense desire, ill will, sloth and torpor, restlessness and remorse, doubt — are five "baits" around the mind's pond; the first step is correct naming, the second is per-hindrance antidote (loving-kindness, straightening the back and walking, lengthening the breath, looking at data); the general principle: do not feed, do not fight, nourish the corresponding awakening factor.
  • The meditative sign (nimitta) — the image of the subject — develops through three stages (preliminary → learning → counterpart) in the Theravāda commentarial system; it is a token of deep collectedness, not the goal, not the standard, not to be sought.
  • Both stress the same attitude: do your part rightly, and let phenomena come and go by themselves.

Glossary:

  • Five hindrances (Pali: pañca nīvaraṇa): the five states obstructing collectedness and wisdom — sense desire, ill will, sloth and torpor, restlessness and remorse, doubt.
  • Loving-kindness (Pali: mettā): goodwill wishing well for oneself and others; the canonical antidote to ill will, presented separately in group D2.
  • Seven factors of awakening (Pali: bojjhaṅga): the seven factors leading to awakening — mindfulness, investigation, effort, joy, tranquillity, concentration, equanimity; corresponding to the five hindrances (each factor the remedy for its hindrance).
  • Meditative sign (Pali: nimitta): the image of the subject appearing in the mind as collectedness deepens; in the Theravāda commentarial system it has three stages: preliminary, learning, and counterpart sign.
  • Access concentration: the level of collectedness near the threshold of absorption — deep enough for the counterpart sign to arise; presented in the app's "Three levels of concentration".
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Visuddhimagga (Pali: visuddhimagga): the compendium of meditation practice in Theravāda Buddhism, composed by the Elder Buddhaghosa around the 5th century.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
