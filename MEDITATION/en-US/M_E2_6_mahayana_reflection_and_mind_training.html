M.E2.6. Mahāyāna Reflection and Mind-Training Approaches

This article closes group E2 — the Mahāyāna tradition — with three directions belonging to the reflection and mind-training floor, exactly the map erected in article M.E2.1: mind training through slogans and giving–taking (lojong / tonglen — M142), Zen in Consciousness-Only (M138), and Zen with emptiness-nature (M070). These three directions are how Mahāyāna unfolds the "contemplation" part on the bodhicitta (Sanskrit: bodhicitta — the mind resolved on awakening for all beings) and loving-kindness – compassion basis; the article presents at introductory and map level — all advanced parts need specific sources and guides.

1. Mind training and giving–taking (Lojong / Tonglen)

Among Mahāyāna practices, this group holds a distinctive place: requiring no empowerment rites (initiation / empowerment — rites performed by teachers, met again in group E3), hence teachable widely — and hence also the group Vietnamese reading English meet most through modern books.

1.1. Lojong — training the mind through slogans

Lojong (Tibetan: blo sbyong — literally "training the mind") is the mind-training system through short slogan sentences, belonging to the Tibetan Kadampa tradition — originating with Master Atiśa (Sanskrit: Atīśa, 982–1054) transmitting from India to Tibet. Famous texts: Langri Tangpa's Eight Verses and Chekawa's Seven Points of Mind Training — synthesized into 59 slogan sentences.

The use is very simple and very "lay": memorizing one short sentence, then shining that sentence into daily life — especially into adversity. The slogan representative of this spirit: "Turn all adversity into the awakening road". No separate sitting needed to begin; training happens right at contact points — blame, failures, difficult persons — exactly the "meditation as life" spirit met in articles M.B6 and E2.2.

1.2. Tonglen — taking suffering, giving ease

Tonglen (Tibetan: gtong len — "giving and taking") is lojong's central practice, performed on the breath basis:

  • Breathing in — picturing taking others' suffering (as smoke, dark mist).
  • Breathing out — giving ease, brightness, cool wholesomeness.

This method's essence: an intentional visualization reversing mind habits — our default habit keeps pleasant parts for ourselves and pushes unpleasant parts outward; tonglen trains exactly the reverse. It is erected on the loving-kindness – compassion basis already learned in group M.D2 (articles M.D2.1, M.D2.2) — those without a loving-kindness – compassion basis often find this method abstract or heavy, hence the path placing group M.D2 first.

The object sequence resembles article M.D2.1: beginning with easy persons (oneself, loved persons), then gradually widening to neutral persons, difficult persons, and finally "all beings". The measure is not in-sitting feelings, but whether daily-life tolerance increases (article M.B7).

1.3. Safety warnings

  • Emotionally overloaded persons should adjust or temporarily postpone: because the "taking suffering" method operates through pain-aimed imagination, those in crisis phases, depression, or post-trauma may find unpleasant memories surfacing. Then: keeping only the "giving ease" part (dropping the breathing-in part), or temporarily switching to ordinary loving-kindness (article M.D2.1) — and with medical matters, returning to article M.A3 for load-reducing and grounding steps.
  • Tonglen does not replace therapy, and is not "loading genuine suffering into the body" — if practice leaves the body with prolonged heaviness, that signals wrong-level practice: slowing rhythm, opening the eyes, back to natural breath (articles M.A5, M.A3).

2. Zen in Consciousness-Only (Yogācāra) — overview

2.1. What is Consciousness-Only

Consciousness-Only (Sanskrit: yogācāra — literally "meditation conduct") is one of Mahāyāna's two philosophical pillars (beside the Middle Way system — section 3). The central idea: all experience appears through consciousness — what we call the "outer world" always arrives as images erected and known by mind; no experience lies outside this.

The system is systematized from the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra and treatises of Bodhisattvas Asaṅga and Vasubandhu — around the 4th century — through a cognition map of eight consciousnesses:

  • Five consciousnesses by five senses: eye, ear, nose, tongue, body — knowing corresponding objects.
  • The sixth consciousness (mind): processing concepts and thoughts — the mind's most familiar part.
  • The seventh consciousness (afflicted mind): the underground layer always affirming "there is a self" — the root of self-notions.
  • The eighth consciousness (storehouse) (Sanskrit: ālaya-vijñāna): the basis holding all "seeds" — traces of all experience, from which later experiences sprout.

Readers will recognize this "analysing experience into components" direction as familiar: Theravāda Abhidhamma does similarly with different conceptual frames (articles M.C2.4, M.E1.4). Both microscopically examining experience, different microscopes.

2.2. Meaning for contemplative practice — transformation of the basis

For meditation, Consciousness-Only redirects contemplation: contemplation is not viewing "outer objects" more carefully, but clearly seeing how mind erects experience — each seeing, hearing, and thinking a consciousness phenomenon. When this seeing ripens on a collectedness basis, the Consciousness-Only system describes a radical transformation called transformation of the basis (Sanskrit: āśraya-parāvṛtti) — the "overturning" of cognition's basis: the seed-holding basis purified, cognition no longer erecting images through self- and phenomenon-grasping. At this article's level, only understand transformation of the basis is the path's destination in the Consciousness-Only frame, equivalent to where "liberation" stands in other frames.

This thought lineage deeply influences East Asian Zen — Zen's "outside mind no phenomena" bears distinct Consciousness-Only marks, and Master Dōgen once studied Consciousness-Only before founding Japanese Sōtō (article M.E2.5). For beginners, this article is a map for reading books — practising within the Consciousness-Only frame needs sūtra-treatise sources and guiding teachers (article M.A6).

3. Zen and emptiness-nature (Madhyamaka / Prajñāpāramitā)

3.1. What is emptiness-nature — avoiding two misunderstandings

Emptiness (Sanskrit: śūnyatā) is Mahāyāna's central concept: all phenomena without separate, fixed, self-standing essences — everything appearing through converging conditions, hence nothing "standing firm alone by itself". Basis texts are the perfection-of-wisdom collection (Sanskrit: prajñāpāramitā — "wisdom carrying to the other shore", including the Heart and Diamond Sūtras), closely argued by the Middle Way (Sanskrit: madhyamaka) system of Bodhisattva Nāgārjuna (2nd century) in the Fundamental Verses.

Two misunderstandings to avoid at once:

  • Emptiness is not nihilism — "nothing at all". The Heart Sūtra says very compactly: "Form is emptiness, emptiness is form" — phenomena still fully appear, only not solid as imagined. Regarding emptiness as "nothing whatsoever" falls into nihilistic views — an error Mahāyāna sūtra-treatises themselves severely warn against.
  • Emptiness is also not a new "something" to grasp. It is not a state, a light region, or a place; grasping the word "emptiness" is also a grasping form.

3.2. Zen with emptiness-nature — two levels

  • The argument-analysis level: using analysis itself to strip grasping. Nāgārjuna's classical example: a chariot lies not in wheels, frame, body, or part assemblies; dissecting each part, no chariot is seen — hence "chariot" is a designation placed on converging parts, without a self-standing chariot essence. Performing this analysis on "self" and things — grasping at "fixed realities" gradually loosens.
  • The direct-contemplation level: with a collectedness basis, directly seeing all phenomena's unsolid nature right in living experience — without argument. This is exactly the Mahāyāna tranquillity – insight pair with "emptiness-marked" observation objects discussed in article M.E2.1, and where "true-mark" buddha-recitation merges into emptiness-nature (article M.E2.3) meets again.

A note per the taxonomy: the advanced part of this road needs basing on specific sources and schools — emptiness-explaining systems have subtle differences, un-self-mergeable from many books; for beginners, the reasonable level is conceptually understanding for reading books and meeting teachers when wanting depth (article M.A6). Additionally: in Theravāda, the wisdom floor also reaches "not-self" through direct seeing (article M.C2.3) — differing in conceptual frames and analysis ways, not in "one side having wisdom, one side not".

4. Where the three directions meet — and summary

Placing the three directions side by side shows the same structure: all are how Mahāyāna trains the "contemplation" part on the bodhicitta and loving-kindness – compassion basis — differing in force-placement:

  • Lojong / tonglen trains movement direction — attitudes toward others and daily adversity; the highest application level, easiest to begin (L3).
  • Consciousness-Only examines cognition's structure — how experience is erected; the analytical frame for contemplation (L4).
  • Emptiness-nature dismantles essence-grasping — Mahāyāna argument and contemplation's peak (L4).

Summary:

  • Lojong (mind training): training the mind through slogans, the Kadampa tradition; tonglen: breathing in taking suffering, breathing out giving ease — reversing mind habits; needing no empowerment yet emotionally overloaded persons need adjusting or postponing (article M.A3).
  • Consciousness-Only (Yogācāra): all experience appearing through eight consciousnesses; contemplation is seeing how mind erects experience; transformation of the basis (āśraya-parāvṛtti) is the destination — the cognition basis overturned; distinctly influencing East Asian Zen.
  • Emptiness (śūnyatā): all phenomena without fixed separate essences; not nihilism; Zen with emptiness has two levels — argument analysis and direct contemplation; advanced parts need specific sources and schools.
  • The three directions share a basis, differing in force-placement — by persons and lineages; the "one teacher – one sequence" principle (article M.A6) remains the compass when wanting depth.

Glossary:

  • Kadampa tradition (Tibetan: bka' gdams pa): the lineage started by Master Atiśa's students in Tibet, famous for lojong mind-training.
  • Atiśa (Sanskrit: Atīśa, 982–1054): the Mahāyāna master travelling from India to Tibet in the 11th century, initiating the Kadampa tradition.
  • Empowerment: rites performed by teachers, conditions for learning many Vajrayāna methods — met again in group E3.
  • Storehouse consciousness (Sanskrit: ālaya-vijñāna): the eighth consciousness in the Consciousness-Only system — the basis holding all experience's seeds (traces).
  • Transformation of the basis (Sanskrit: āśraya-parāvṛtti): "overturning" cognition's basis — the path's destination in the Consciousness-Only frame.
  • Middle Way (Sanskrit: madhyamaka): the Mahāyāna philosophical school initiated by Bodhisattva Nāgārjuna, arguing on emptiness.
  • Perfection of wisdom (Sanskrit: prajñāpāramitā): "wisdom carrying to the other shore" — the emptiness basis-sūtra collection name (Heart Sūtra, Diamond Sūtra...).
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
