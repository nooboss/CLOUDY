M.B4. Observing and Directing Experience

Previous articles have built the seat of attention: the mind knows how to return to the breath, knows how to recognize distraction, and holds a balanced attitude. This article adds two tools that raise the quality of knowing. One is noting / silent labeling — the technique of placing a short label on each experience as it happens, helping mindfulness grow more continuous and distinct. The other is wise attention — the art of directing attention to the right aspect of experience, regarded as the hinge joining attentional skills to insight meditation. The two go together: one records what is happening, the other decides from which angle to look.

1. Noting / silent labeling

1.1. What the technique is

In article M.B3, we already used short labels naturally: "thinking", "hearing". The noting technique makes this a systematic tool: when a distinct experience appears, give it a very short, silent, timely label — then return to observing:

  • Belly rising → silently "rising"; belly falling → silently "falling".
  • A vehicle passing → "hearing".
  • A thought surfacing → "thinking".
  • A pain in the leg → "pain".
  • A wave of discomfort rising → "unpleasant".

In English this technique is called noting or labeling. The practical rule: the label as short as possible (one word is best), silent in the head without moving the lips, placed in rhythm with the experience — like tapping a walking companion lightly on the shoulder, not like holding a press conference. Once the label has done its work, it is done: "knowing" stays pasted on the sensation, not on the word.

1.2. Origins

The seed of this technique is already in the discourses: the Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta repeats the words "clearly knows" — "breathing in long, one clearly knows 'breathing in long'; breathing in short, one clearly knows 'breathing in short'". [Source: Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 10 - Theravāda Buddhism.] This very act of "clearly knowing each separate phenomenon" was systematized into the labelling technique by Mahāsi Sayādaw in 20th-century Myanmar, and from there became one of the most widespread modern presentations of insight meditation (Pali: vipassanā).

1.3. What the technique helps

  • Keeps mindfulness continuous: each label is a marker — even with fragile experience (faint breath, fleeting sensations), labelling forces attention to "touch" it once more.
  • Recognizes distraction earlier: the label "thinking" is placed at the very moment of recognition — the span of being carried away shortens over sessions.
  • Creates a pause before reaction: correctly naming — "irritated" — by itself makes the irritation less sweeping; this is the "gap" form discussed in the previous article, now with a concrete tool.
  • Reveals the flow: after a time, a chain of labels ("rising – falling – hearing – itching – thinking – rising...") shows experience proceeding as a continuous stream, each moment replacing the next — the very doorway to observing arising – passing in the later part of the path.

1.4. Limits — when not to use labels

Labels are tools, not obligatory ritual. Four important notes:

  • Too-dense noting turns into thinking about experience: straining to label everything turns the sitting into "list-making" — tiring and tangled. When you see this, reduce labels to a minimum.
  • Coarse labels can tear fine experience: when the breath has refined and sensations settled — hard "rising", "falling" labels drop like stones thrown on a lake surface. When experience is subtle, leave it alone and only know.
  • Many traditions use no labels: quiet breath-training approaches, and other meditation lineages, use no labels at all yet still arrive fully — labelling is one way in, not the only road.
  • Words must not replace knowing: the test is very simple — after a label, are you clearer about what is happening, or hazier? Clearer, continue; hazier, drop the label and keep knowing.

2. Wise attention

2.1. Name and meaning

Wise attention (Pali: yoniso manasikāra) is the canonical term for directing attention to the right aspect of experience. "Attention" is the act of bringing the mind to touch an object — at every moment, everywhere, the mind is "attending" to something; "wise" (yoniso) means "according to the origin" ("yoni" literally means the place of arising, the root). Its opposite is unwise attention (Pali: ayoniso manasikāra) — the same event, but attention lands on the aspect that increases tangling.

The canon states plainly: unwise attention is the "food" that nourishes the five hindrances (Pali: pañca nīvaraṇa) — unarisen, they arise; arisen, they grow; conversely, wise attention is the condition by which mindfulness (Pali: sati) and concentration (Pali: samādhi) are fulfilled. [Source: Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 46.2 (nīvaraṇa-āhāra) and Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 8.81 (Sati-samādhi Sutta) - Theravāda Buddhism.] In everyday terms: in the same sitting, where attention "perches" decides whether that sitting sprouts or sinks.

2.2. Shifting the question: from "to me" to "this phenomenon"

In practice, wise attention shows most clearly as a very small redirection:

  • Unwise: "what is happening to me?" — attention lands on the plot, on the role of "I" in the story: disturbed by noise, tormented by pain, harassed by thoughts.
  • Wise: "how is this phenomenon arising and passing as such?" — attention lands on the arising–passing nature of the phenomenon itself: sound arising then ceasing, painful sensation changing beat by beat, thoughts popping up then dissolving.

The difference sounds small, the consequences large: the first direction grows tenser the longer you look (because "I" is always the victim or owner of the affair); the second, looked at long enough, shows everything changing by itself — and "I am enduring" fades, leaving changing sensation.

2.3. Examples in each situation

  • Aching legs: unwise — "why is today so miserable, I cannot bear it any more"; wise — "there is a painful sensation; it is hot, it throbs, it does not stand still — each second it differs; it does not equal the whole sitting".
  • Repeating thoughts: unwise — following the plot to its ending; wise — "there is a thought arising; it has passed; here is another thought arising".
  • Unpleasant sound: unwise — "the noise is ruining the meditation"; wise — "there is sound; it arises then ceases; and the mind reacting is also seen: 'flaring up' — that reaction too is a phenomenon".
  • Rising sadness: unwise — sinking into the sad story, continuing the tale in the head; wise — "there is sadness in the mind; this is a mental state present; where in the body is it? How is it changing?".

Note: the wise is not positive thinking — it does not deny discomfort, nor tell itself "actually everything is fine". It only places attention in the right place: the changing nature of phenomena, instead of the plot about "me". That is why this article is regarded as the hinge: meditation practical skills (articles M.B1 > M.B3) make attention stable; wise attention decides where attention perches; and when it is stable + perched rightly, the door of insight meditation — directly observing the arising – passing of body and mind — opens. The Insight section (article group M.C2) will walk through that door.

3. Summary and next steps

  • Noting / silent labeling: labels short, silent, in rhythm; they keep mindfulness continuous, recognize distraction early, and reveal the flowing stream of experience; but too-dense noting becomes thinking, coarse labels tear fine experience, and many traditions walk a labelless road — labels serve knowing, not replace it.
  • Wise attention: directing attention from "what is happening to me" to "how is this phenomenon arising – passing"; the unwise nourishes hindrances, the wise nourishes mindfulness and concentration; this is not positive thinking, but the hinge between attentional skills and insight meditation.
  • Two tools shared by all methods: whatever you sit with — breath, body, or mind — the questions "how is the label placed" and "where does attention perch" are always present.

Glossary:

  • Noting / silent labeling: the technique of placing a short, silent label on experience as it happens ("rising", "hearing", "thinking", "pain") in order to keep mindfulness continuous; systematized in modern insight meditation.
  • Mahāsi system: the presentation of insight meditation initiated by Mahāsi Sayādaw (Myanmar, 20th century), characterized by the noting technique and the rhythm of observing each phenomenon; the app has a separate article on the Burmese meditation systems.
  • Wise attention (Pali: yoniso manasikāra): directing attention to the right aspect of experience — the arising–passing nature of phenomena rather than the plot about "me"; "yoni" literally means the place of arising, the root.
  • Unwise attention (Pali: ayoniso manasikāra): directing the mind to the aspect that further agitates — blaming, storytelling, seeking perfection; the discourses liken it to "food" nourishing the hindrances.
  • Five hindrances (Pali: pañca nīvaraṇa): the five states obstructing collectedness and wisdom — sensual desire, ill will, dullness and drowsiness, restlessness and remorse, doubt; presented separately in one article of the app.
  • Insight meditation (Pali: vipassanā): the direction of practice observing experience in order to clearly see the changing nature and characteristics of body and mind.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
