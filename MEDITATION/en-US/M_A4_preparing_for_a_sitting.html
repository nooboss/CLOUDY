M.A4. Preparing for a Sitting

The two previous articles answered what to practise with (mindfulness and clear comprehension) and how to stay safe. This article turns to the first concrete step of a session: preparation. It has three parts — preparing outer conditions, choosing a posture, and setting the duration. One principle runs throughout: keep preparation as simple as possible — do not let getting ready become the biggest obstacle on the way to the cushion.

1. Preparing before you meditate

The purpose of preparation is very practical: to reduce excuses for stopping halfway — a vibrating phone, a family member calling, tight clothing, a back-aching seat. You do not need a perfect space; just good enough that the mind is not pulled away by trifles.

1.1. Choosing a space

  • Relatively quiet is enough. You do not need a soundproof room. A corner of a room, the edge of a bed, or a chair on the balcony all work, as long as few people pass by for the next 10 minutes.
  • Soft light, comfortable temperature. A room too dark invites drowsiness or fear; one too bright with harsh sun dazzles. Gentle light and cool air are ideal.
  • Keep to one spot if you can. Sitting in the same place each day creates a familiar cue: the mind settles into gear faster, like sleepiness arriving as soon as you lie down in your own bed.

1.2. Choosing a time

  • Early morning after waking is favoured by many traditions: the mind is still quiet, surroundings still calm, and what is done first in the day cannot be "taken away" by later business.
  • Evening before sleep is also good for unwinding the day's tension — but choose a gentle practice, not close-focus work near bedtime.
  • Avoid sitting right after a heavy meal: the body sends blood to digestion and easily grows dull and drowsy. Wait about an hour.
  • Avoid sitting when highly agitated. Just after an argument, just rushing home to pick up the children — take a few minutes to walk about or a few extended breaths first, then sit. A highly stirred person who sits often turns the sitting into a battle.
  • Most important is not a beautiful hour, but a fixed hour. The same time each day is the firmest stairway to building a habit. "Building a meditation habit" (article M.A6) treats this more deeply.

1.3. Clothing and equipment

Clothing: choose loose, stretchy clothes; loosen the belt, remove watch and glasses if you can. In cold weather, drape a thin blanket over the legs — cold feet are one of the most common reasons beginners stand up halfway.

The minimum equipment is just two things:

  • A stable seat — a straight-backed chair or a thin mat on the floor. If sitting on the floor, a cushion under the hips (about 7–15 cm high) raises the hips above the knees, so the back straightens by itself and tires far less. No need to buy a specialized meditation cushion; a folded sofa cushion is enough for the first few months.
  • A timer with a gentle bell (a wooden bell, a singing bowl). A timer lets you drop the question "how much longer?" — one of the biggest distractions for beginners.

1.4. Noise and how to handle it

Do not aim for absolute silence — it rarely exists, and "guarding against noise" itself becomes the distraction. The right understanding:

  • Steady background sound (a fan, falling rain, insects) disturbs far less than sudden sound (a horn, a message chime, footsteps passing). A small fan both masks and cools.
  • When noise floods in, do not resist it: note "there is sound" and return to the object; or even use sound itself as the practice object (article M.C1, "Calm abiding with sound").
  • In a crowded house: announce beforehand "for about 10 minutes I need quiet", or choose an earlier hour before everyone wakes.

1.5. Small things to do right before sitting

  • Switch the phone fully to silent (including vibration), and set the timer before sitting down.
  • Personal hygiene, a sip of water — so the body does not "ask permission" mid-sitting.
  • A few gentle stretches of neck, shoulders, and back ("Releasing bodily tension" in article M.B1 guides each area).
  • Sit down 1–2 minutes early and take a few slow, extended breaths — a "buffer" that shifts from everyday speed to practice speed, instead of starting the timer and sitting abruptly.

2. Meditation posture

Three keywords for every posture: stable (firm, needing no constant adjustment), alert (not so comfortable that you doze), pain-free (lingering pain breeds further agitation and occupies the mind). The canon describes the sitting posture within the four establishments of mindfulness (Pali: satipaṭṭhāna) as: "sitting with legs crossed, holding the body upright, establishing mindfulness (Pali: sati) in front". [Source: Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 10 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

The discourses also list the four postures (Pali: iriyāpatha) — walking, standing, sitting, lying down — in all of which mindfulness can be practised — meaning there is no single "orthodox" posture. The options below are reviewed in turn so you can choose what suits your body.

2.1. Sitting on a chair — the easiest start

Sitting on a chair is fully legitimate and the first choice for beginners, those with back or joint pain, or pregnant practitioners:

  • Both feet fully on the floor, hip-width apart.
  • Back upright in a natural way, without bracing — you may lean lightly against the backrest if in pain; leaning is far better than abandoning meditation because of pain.
  • Both hands resting on the thighs, palms down or up — choose one way and keep it for the whole sitting.
  • Chin slightly tucked; imagine the crown of the head lifted by a fine thread, with the rest of the body hanging loose below.

2.2. Sitting on the floor

If you are used to the floor, three common ways:

  • Simple cross-legged sitting: both legs folded neatly in front, knees touching the floor (or supported by cushions beneath), neither leg on top of the other — the easiest way; start here.
  • Half-lotus: one shin resting on the opposite calf.
  • Full lotus: both feet crossed upon each other: not required and not "more advanced" than other postures — that is a common misconception. A difficult posture with aching joints only turns the sitting into a wrestling match with the knees instead of with the mind.

A few tips for floor sitting: place a cushion under the hips so they sit higher than the knees; alternate which leg is in front between sittings; when a leg goes numb, clearly know "the leg is numb" and then change legs slowly — that itself is one round of mindful movement practice.

2.3. Kneeling

Both knees on the floor, heels pressed to either side of the hips, hips resting on a low bench or a cushion between the heels (the traditional Japanese way of sitting, called seiza). This suits people with stiff hip joints but healthy knees. If a leg numbs, stretch it out, rub gently, and change posture — do not force.

2.4. Lying down

For times of fatigue, severe back pain, or illness: lie on the back, legs extended naturally, arms alongside the body, or lie on the right side — the classic lying posture in the canon. The only weakness of this posture is that it easily leads to sleep. If you fall asleep, that is not failure — usually the body is collecting a sleep debt; but note it and choose a more wakeful hour to sit, because lying down routinely will turn the sitting into a nap. Those with insomnia may deliberately use the lying posture in the evening ("Meditation for sleep", article M.G2.7).

2.5. Walking meditation — the "moving" posture

When sitting numbs the legs or the mind grows drowsy, alternate with walking meditation (Pali: caṅkama): choose a path of 5–10 steps, walk slower than usual, clearly knowing each step of lifting – moving – placing. Alternating sitting and walking eases numb legs, brightens the mind, and lengthens the session. The discourses list five benefits of walking meditation: it builds stamina for long journeys, it arouses effort, it keeps the body healthy, it aids digestion, and the collectedness gained while walking endures. Article M.B6 guides it in detail.

[Source: Caṅkama Sutta, Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 5.29 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

2.6. Small details: eyes, jaw, and itches

  • Eyes: gently closed is most common; or lower the lids three-quarters, gazing softly about a metre ahead. Those prone to drowsiness should open the eyes slightly; those easily scattered by sights should close them.
  • Jaw and tongue: jaw relaxed, lips lightly closed, tip of the tongue lightly touching the upper palate; swallow when needed, quite naturally.
  • Once the body is settled: the most familiar object on which to place the mind is the breath — mindfulness of breathing (Pali: ānāpānassati). Articles in M.B1 guide it from the simplest level.
  • Itches: first simply know "there is an itch"; if it persists, scratch consciously and slowly — a mindful scratch is still practice, an unconscious scratch is distraction.

To repeat the principle of this whole section: no posture is required for meditation "to work". The best posture for you is the one that keeps all three keywords — stable, alert, pain-free — for your particular body, not the full-lotus image you once saw.

3. Session length and frequency

3.1. Start short

Five to ten minutes per sitting is a reasonable start for a beginner; on the very first day, three to five minutes is already precious. The reason lies not in technique but in habit: a long sitting from the outset exposes every difficulty at once (numb legs, scattered mind, drowsiness), leading to discouragement and quitting altogether. A small win each day nourishes motivation more durably than a rare grand battle.

3.2. Regular beats long

Five minutes a day for seven days is worth more than one hour once a week — because mind-training skill grows through closely repeated sessions, not total time. The canon uses a very homely image: wholesome qualities accumulate like drops of water — drop by drop filling the jar, not by pouring a whole bucket at once. [Source: Dhammapada, verse 122 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

A practical tip for staying regular: attach the sitting to something already in the day — after morning tooth-brushing, after coffee, after the children leave for school, after the evening shower. The old habit pulls the new one along.

3.3. When to lengthen, and by how much

  • The signal to lengthen is a sense of "wanting more": when ten minutes ends and your back is not yet tired and your mind not yet reluctant — that is the time to lengthen, not any schedule on paper.
  • Pace of lengthening: add about five minutes every one to two weeks if all remains well; do not increase every day.
  • A sustainable long-term level: fifteen to twenty minutes a day, or two ten-minute sittings, is a level many people sustain for years. More is good if life allows — but do not compete with anyone over time; "sitting long" is not a measure of attainment (see again the "longer is better" misconception in article M.A2, "Understanding How to Practice").

3.4. Signs of over-sitting — and how to step back

The signs: backache lingering beyond the sitting, a sense of dread whenever sitting time comes, dozing through sittings, or dropping practice for several days in a row. When you see this, do not push on — step back to the previous level and hold it steady for one to two weeks before considering more (detailed adjustments are in section 3 of article M.A3, "Safety in Meditation"). The sustainable curve of practice is a zigzag climbing upward, not a steep straight line.

4. Summary and next steps

  • Choose a seat: relatively quiet, soft light, comfortable temperature; keep to this spot if you can.
  • Phone fully silent, a gentle-belled timer set for 5–10 minutes.
  • Loose clothing, watch and glasses off; a thin blanket over the legs if cold.
  • Sit on a chair (safest for beginners) or on the floor with a cushion under the hips; posture upright and natural.
  • Check the three keywords: stable — alert — pain-free.
  • Eyes gently closed or lowered; jaw relaxed.
  • Gentle neck–shoulder–back stretches; a few extended breaths before beginning.
  • Daily regularity at the same hour; lengthen gradually when a sense of "wanting more" appears.

Glossary:

  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
  • Four establishments of mindfulness (Pali: satipaṭṭhāna): the practice framework of four domains of contemplation — body, feelings, mind, dhammas; the sitting posture described in this article appears in the body-contemplation part of this framework.
  • Mindfulness (Pali: sati): remembering and staying with the present object; the phrase "establishing mindfulness in front" in the discourse means placing mindfulness ready on the object as soon as one sits down.
  • Four postures (Pali: iriyāpatha): walking, standing, sitting, and lying down — mindfulness can be present in all four; there is no single "sole orthodox" posture.
  • Walking meditation (Pali: caṅkama): slow walking with mindfulness, clearly knowing each step; "kinh hành" is the familiar Sino-Vietnamese word used in monasteries.
  • Mindfulness of breathing (Pali: ānāpānassati): mindfulness with the in- and out-breath — the most familiar object on which to place the mind once the body is settled.
  • Half-lotus: the cross-legged sitting posture with one shin resting on the opposite calf.
  • Full lotus: the cross-legged sitting posture with both feet crossed upon each other; not required and not "more advanced" than other postures.
  • Seiza (Japanese): the traditional Japanese kneeling way of sitting — knees on the floor, hips resting on a low bench or between the heels.
