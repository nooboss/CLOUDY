1. What is Theravāda?

Theravāda Buddhism (Pali: theravāda) is the Buddhist tradition widespread in Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand, Laos, Cambodia, and southern Vietnam. The name speaks two sides: thera means "elder" (senior monastics preserving the transmitted teaching), vāda means "doctrine" — literally "the Doctrine of the Elders"; its scriptures are recorded in Pali.

A brief history: after the Buddha's final passing, the teaching was compiled through Buddhist councils (assemblies of elders reciting – comparing – settling texts); this tradition preserves the earliest compilation lineage, transmitted to the island of Sri Lanka around the 3rd century BCE in King Asoka's time — per Theravāda chronicles — then spreading across Southeast Asia. [Source: Mahāvaṃsa - Theravāda Buddhism.] The running character: weighting the Pali canon, monastic life, and preserving the teaching's original character as a central value — in other words, Theravāda pays special attention to doing things with distinct roots in the discourses.

2. Foundational scriptures — the Pali Three Baskets and the commentarial layer

The tradition's scriptures are called the Three Baskets (Pali: tipiṭaka):

  • Vinaya (Pali: vinaya): the precepts and procedures of monastic life — already met through the rains-retreat rules;
  • Sutta (Pali: sutta): the Buddha's teachings — five Nikāyas, housing most of articles A–D's meditation contents;
  • Abhidhamma (Pali: abhidhamma): the system analysing mind – matter into basic parts, the teaching's "schematized" presentation.

Above the three baskets lies the commentarial layer (Pali: aṭṭhakathā) and treatise systems — most notably the Visuddhimagga (article M.E1.4 deepens the textual map). This layering matters for correct reading — previous articles repeatedly noted "canonical layer" or "commentarial layer": for example the four establishments of mindfulness, mindfulness of breathing, and divine abodes belong to the canonical layer; while the forty-subject system (group D3), three concentration levels (article M.C1.4), and progress of insight (article M.C2.5) are commentarial systematizations. Both layers are the tradition's assets — but layering helps knowing which "speaks nearest the discourses" and which is a later synthesized map.

3. "Theravāda" is not synonymous with a single technique

This is what this article must seal most (exactly the taxonomy's main content). A very common misunderstanding: people meet "Theravāda" in one retreat, book, or app — then assign the tradition's name to that course's very technique: "Theravāda is sitting noting rising-falling belly", "Theravāda is scanning sensations for ten days", "Theravāda is counting breaths". Each sentence describes one particular lineage — not the tradition.

In fact: within Theravāda exist many parallel implementations, sharing roots in canon and commentaries but differing in four points (the standard comparison criteria of group M.E1.3):

  • Principal subject: one lineage takes the breath, another belly movements, another mind and attitude;
  • Use of collectedness: some lineages go to deep jhāna first then observe, some need only momentary concentration and go straight to observing (the combining models in article M.C1.5);
  • Noting technique: some lineages use dense silent labels, some use no labels;
  • Retreat structure: dense – silent schedules of some lineages, or at-home self-practice of others.

Theravāda resembles a shared house with many rooms: same walls – roof (canon, four establishments, tranquillity – insight), each room its own decoration. Hence fair comparison is neutral description by the four criteria above, not ranking "right – wrong" or "high – low"; and for practitioners, the principle remains one teacher – one lineage – one time (article M.A6), walking long enough then evaluating (article M.B7).

4. The shared practice frame — the tradition's "language"

Though lineages differ in technique, they share one frame — exactly the articles already learned:

  • The four establishments of mindfulness (group M.D1) — the four-domain observation frame of body, feeling, mind, dhammas;
  • Mindfulness of breathing — the most central subject, with the sixteen-step text (advanced L4 article);
  • Tranquillity and insight meditation — two complementary directions; combining models (article M.C1.5);
  • The forty-subject system (group D3) — the commentarial subject map;
  • Concentration levels (article M.C1.4) and progress of insight (article M.C2.5) — the commentarial road maps;
  • The four divine abodes (group M.D2) — the shared emotional – ethical basis of all lineages.

Hence the app's ordering placing group E after groups C – D is reasonable: by now you have enough "vocabulary" to read how each lineage uses the shared frame in its own way — instead of meeting a series of lineage names with strange techniques without knowing where they differ.

5. A map of modern branches

At map level (in detail in articles M.E1.3), modern implementations divide into two clusters:

  • The Burmese cluster — where modern Theravāda meditations were born earliest and most influential: the theoretical foundation of Ledi Sayādaw (late 19th century), the noting lineage of Mahāsi Sayādaw, the sensation-scanning lineage of U Ba Khin – S. N. Goenka, the commentarial deep-collectedness lineage of Pa-Auk Sayādaw, and the mind-observation direction of U Tejaniya — presented neutrally, unranked;
  • The Thai cluster — the Forest Tradition (monastics living meditation in forests: Ajahn Mun, Ajahn Chah and successors — stressing ethics, lifestyle, the "Buddho" subject), and Buddhadāsa's distinct breath presentation.

The above lineages are only modern readings and workings on the shared basis — not separate "religions"; meeting any lineage name, article M.E1.3 will describe by section 3's four criteria.

6. Theravāda in Vietnam

Theravāda Buddhism is present in Vietnam through two main streams: (1) the southern Khmer stream — long present, tightly joined to Khmer temples and community life; (2) the Vietnamese Theravāda stream — forming its own Saṅgha in the 20th century, with Theravāda temples in Saigon and many southern provinces. The canon is translated into Vietnamese from Pali sources; practice life has its own marks: robe-offering rites, longevity-wishing rites, group sittings on festival occasions. This article only briefly locates — the detailed map of meditation practice in Vietnam (including Chinese-origin Zen lineages, Trúc Lâm...) is group F1 — Meditation Practice in Vietnamese Buddhism's content.

Glossary:

  • Theravāda (Pali: theravāda): the "Doctrine of the Elders" tradition; the familiar Vietnamese address: Southern Buddhism.
  • Elder (Pali: thera): senior monastics preserving and transmitting the teaching; the root of Theravāda.
  • Three Baskets (Pali: tipiṭaka): Vinaya Piṭaka, Sutta Piṭaka, Abhidhamma Piṭaka — the whole Theravāda canon.
  • Commentaries (Pali: aṭṭhakathā): canonical exegesis systems systematized from around the 5th century — the tradition's "second" textual layer.
  • Buddhist councils: assemblies of elders reciting – comparing – settling the teaching's texts after the Buddha's final passing.
  • Mahāvaṃsa (Pali: mahāvaṃsa): the Pali chronicle of Sri Lanka — historical source on Theravāda Buddhism reaching the island.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
