M.A1. Understanding Meditation

This article is for complete beginners. It answers three foundational questions: what meditation really is, how Buddhist meditation differs from secular meditation, and where meditation sits within the Buddhist path as a whole. Understanding these three points before you sit down will help you avoid most of the common misunderstandings later on.

1. What is meditation?

1.1. The origin of the word "meditation"

The Vietnamese word thiền is a shortened form of thiền-na, the Sino-Vietnamese transcription of jhāna (Pali: jhāna, Sanskrit: dhyāna), which denotes a state of deep, stable, and calm collectedness of mind.

The canonical term for the whole undertaking, however, is broader: mental cultivation (Pali and Sanskrit: bhāvanā, literally "causing to develop"). In other words, jhāna is a state that can be attained, while bhāvanā is the process of training the mind. When Vietnamese speakers say "practising meditation", the meaning is usually closer to bhāvanā.

1.2. A workable definition

For a beginner, meditation can be understood as the deliberate and methodical training of the mind, with three elements that always go together:

  • Directing the mind to a chosen object — called a meditation subject (Pali: kammaṭṭhāna), for example the breath, bodily sensations, or loving-kindness.
  • Clearly knowing what is happening as it happens — this is mindfulness (Pali: sati), understood as "remembering and staying with the present object", not merely "paying attention".
  • Holding the right attitude: patient, unforced, and without judging yourself when the mind wanders.

Without the third element, sitting in meditation very easily turns into a tense struggle with yourself.

1.3. What meditation is not

  • It is not emptying the mind. Thoughts arising is the normal activity of the mind. The meditator's task is to recognize "I am thinking" and gently return to the object — that very returning is the exercise.
  • It is not the same as relaxation. Relaxation often appears as a side effect, but many stages of practice are not at all comfortable.
  • It does not require sitting in full lotus. The discourses speak of four postures: walking, standing, sitting, and lying down. Walking meditation (Pali: caṅkama) is a complete practice in its own right.
  • It is not hunting for strange experiences. Seeing lights, visions, or feeling elated is not a measure of progress.
  • It is not a way of escaping problems. On the contrary, meditation often makes you see more clearly what is already in the mind.

1.4. The two core directions of practice

Although there are dozens of different techniques, the discourses group them into two complementary directions:

Tranquillity meditation (Pali: samatha)

  • How it is practised: gathering the mind onto one object until the mind settles and steadies.
  • Result: collectedness, calm, and a mind that wavers less.
  • Examples: following the breath, radiating loving-kindness.

Insight meditation (Pali: vipassanā)

  • How it is practised: observing body, feelings, mind, and dhammas as they actually are.
  • Result: clearly seeing the impermanent and ungraspable nature of all experience.
  • Example: noting sensations arising and passing in the body.

[Source: the Buddha taught that two things are to be cultivated: tranquillity and insight — Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 2.31 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

2. Buddhist meditation and secular meditation

Today the word "meditation" is used for two quite different things, and confusing them is the source of many misunderstandings.

2.1. What is secular meditation?

Secular meditation refers to programmes that borrow techniques from the Buddhist tradition but present them independently of religion, oriented toward health and daily life. The best known are Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR), founded by Jon Kabat-Zinn in 1979 at the University of Massachusetts Medical Center, and Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT), used in preventing relapse into depression. There are also applications in sports, business, and many other fields.

2.2. What they share

  • Both use the same foundational skills: anchoring the mind on the breath or bodily sensations, noticing distraction, and returning.
  • Both bring observable benefits: less impulsive reactivity, better sleep, less stress.
  • Both require regular practice to bear fruit.

2.3. The key differences

Meditation in Buddhism:

  • Goal: ending suffering at its root, reaching liberation — nirvana (Pali: nibbāna, Sanskrit: nirvāṇa).
  • Framework: the Four Noble Truths and the teaching of not-self (explained below).
  • Ethics: a required foundation that cannot be separated (see section 3).
  • Measure: defilements decrease, wisdom grows.

Secular meditation:

  • Goal: improving mental health, focus, work performance, and quality of life.
  • Framework: psychology, medicine, and clinical research.
  • Ethics: usually not included as part of the programme.
  • Measure: stress scores, symptoms, feelings of ease.

2.4. The Four Noble Truths and the teaching of not-self

These two concepts are the essential background for understanding what Buddhist meditation is directed toward. Simply put, the Four Noble Truths explain the problem of suffering and the way to end suffering, while not-self looks deeply into present experience to see that there is no fixed, independent "self" fully in control of everything.

The Four Noble Truths (Pali: cattāri ariyasaccāni, Sanskrit: catvāry āryasatyāni) can be understood in four points:

  • Suffering (Pali: dukkha, Sanskrit: duḥkha): life contains unsatisfactoriness — what we want to keep changes, what we do not want can still happen, and even pleasant states cannot be held forever.
  • The origin of suffering: craving and clinging (Pali: taṇhā) that demand experience conform to our wishes and identify it as "I" or "mine".
  • The cessation of suffering: when craving and clinging are removed, suffering ceases — this points toward nirvana.
  • The path leading to the cessation of suffering: the Noble Eightfold Path (Pali: ariya aṭṭhaṅgika magga), the path of practice with eight factors: right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration.

The teaching of not-self (Pali: anattā, Sanskrit: anātman) does not simply mean "this body does not exist". Its main point is: what we usually call "myself" — body, feelings, perceptions, thoughts, volitions, and consciousness — all change, depend on many conditions, and are not fully under the control of a fixed "self".

Therefore, in meditation, instead of immediately thinking "this is me" or "this is mine", we learn to observe directly: feelings arising and passing, the mind now glad then sad, thoughts appearing and disappearing. Seeing this process clearly loosens clinging to experience and illuminates the three characteristics of impermanence, suffering, and not-self. [Source: the Four Noble Truths — Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 56.11]

2.5. So which should you choose?

The two directions do not exclude each other. Secular meditation is an accessible doorway, has scientific evidence for certain benefits, and is entirely suitable if you simply need less stress. What must be avoided is confusing the two goals: expecting an eight-week course to resolve the matter of birth and death, or conversely assuming meditation is only a relaxation tool and dismissing its entire depth.

3. Meditation within the three trainings — ethics, concentration, wisdom

This is the part most often skipped, yet it explains why the tradition never separates meditation from a way of life.

3.1. The three trainings

The whole path of practice is summarized in the three trainings (Pali: tisso sikkhā):

  • Ethics (Pali: sīla): a way of living that does no harm to oneself or others.
  • Concentration (Pali: samādhi): the stability, collectedness, and workability of the mind.
  • Wisdom (Pali: paññā, Sanskrit: prajñā): seeing experience as it truly is.

The eight factors of the Noble Eightfold Path are grouped under these three: right speech, right action, and right livelihood belong to ethics; right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration belong to concentration; right view and right intention belong to wisdom. [Source: Cūḷavedalla Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 44, where the bhikkhunī Dhammadinnā presents this grouping - Theravāda Buddhism.]

In this way, meditation practice sits entirely within concentration, and serves wisdom. It is one part of the mechanism, not the whole mechanism.

3.2. Why must ethics come first?

The reason is very practical and psychological: someone who has just done something they regret carries that unease onto the cushion. A remorseful mind cannot settle. The discourses describe a natural sequence: keeping ethics → freedom from remorse → gladness → tranquillity of body and mind → happiness → concentration → seeing things as they are → disenchantment, dispassion → liberation. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 11.2 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

For lay practitioners, this foundation is usually the five precepts (Pali: pañca-sīla): not killing, not taking what is not given, refraining from sexual misconduct, not lying, and not using intoxicants that cloud the mind. The more fully they are kept, the lighter the mind — and that evening's sitting will be quite different.

3.3. What concentration does — and does not do

Concentration makes the mind less scattered, more pliant and usable, like sharpening a blade. At deep levels it leads to the four jhānas. But concentration by itself is not the goal: a very calm mind that is not used for observation only suppresses defilements temporarily; it does not uproot them.

3.4. What does wisdom do?

Wisdom uses the stabilized mind to look directly at experience and recognize the three characteristics (Pali: tilakkhaṇa): impermanence (Pali: anicca), suffering or unsatisfactoriness (Pali: dukkha), and not-self — there is no fixed "self" standing behind as master. It is this seeing, not the pleasant feeling while sitting, that changes the mind at its root.

3.5. The three support one another

The three trainings are not three rungs to climb one after another, but a spiral: keeping ethics helps the mind settle; a settled mind sees clearly; clear seeing naturally makes one unwilling to do harmful deeds, so ethics grows firmer still. [Source: Dhammapada, verse 372 - Theravāda Buddhism, expressing the relationship between meditation and wisdom: there is no meditation in one without wisdom, nor wisdom in one without meditation.]

3.6. What does this mean for a lay practitioner?

  • Daily life is part of the practice, not something that interrupts the sittings. How you speak, work, and earn a living all belong to ethics.
  • Sitting briefly but regularly is worth more than sitting long but irregularly — 10 to 15 minutes a day is a reasonable start.
  • When meditation stalls, instead of changing techniques, check two places: whether something in daily life is causing unease, and whether the attitude in practice has become too forceful.

All three major traditions keep the framework of the three trainings, differing only in how they elaborate it: Mahāyāna Buddhism expands it into the six perfections (Sanskrit: pāramitā) grounded in bodhicitta; Vajrayāna Buddhism keeps the same foundation of ethics, concentration, and wisdom but adds practices that require a teacher's guidance and lineage transmission.

4. Summary and next steps

  • Meditation is methodical training of the mind (bhāvanā), not emptying the mind and not merely relaxing.
  • All techniques come down to two complementary directions: tranquillity calms the mind, insight clarifies it.
  • Secular and Buddhist meditation share skills but differ in goal and framework; each has its own place, provided they are not confused.
  • In Buddhism, meditation belongs to concentration within the three trainings, stands on ethics, and points toward wisdom. Separated from this framework, meditation remains useful, but it is no longer the path described in the discourses.

Glossary:

  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
  • Jhāna (Pali: jhāna, Sanskrit: dhyāna): a state of deep, stable, and calm collectedness of mind; the Vietnamese word "thiền" is the shortened Sino-Vietnamese transcription "thiền-na".
  • Mental cultivation (Pali, Sanskrit: bhāvanā): literally "causing to develop" — the whole methodical process of training the mind; "practising meditation" in this broad sense is more than a single state.
  • Meditation subject (Pali: kammaṭṭhāna): the chosen object on which the mind is placed in practice — the breath, bodily sensations, loving-kindness.
  • Tranquillity meditation (Pali: samatha): the practice of gathering the mind onto one object until it settles and steadies; its result is collectedness.
  • Insight meditation (Pali: vipassanā): the practice of observing body, feelings, mind, and dhammas as they are, in order to see the true nature of experience.
  • Nirvana (Pali: nibbāna, Sanskrit: nirvāṇa): the final goal of the Buddhist path — the complete ending of suffering and its causes.
  • Four Noble Truths (Pali: cattāri ariyasaccāni): the four truths of suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path leading to its cessation.
  • Not-self (Pali: anattā, Sanskrit: anātman): there is no fixed, independent "self" fully controlling the elements of experience; body and mind both arise and change according to conditions.
  • Three characteristics (Pali: tilakkhaṇa): the three universal characteristics of all experience — impermanence (Pali: anicca), suffering / unsatisfactoriness (Pali: dukkha), not-self (Pali: anattā).
  • Noble Eightfold Path (Pali: ariya aṭṭhaṅgika magga): the eight-factored path leading to the cessation of suffering; mindfulness is one factor of this path.
  • Three trainings (Pali: tisso sikkhā): the three divisions of Buddhist training — ethics (Pali: sīla), concentration (Pali: samādhi), wisdom (Pali: paññā, Sanskrit: prajñā).
  • Five precepts (Pali: pañca-sīla): the five basic ethical commitments of lay practitioners — not killing, not taking what is not given, refraining from sexual misconduct, not lying, and not using intoxicants that cloud the mind.
  • Walking meditation (Pali: caṅkama): slow walking with mindfulness, clearly knowing each step — a complete practice, not merely a substitute for sitting.
  • Four postures (Pali: iriyāpatha): walking, standing, sitting, and lying down — mindfulness can be present in all four.
  • Perfections (Sanskrit: pāramitā): the perfected qualities elaborated in Mahāyāna Buddhism — generosity, ethics, patience, effort, meditation, wisdom; "six perfections" is the familiar list.
  • Awakening (Pali and Sanskrit: bodhi): awakening — clearly seeing the true nature and being fully liberated; "bodhicitta" is the mind directed toward awakening for oneself and all beings.
  • Bhikkhunī (Pali: bhikkhunī): a fully ordained nun in Theravāda Buddhism; the one who presents the Cūḷavedalla Sutta discussed in this article.
  • Mental defilements (Pali: kilesa): states of mind that cause suffering and obscure — greed, hatred, delusion, conceit, doubt; "defilements decrease" is a reliable measure of progress.
  • Meditation cushion (Sino-Vietnamese): the practitioner's seat (a stool or cushion for sitting meditation); in the app it refers generally to the place of practice.
