M.B7. Tracking and Reflecting on Practice

By now the skills are complete: from releasing the body, following the breath, holding and sustaining attention, working with distraction, noting and wise attention, balancing the five faculties, to carrying practice into daily life. The final article of the group answers a very practical question: how do you know you are progressing? There is no scoreboard for training the mind — on the contrary, surface measures often mislead. This article provides two tools: a minimalist journal for recording, and a filter for distinguishing genuine signs of progress from easily misread ones.

1. Why track practice

The first thing to admit: the feeling after a sitting is very easily mistaken. A "smooth, pleasant" sitting may only be drowsiness drifting by unnoticed; a "difficult, buzzing-minded" sitting may be the sitting of sharpest knowing — because for the first time you clearly see the mind's real noisiness, long present but never before looked at. Trusting only per-sitting feelings steers wrongly, like driving by the nearest lamppost instead of the map.

Second: the mind tends to remember what was wonderful and magnify what was unpleasant, so the picture of "my progress" after a few weeks is often skewed from reality. The canon encourages self-examination in exactly this spirit: "Oneself is one's own refuge; let oneself control oneself" — Dhammapada, verse 160 - Theravāda Buddhism; and the Upakkilesa Sutta teaches learners to periodically question themselves about defilements present in the mind in order to gradually remove them — like checking crops by season, not uprooting the whole field daily to look. [Source: Upakkilesa Sutta, Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 10.51 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

Proper tracking is the feedback loop of training: record — view the chain over weeks — draw one small adjustment — continue. No more.

2. A meditation journal — what and how to note

2.1. Principles: short, regular, right after the sitting

A daily journal needs only twenty seconds and a few lines. Write right after standing up — memory starts embellishing very quickly. Three rules: short (long entries will be abandoned after a week), regular (missing one day is fine, never miss two days in a row — the rule already met in habit building), honest (record what is true, not what "should have" happened).

2.2. The two-line template

An example of a minimalist record for one sitting:

  • Time – duration – subject: "6:15 a.m. — 15 minutes — counting the breath".
  • State line: "today mainly... (thinking / drowsy / relatively calm); returned about... times".
  • One memorable sentence: "first time noticing the out-breath longer than the in-breath" or "knee pain, changed posture twice".

The "returned about how many times" number need not be exact — it is a rough estimate, and the very estimating forces you to look back over the sitting once, not to produce statistics. The "memorable" line is where value accumulates: after a month, re-reading these lines shows what per-sitting feelings could never show.

2.3. Separating daily journal and reflective journal

Do not merge two different tasks into one notebook. Daily: twenty seconds as above. Each week or month: one five-minute sit-back, re-reading the lines and answering two or three open questions (in section 5). Daily entries record events; reflection draws patterns. Long daily entries both cost time and turn recording into autobiographical prose instead of data.

3. Reliable signs of progress

The general standard: genuine progress appears first in daily life, before appearing in the sitting hour. The most reliable signs:

  • Less impulsive reactivity: anger arriving a beat later, harsh words leaving softer, or a pause before pressing Enter. This is the "golden" sign — mindfulness doing its work in the hardest place.
  • Recognizing distraction earlier: the span of being swept into thought — in sittings and in daily life (scrolling the phone, zoning out mid-conversation) — gradually shortens. The "recognize — release — return" loop runs smoother.
  • Faster recovery after strong emotions: a shock, bad news, an argument — the time for the mind to return to normal shortens. Not that waves cease, but the boat rights itself faster.
  • Earlier bodily awareness: knowing the shoulders already lifted, the jaw already clenched, the belly already tightened before those tensions become headache or insomnia — thanks to the release skill (article M.B1) and clear knowing (article M.B6) running in the background all day.
  • Self-sustaining regularity: the number of practice days in the month rising without strain — the habit standing firm.

Note: all these signs are changes against your own past, measured through the journal and through daily life — not the feeling in any single sitting, and still less comparison with others.

4. Easily misread signs

  • Pleasant, buoyant states: real, but coming and going like all phenomena; often only a sign the body has rested enough. That is "weather", not the "geology" of practice.
  • Visions, lights, strange sensations: the canon places them among phenomena possibly met along the way, not a measure of level (as discussed in article M.A2 Understanding How to Practice). Treat them like passing wind: know, then let pass.
  • Grading each sitting: "today good / yesterday bad" is the biggest noise, because two adjacent sittings may differ through lack of sleep, heavy meals, bad news — factors outside practice. Only a chain of many weeks is trustworthy enough.
  • Sitting longer = being better: wrong — the sustainable measure is regularity and quality of knowing, not minutes.
  • Comparing with others: each person has a different body, history, and circumstances; comparison breeds only conceit or inferiority — both block the way. The discourses teach: "do not look at others' faults and what they have or have not done; look at your own faults and what you have or have not done" — Dhammapada, verse 50 - Theravāda Buddhism.

One more serious warning: if in practice there appear very pleasant states accompanied by the wish for others to notice, the wish to declare that you have "attained" something — stop and treat this as a signal for self-checking, not an achievement. In insight systems, this group of phenomena is called the corruptions of insight (Pali: vipassanupakkilesa) and will be presented separately in the advanced section (group C2); for now, the safest handling is returning to the familiar subject, lowering expectations, and discussing with a guide if you have one (article M.A7 Good friends and teachers). If practice brings prolonged increased anxiety or insomnia — return to article M.A3 Safety in Meditation.

5. Reviewing by week and by month

Each week (or each month), spend five minutes re-reading the journal and answering a few open questions:

  • Rhythm: how many days with practice? Steadier than last month? (Remember: regular beats long — article M.A6)
  • Direction: which way are the reliable signs of section 3 moving — less reactive? Earlier recognition? Faster recovery? "Slightly more" is enough; no need for "far more".
  • Repeating obstacles: any difficulty appearing three times or more (drowsiness in afternoon sittings, the phone swallowing evening sittings...)? — a repeating difficulty is a redesignable problem (change hour, place, subject), unlike a random one.
  • A single adjustment for the coming phase: only one, the smallest possible — two more minutes, a changed sitting hour, one more mindfulness marker. Many simultaneous adjustments leave no knowing which produced the result.

After a few months the journal becomes a precious mirror — exactly as the retreat article reminded: notes from an intensive practice period are "a mirror for later self-assessment". And recall the spirit of this whole article: a mirror for clear seeing, not for sentencing. One who sees tangled hair in the mirror combs it; one does not blame the mirror.

6. Summary and next steps

  • A twenty-second journal right after each sitting: time – duration – subject, a state line, one memorable sentence; keep weekly / monthly reflection separate.
  • Reliable signs appear in daily life: less impulsive, earlier recognition of distraction, faster recovery, earlier bodily awareness, more regularity — all measured against yourself.
  • Misleading signs: pleasant feelings, strange visions, grading each sitting, sitting longer, comparing with others; pleasant states kèm the wish to display attainment are a signal for self-checking, not achievement.
  • Monthly reflection: view rhythm, view direction, find repeating obstacles, choose a single small adjustment.

Glossary:

  • Reflection: sitting back to view the chain of practice past in order to draw patterns, unlike grading each sitting; done weekly or monthly.
  • Corruptions of insight (Pali: vipassanupakkilesa): a group of pleasant, luminous states (light, joy, bliss...) easily mistaken for attainment on the insight path; presented separately in the app's advanced section.
  • Upakkilesa Sutta (Pali: Upakkilesa Sutta): the discourse teaching periodic self-questioning about defilements in the mind — this discourse title means "defilements".
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
