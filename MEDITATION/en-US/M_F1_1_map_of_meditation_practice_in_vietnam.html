M.F1.1. A Map of Meditation Practice in Vietnam

This opening article has two joined pieces: a map of currently present practice lineages and the three historical Zen lineages often mentioned in records — with one small yet important skill: how to read Zen records with critical awareness.

1. Why a separate map for Vietnam?

Vietnamese approach meditation not mainly through Pali or Chinese books, but through village temples, weekend meditation classes, retreats, apps, and surrounding practice communities.

Meditation in Vietnam is also not a "copy" of any single tradition: it results from over fifteen centuries of reception, transformation, and growth on Vietnamese soil — from early Zen lineages, through Trần-era Trúc Lâm Zen, to today's present lineages.

Before entering the map, we need always remembering the right spirit with three principles:

  • Neutrality: lineages described by distinct characters, unranked "high – low" or "right – wrong".
  • One shared basis: though forms differ, all lineages come down to the same ethics – concentration – wisdom basis and the two tranquillity – insight directions already learned.
  • Practitioners self-calibrating: the map helps recognizing which lineage one meets, not for "choosing sides" from the very start (the one-teacher – one-lineage – one-time principle).

2. Five currently present lineages — quick recognition points

Viewing the present, five main lineages can be drawn present in Vietnamese Buddhist and spiritual life:

  • Zen (Chinese: Chán, Japanese: Zen): including Trúc Lâm Zen — the Vietnamese-rooted lineage strongly revived in recent decades — and Chinese-rooted Zen lineages like the Línjì (Chinese: Línjì, Japanese: Rinzai) lineage transmitted to the South from the 17th century. Recognition points: seated meditation, walking meditation (Pali: caṅkama), practice questions joined to daily life.
  • Pure Land and buddha-recitation (Chinese: Jìngtǔ): the widest in headcount form — from at-home name-holding to temple chanting retreats, from literacy schools to hộ niệm (chanting support for those about to pass or just passed). Meanwhile, in many Vietnamese temples, Zen and Pure Land go in harness in the same activities — exactly the "separated hours" form of combined Zen–Pure Land practice.
  • Plum Village applied mindfulness: the meditation presentation of the Plum Village community founded by Master Thích Nhất Hạnh — with distinctive forms like verses, mindfulness bells, slow walking meditation, practice in daily activities and relations. This is the most prominent bridge between Buddhism and secular mindfulness.
  • Theravāda Buddhism: present through Theravāda temples and regularly organized insight meditation retreats (Pali: vipassanā).
  • Secular meditation: classes, health programmes, apps — presenting techniques unjoined to schools, aiming at stress reduction and life quality. This very article collection at "overview" level also combines both: a Buddhist conceptual basis, an easily approachable presentation for beginners.

A practical observation: boundaries between lineages in daily life are hazier than on drawings. Very many Vietnamese Buddhists practise in parallel — evening chanting-recitation, morning sitting or walking meditation, attending one lineage's retreats or another's. That is not "method confusion", but exactly Vietnamese Buddhist culture's character: lineages co-dwelling rather than excluding one another.

3. Three historical Zen lineages

All today's lineages have prehistories. In Vietnamese Zen literature, people often mention three early Zen lineages, before Trúc Lâm Zen formed in the late 13th century:

  • The Vinitaruci lineage: beginning around 580, when the Indian monk Vinitaruci (Sanskrit: Vinitaruci) arrived at Pháp Vân Temple (today's northern region) and transmitted the teaching to the first Vietnamese disciple. Regarded as Vietnam's earliest Zen lineage, lasting through dozens of teacher-student generations.
  • The Vô Ngôn Thông lineage: beginning in the early 9th century, following the Chinese monk styled Vô Ngôn Thông (Chinese: Wúyántōng — literally "communicating without words", alluding to a wordless transmission way). This lineage flourished in northern rural regions.
  • The Thảo Đường lineage: beginning in the 11th century, following the meditation master styled Thảo Đường (Chinese: Cǎotáng). Records show this lineage's practice containing Tantric elements (Sanskrit: tantra — special mantra and rite methods), a rarely met mark in the previous two lineages.

The three lineages share one noteworthy point: all beginning from "outsiders arriving" (India, China), then quickly Vietnamized through indigenous disciple generations — later, lineages gradually merged into one another, mostly converging into Trúc Lâm Zen when this lineage formed in the late 13th century. The three early lineages are thus read as Vietnamese Zen's prehistory, rather than still-existing practice options today. 4. Reading Zen records — notes on the Thiền Uyển Tập Anh

Nearly all details on the above three lineages rely on the Thiền Uyển Tập Anh (Chinese: Chányuán jíyīng — meaning "a collection of outstanding figures in the Zen garden"), a biographical collection of Vietnamese Zen masters compiled around 1337, under the Trần dynasty. This is the most important source — yet needs reading with awareness of three points:

  • Time distance: this collection was born nearly eight centuries after the Vinitaruci lineage's opening events (580). Such late records certainly depend on intermediate materials many no longer extant; some details are hard to verify.
  • Purpose and genre: this is school-biographical literature (hagiography): recording figures and transmission stories to affirm lineages' legitimacy — for that purpose, selecting figures and highlighting miraculous stories is the genre's normal mark — needing reading as school literature, not historical chronicles.
  • Cross-sourcing needed: modern research compares the Thiền Uyển Tập Anh with Lý – Trần era stele inscriptions (carved earlier, nearer events) and other sources; some datings and inter-lineage relations have been revisited.

The practical conclusion for practitioners: the three historical lineages are a family map — showing where Zen arrived in Vietnam from and through which roads — not a practice handbook. 5. Summary and next steps

  • Meditation in Vietnam results from over fifteen centuries of reception and transformation; five lineages present today: Zen (Trúc Lâm and Chinese-rooted lineages), Pure Land – buddha-recitation, Plum Village applied mindfulness, Theravāda, and secular meditation — lineages co-dwelling rather than excluding one another.
  • Three historical Zen lineages — Vinitaruci (6th century), Vô Ngôn Thông (9th century), Thảo Đường (11th century) — all beginning from foreigners then Vietnamized; they are Vietnamese Zen's prehistory, gradually merging into Trúc Lâm Zen.
  • Details on these lineages mainly through the Thiền Uyển Tập Anh (around 1337) — a source needing reading with awareness of time distance, biographical genre, and cross-source comparison.
  • The running principle: neutral description, not praising any lineage as "most right" — for beginners, keeping one lineage and walking long enough still matters more than comparing lineages.

Suggested next reading: among the three historical lineages, the lineage still most vivid today is Trúc Lâm Yên Tử Zen — the Zen lineage established by King Trần Nhân Tông, with the famous spirit "Dwelling in the World, Content with the Way": practising amid ordinary life. Glossary:

  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
  • Zen (Chinese: Chán, Japanese: Zen): East Asian Buddhist meditation traditions, originating in China, stressing directly pointing at the mind-nature.
  • Pure Land (Chinese: Jìngtǔ): the method of faith and vowing rebirth in Amitābha Buddha's realm, joined to buddha-recitation practice.
  • Southern Buddhism: Vietnamese addressing of Theravāda Buddhism, because the Buddhist transmission road went to southern Asia.
  • Walking meditation (Pali: caṅkama): walking meditation — practising mindfulness while walking slowly, on par with sitting meditation.
  • Insight meditation (Pali: vipassanā): observing body, feeling, mind, and dhammas to see clearly impermanence, suffering, and not-self.
  • Secular mindfulness: mindfulness programmes presented independently of religion, aiming at health and daily life.
  • Plum Village: the practice community co-founded by Master Thích Nhất Hạnh in France; named for the Dordogne plum village.
  • Hộ niệm: the custom of assisting chanting and buddha-recitation for those about to pass or just passed — a widespread form of buddha-recitation in Vietnam.
  • Vinitaruci (Sanskrit: Vinitaruci): the 6th-century Indian monk, opener of Vietnam's earliest Zen lineage.
  • Vô Ngôn Thông (Chinese: Wúyántōng — literally "communicating without words"): the early-9th-century Chinese monk, opener of Vietnam's second Zen lineage.
  • Thảo Đường (Chinese: Cǎotáng): the 11th-century meditation master, opener of Vietnam's third Zen lineage, whose practice has Tantric elements.
  • Tantra (Sanskrit: tantra): the practice floor using mantras, rites, and images, widespread in Vajrayāna Buddhism; in East Asia existing as a separate branch.
  • Thiền Uyển Tập Anh (Chinese: Chányuán jíyīng — meaning "a collection of outstanding figures in the Zen garden"): the biographical collection of Vietnamese Zen masters compiled around 1337, this Vietnamese Zen history part's principal source.
  • Hagiography: the genre recording cultivators' biographies, honorific and selective — read differently from historical chronicles.
  • Lineage: teacher-student transmission lines, used to determine a Zen lineage's origins and legitimacy.

Source notes

  • Biographies of three early Zen lineages — Thiền Uyển Tập Anh, compiled around 1337 - Vietnamese Buddhist Zen sources; readers should read with modern research on this material's dating and genre.
  • Lý – Trần era steles — parallel supplementary sources helping compare dharma lineages recorded in the Thiền Uyển Tập Anh; used in modern Vietnamese Buddhist historiographical research.
