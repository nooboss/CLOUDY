M.D1.2. Contemplation of Feeling (Vedanānupassanā)

After the body domain, this article enters the second domain of the four establishments of mindfulness (Pali: satipaṭṭhāna): contemplation of feeling. Feeling is the thinnest yet most pivotal layer of experience — because this is exactly where the seeds of liking / aversion form, earlier than emotions and behaviour. Contents: what feeling is, the three kinds of feeling and the mind's reactions to each, how the discourses distinguish two sources of feeling, why feeling is the pivotal training point, and how to begin a feeling-contemplation sitting.

1. What is feeling — and how does it differ from emotion?

Feeling (Pali: vedanā) is the "tone" colouring all experience: pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral. Each time a sense meets an object — eye seeing colour, ear hearing sound, body sensing warmth, mind registering a thought — a feeling appears along with it, usually too quick and thin to notice. Within the four establishments, placing mindfulness (Pali: sati) on this layer is called contemplation of feeling (Pali: vedanānupassanā). [Source: Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 10 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

Feeling must be distinguished from emotion: emotions (sadness, anger, joy, anxiety...) are complex chains — feeling plus mental states, inner stories, and urges to act. Feeling is the first "seed"; emotion is the grown "tree". Hence knowing feeling early is intervening early: in dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda), feeling stands right after contact (Pali: phassa) and right before craving (Pali: taṇhā) — exactly the "gap" the trained skills exploit.

Feeling's place in the four establishments is also meaningful: feeling lies between body and mind, because it is the "bridge" between the two sides — aching legs are a body phenomenon; the accompanying unpleasantness is feeling; the irritation in the head wanting to get up is mind. These three layers separate distinctly, and this article trains the middle layer.

2. Three kinds of feeling and the mind's reactions

The Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta teaches: "when feeling a pleasant feeling, one clearly knows 'I feel a pleasant feeling'; when feeling an unpleasant feeling, one clearly knows...; when feeling a neutral feeling, one clearly knows...". That clear knowing goes together with the mind's reactions — because each kind of feeling pulls a default leaning:

  • Pleasant feeling (Pali: sukha): the mind automatically leans in — wanting to keep, prolong, have more. Contemplation here is clearly seeing two separate events: pleasant feeling is one thing, the "leaning in" another — normally they stick as one.
  • Unpleasant feeling (Pali: dukkha): the mind tightens, wanting to expel, escape. Contemplation here is separating raw sensation from story ("so bad", "why me") — keeping the body soft instead of bracing.
  • Neutral feeling (Pali: adukkhamasukha vedanā): the kind most easily forgotten, because it stands out least. Without mindfulness, neutral feeling quickly turns into boredom and the mind goes seeking stimulation. With mindfulness, it becomes a gentle ground for the mind to settle.

The shared template: the discourses advise neither rewarding pleasant feeling nor punishing unpleasant feeling — only clearly knowing, neither welcoming nor chasing away. The three kinds of feeling are like three guests; whoever comes is greeted — exactly the "non-judging" attitude from articles M.A1 and B3.

3. "Material" and "unmaterial" feeling

The canon divides feelings by source into two groups: material (literally "with flesh, with body", joined to the life of the five sense pleasures and worldly objects - Pali: sāmisa) and unmaterial (born of renunciation, appearing when the mind settles in collectedness or meditation practice - Pali: nirāmisa). [Source: Cūḷavedalla Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 44 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

The practical meaning: the same "pleasant" differs deeply — a tasty bite or praise is material feeling; the softness when the mind settles mid-sitting is unmaterial feeling. This distinction helps two things: (1) not confusing collectedness bliss with sense-pleasure bliss — both pleasant, but one nourishes thirst, the other clarifies the mind; (2) not turning collectedness bliss into new greed — clinging to a sitting's softness and awaiting it next time, exactly the "bliss" trap among the corruptions of insight.

For unpleasant feeling, the discourses have the two arrows image: ordinary people struck by the first arrow — the body's unpleasant feeling — shoot themselves with a second: sorrow, grief, lamentation in the mind. The wise are struck by one arrow only: pain is one matter; mental suffering about pain is a second matter — and the second is the part we add. [Source: Sallatha Sutta, Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 36.6 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

4. Why feeling is the pivotal training point

  • Feeling is always present. Unlike rarely met objects, feeling runs continuously at all times — even outside sittings. This is a perfect "mobile" practice ground, joining the mindfulness markers of article B6.
  • Feeling is the earliest gap. Blocking reactions at the feeling layer — before becoming craving, before becoming behaviour — is far easier than blocking once flared into anger or craving.
  • Feeling is the doorway to the three characteristics. Feeling arises – passes extremely fast; seeing feeling arising then passing right where it arises is seeing impermanence most distinctly; and feeling comes and goes conditionally, unpermitted by any "self" — directly penetrating the spirit of not-self (Pali: anattā).
  • Warning: observing ≠ suppressing. Unpleasant feeling flooding to overload, use article M.A3's safety tools — lowering intensity, grounding, opening the eyes — then continue; "forced observing" only turns sittings into battles.

5. How to begin a feeling-contemplation sitting

  • Step 1 — foundation: a few minutes holding the breath as subject for the mind to settle enough.
  • Step 2 — widening the view: shifting attention to the feeling accompanying the breath: is this in–out breath pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral?
  • Step 3 — labelling: using article M.B4's noting skill: silently labelling briefly "pleasant", "unpleasant", "neutral" — then noticing the mind's reaction: is it leaning in or leaning away?
  • Step 4 — with other arising feelings: aching legs, itches, softness, buoyancy — clearly knowing each, neither welcoming nor chasing; adjusting as usual when needed (a stretch of walking meditation — article M.B6) then returning.
  • Outside sittings: choosing one marker in the day (drinking water, hearing news, blocked in traffic) and asking two questions: "what feeling just arose?" and "how is the mind leaning?" — nothing further needed; clearly seeing is already the exercise.

6. Summary and next steps

  • Feeling is the pleasant – unpleasant – neutral "tone" colouring all experience; unlike emotion in that it is the first seed, not yet the tree.
  • Contemplation of feeling is clearly knowing each kind of feeling with the mind's leaning reaction — seeing two separate events instead of one stuck block.
  • The discourses divide feeling by source: material (five sense pleasures) and unmaterial (renunciation) — so as not to confuse collectedness bliss with sense-pleasure bliss, and not to cling to collectedness bliss as new greed.
  • Two arrows: pain is one matter; mental suffering about pain is a second — contemplation of feeling drops the second arrow.

Suggested next reading: with the "tone" layer of experience clearly known, the next step is a deeper layer — contemplation of mind (Cittānupassanā): clearly knowing the mind's very state (greed, hatred, scattering, contraction...), no longer stopping at sensation but going straight into "the knowing". See you there.

Glossary:

  • Feeling (Pali: vedanā): the pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral "tone" accompanying each contact.
  • Three kinds of feeling: pleasant (sukha), unpleasant (dukkha), neutral neither-painful-nor-pleasant (adukkhamasukha) — the canon's basic classification.
  • Material feeling (Pali: sāmisa): feeling born of the five sense-pleasure objects, worldly life.
  • Unmaterial feeling (Pali: nirāmisa): feeling born of renunciation, appearing when the mind settles in collectedness and meditation practice.
  • Two arrows (SN 36.6): the discourse image distinguishing the body's unpleasant feeling (first arrow) from self-added sorrow (second arrow).
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
