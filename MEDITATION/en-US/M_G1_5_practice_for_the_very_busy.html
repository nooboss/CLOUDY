M.G1.5. Practice for the Very Busy

The previous article gathered the short toolkit; this article is for even harder circumstances: no separate time intervals — days running mornings to evenings, packed schedules, housework piled on outside work. Instead of trying to "find time" for meditation, this article reverses direction: attaching practice to gaps and mandatory activities — things you must do daily however busy. The article directly uses the toolkit and daily-life observation skills of the 2 previous articles — adding nothing new.

1. Very busy persons — the genuine problem and changing questions

First, being honest with the problem: for two-shift workers, small-child raisers, and relatives' caregivers, the advice "spending thirty minutes each morning" is not advice — one more item in undone-task lists, each sighting only adding self-blame. Durably practising persons' experience in busy circumstances points to one different change: changing questions from "which time for meditation?" to "which gaps already available?".

What is surprising: gaps are more numerous than imagined. Very busy persons' days contain dozens of short intervals from seconds to minutes — awaiting boiling water, awaiting elevators, using toilets, washing hands, moving between two points, intervals between two calls — totalling on average dozens of minutes daily. They are only unusable because unseen as time. This article is essentially a re-viewing: most "no-longer" time of busy persons is really unused time.

And one consolation for busy persons: short practices repeated many times daily have genuine value — not "temporary versions" awaiting free days. Eight thirty-second times evenly spread daily nourish mindfulness (Pali: sati) in its own way: training exactly the hardest skill — returning to the present amid activities, needing no private rooms and sitting cushions. This is exactly what article M.B6 said about meditation in daily life, brought to maximum utilization.

2. Attaching practice to mandatory activities

This article's central principle: choosing several daily "must-do" activities, turning them into one's own "bells" — when those activities occur, practising one beat. These activities have advantages no self-scheduled time has: certainly occurring daily, no schedule-remembering needed, and no one preventable. How to choose and do:

  • Choosing two to three fixed "anchors": familiar suggestions — waking up (before leaving beds, three breaths), brushing teeth (brush sensations, only that business), awaiting boiling water (standing still, knowing standing), washing hands, awaiting computers booting, lying down before sleep. Choosing few — two or three — and choosing certain ones present in one's own days; more than three is a new burden.
  • Tying one short article to each anchor: each anchor going with exactly one concrete short article (from article M.G1.4): waking up → three complete breaths; awaiting boiling water → hearing sounds one minute; washing hands → water sensations on hands; walking in areas → knowing each step. The "one anchor – one article" rule helps no further deciding — reaching anchors automatically doing.
  • Using verses if wanted: this attaching way already exists traditionally: one short verse going with activities — washing faces, eating rice, going to sleep (article M.F1.4 will introduce Plum Village verses). Those fitting literary words may self-write a few sentences in their own rhythms — self-written verses usable as well as ready verses.
  • Not demanding completeness: brushing teeth knowing the first twenty seconds suffices; no need to "keep mindfulness throughout activities". Remembering whichever parts, whichever parts — not turning anchors into exams.

3. Using the day's gaps

Besides fixed anchors, "preselecting" responses for indefinite gaps — simple if–then rules:

  • Seconds-long gaps (awaiting pots, awaiting messages, elevators): one known breath — knowing standing, waiting; nothing further needed.
  • Minutes-long gaps (moving, awaiting postponed meetings): hearing sounds or knowing each step — subjects needing neither eyes nor seats.
  • Unexpected long "dead" gaps (cancelled meetings, long traffic jams): regarding as reluctant meditation sessions: counting ten breaths, or quickly scanning bodies from shoulders to feet. Every day has at least one such gap — familiar users will catch it.

In busy days, emotional situations also continuously arise — arguments, frustration, fatigue — exactly when article M.G1.4's "regaining pauses" set (one breath before answering, stopping – breathing – asking) promotes; and stress signals need reading per article G1.2. For very busy persons, these articles matter no less than "positive training" parts: much busy means much pressure, and timely pauses are what keep busy days from becoming broken days.

4. Keeping threads — the most important thing

For very busy persons, the greatest danger is not "little practice" — but faltering then abandoning entirely. Three thread-keeping ways already learned in article M.A6, applied distinctly to these circumstances:

  • Predetermining "unfailable" minimum levels: one morning anchor (three breaths when waking) is the floor level — keepable daily, including worst days. Floor levels keep threads; other articles are plus parts when days are easier.
  • Counting "whether or not", not "long or short": for busy persons, the only worthy measure is the yes / no question: "today did I knowingly return at least once?". Journals need only one mark daily — exactly the two-line journal spirit, further abridged for busy persons.
  • Accepting phases: weeks with maximally packed schedules, all anchors broken — normal business. The "never miss two consecutive days" rule suffices: today dropping, tomorrow restarting from floor levels. Busy persons need not be perfect — needing un-severed.

And a balancing reminder: this article helps meditation living in packed schedules, but if packed schedules continuously alarm bodies (prolonged insomnia, breathlessness, whole-body rigidity — signals already discussed in article M.G1.2), then the problem needing solving is not "adding meditation techniques" but reducing schedule loads. Practice supports busy persons — not for busy persons bearing boundless busyness.

5. Summary and next steps

  • Changing questions: from "which time for meditation?" to "which gaps already available?" — busy days contain many short intervals totalling considerably.
  • The central principle: attaching practice to mandatory activities — two or three fixed daily "anchors", each anchor one concrete short article; verses usable.
  • Indefinite gaps used through if–then rules: seconds → one breath; minutes → hearing sounds, knowing each step; unexpected long gaps → reluctant meditation sessions.
  • Keeping threads through unfailable floor levels, yes / no measures, and the "never miss two consecutive days" rule; busy schedules alarming bodies need load reduction, not more techniques.

Suggested next reading: "busy" circumstances usually go with "family" circumstances — and many think small children make practice even more impossible. The next article will reverse that: Practicing with Family and Children — turning household members into practice parts, instead of its barriers.

Glossary:

  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
  • Anchors: fixed points used to hold attention returning — in this article daily mandatory activities attached to one short practice article.
  • Verses (Sanskrit: gāthā): short verses going with activities to remind mindfulness — already presented in article F1.4.
  • If–then rules: predetermining responses before situations ("if awaiting boiling water then hearing sounds") — anchoring habits into circumstances instead of willpower.
  • Mindfulness (Pali: sati): remembering and staying with what is happening right now — the basis capacity of all articles in the collection.
  • Minimum baseline: minimum practice levels keepable in hardest days — used to keep threads, not to limit ceilings.
