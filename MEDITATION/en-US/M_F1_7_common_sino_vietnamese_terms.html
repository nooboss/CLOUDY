1. How to use this reference article?

The use is simple: meeting one strange Sino-Vietnamese word when reading → looking up in below sections → reading short meanings → if needing details, following "(article ...)" leads to read corresponding articles. Because this is a reference (R level in the collection's grading), all items are written briefly and selectively — not aiming to reteach whole concepts.

Why Sino-Vietnamese occupies a large place in Vietnamese meditation materials: most terms entered Vietnamese through scriptural baskets translated into Chinese characters — three Pali – Sanskrit – Sino-Vietnamese language floors. For example: establishments in "four establishments" is the Chinese translation of Pali satipaṭṭhāna. 2. Basis word pairs — tranquillity-contemplation, collectedness-wisdom, three trainings

These three word pairs are most meditation materials' "skeleton" — repeatedly met in all traditions:

  • Tranquillity – contemplation: the pair of two core practice directions. Tranquillity is gathering the mind onto one subject for the mind to settle (tranquillity meditation — Pali: samatha); contemplation is observing to see clearly experience's nature (contemplation meditation, or insight meditation — Pali: vipassanā). The two sides supplement each other, not choosing one abandoning one (article M.A1, group C).
  • Collectedness – wisdom: two corresponding results of tranquillity and contemplation. Collectedness is the mind's collectedness and stability (Pali: samādhi); wisdom is rightly knowing as it is (Pali: paññā). East Asian materials often say "collectedness and wisdom jointly cultivated" — meaning two sides need parallel (article M.A1).
  • Three trainings: three learnings of the road — ethics – concentration – wisdom. Meditation lies in the concentration part, standing on the ethics basis and heading toward wisdom.
  • Jhāna: the Sino-Vietnamese transcription of jhāna (Pali) — deep meditation levels when minds have gathered; materials often say "four jhāna levels".

Quick-reading tips: front-standing words (tranquillity, collectedness) usually join settling and gathering; back-standing words (contemplation, wisdom) usually join viewing and seeing. Grasping this rule, many difficult book sentences suddenly become readable.

3. Practice frames — four establishments, four divine abodes, five hindrances, seven awakening factors

These are frame names, mentioned like "place names" in talks and books:

  • Four establishments: four places establishing mindfulness — body, feeling, mind, dhammas; Buddhism's basis observation frame (group M.D1).
  • Four divine abodes: four boundlessly limited mental states — loving-kindness, compassion, joy, equanimity; emotion-training methods (article group M.D2).
  • Five hindrances: five things covering minds: sense-desire greed, hatred, dullness, restlessness – remorse, doubt; "hindrance" means covering cloths — covering so minds unclearly see. Recognizing five hindrances is the central skill of the distraction-handling phase (article M.B5).
  • Seven awakening factors: seven awakening limbs — mindfulness, investigation, effort, joy, tranquillity, concentration, equanimity; seven qualities when enough bringing minds to awakening. In materials, seven awakening factors are often presented as the counterweight of five hindrances: which countering which (article M.B5).
  • Five faculties: five mind-capacity roots — faith, effort, mindfulness, concentration, wisdom; trained balanced to skew neither toward effort nor settling (article M.B5).

These "four – five – seven" all share one number-based naming way — when reading meeting "four X, five Y, eight Z", guessing that is a frame list: nearly always right.

4. Practice forms and mental states

The vocabulary group met in describing practice sessions and guiding teachers' instructions:

  • Seated meditation: sitting meditation — generally practice in sitting postures (article M.A4).
  • Walking meditation: walking meditation — slow walking with mindfulness, on par with seated meditation (article M.A4).
  • Daily practices: Buddhists' daily chanting-recitation – practice sessions, usually mornings and evenings; "doing daily practices" means keeping that daily rhythm (article M.F1.5).
  • Buddha-recitation – name-holding: recitation here means continuously remembering; name-holding is holding Buddha names. With the same "recitation" word, Theravāda uses it for Buddha virtue-recollection (recollections), East Asia uses it for name recitation — three levels already distinguished in article M.F1.5 (articles M.D3.7, M.E2.3).
  • Huàtóu – investigating huàtóu – doubt-sensation: huàtóu are short practice questions ("who is reciting the Buddha?"); investigating here means following, continuously examining — not greed; doubt-sensation is living wondering moods, naturally rising when following questions. This trio belongs to advanced Zen, needing guiding teachers (article M.E2.5).
  • Kōans: dialogue stories or hard-to-reason questions, used as subjects making thinking "run out of roads" — the same family as huàtóu (article M.E2.5).
  • Silent illumination – just sitting: two names of the "only sitting" road — settling and illuminating in sitting; silent illumination the Chinese source, just sitting the Japanese (shikantaza) name of the succeeding lineage (article M.E2.5).
  • Wandering thoughts: automatically arising drifting thoughts in mind; the Trúc Lâm approach summarizes in four words "knowing delusion, not following it" (article M.F1.3).
  • Restless minds: images pointing to scattered minds — minds like monkeys jumping restlessly uncalm, intentions like horses galloping unholdable; these words describe exactly what early practice phases most meet (article M.B1).
  • Dullness – restlessness: two skewed states needing balancing while sitting: dullness is exhausted, hazy, drowsy; restlessness is fidgety, wanting to move; with "remorse" is tormenting over committed faults (articles M.B5, M.A3).
  • Abiding: letting minds stably rest on one object — "abiding minds" is how to place minds onto subjects (group C).
  • Awakening: directly realizing what was previously unseen — in materials, this word needs careful reading, not synonymous with strange feelings or visions while sitting (article M.C2.5).

5. Three notes when looking up words — and summary

Before closing the article, three notes helping word look-ups avoid misunderstandings:

  • One word, many traditions: the same "recitation" word — Theravāda speaking recollections, East Asia speaking buddha-recitation; the same "contemplation" word — Theravāda speaking body contemplation (observing the body), Zen speaking silent illumination (whole illuminating). When looking up, always noting which traditional context the word belongs to.
  • Do not mix two vocabulary systems in one practice: East Asian Sino-Vietnamese words and Theravāda Pali words sometimes translate the same root, but practice systems around each side differ — the already-sealed principle in article E2.3.
  • Sino-Vietnamese words are translations: Vietnamese words are Chinese translations, while Chinese words are translations from India — two translation layers mean easily depleted meanings. If one Sino-Vietnamese word puzzles you, additionally looking up Pali / Sanskrit forms in the app's comparison table.

Glossary:

  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
  • Hindrances (Pali: nīvaraṇa): the Pali name of "hindrances" — things covering minds; "wrapping" meaning winding around, "hindrance" meaning covering.
  • Tranquillity (Pali: passaddhi): the gentle settledness of body and mind — one of seven awakening limbs.
  • Investigation (Pali: dhammavicaya): the capacity to examine mind – body phenomena — one of seven awakening limbs.
  • Huàtóu (Chinese: huàtóu): short practice questions in Zen, used as followed subjects (article M.E2.5).
  • Kōans (Chinese: gōng'àn, Japanese: kōan): dialogue stories or questions cutting across ordinary thinking ways (article M.E2.5).
  • Jhāna (Pali: jhāna, Sanskrit: dhyāna): deep meditation levels when minds have gathered (articles M.A1, M.C1.4).
  • Zen: East Asian Buddhist meditation tradition groups, originating in China (Chinese: Chán) — in detail in article E2.2.
  • Awakening (Chinese: wù): directly realizing truths on the path — not synonymous with strange sitting experiences (article M.C2.5).
