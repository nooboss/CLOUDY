M.E1.4. The Visuddhimagga and the Commentarial Tradition

All modern Theravāda meditations stand on one shared textual basis — and most technical maps previous articles used (the forty-subject system, three concentration levels, progress of insight, sign stages) lie not in the canonical basket but in the commentarial layer. This article maps that textual layer: the Visuddhimagga — the most complete compendium on Southern meditation, its author, structure, and distinction from the Vimuttimagga; simultaneously sealing the important reading skill of the whole E1 group: knowing which layer one reads — discourses or commentaries.

1. Why know the textual layer?

In previous articles, source citations always marked the layer: "Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta MN 10" is the canonical layer; "Visuddhimagga chapter IX" is the commentarial layer. This distinction is not formal — it decides a statement's weight:

  • Canonical layer (Pali: sutta) — teachings recorded near the Buddha's time, in slogan – image – practice-sequence forms; when canon and commentaries differ, Theravāda lineages view the canon as the root standard.
  • Commentarial (Pali: aṭṭhakathā) and treatise layers — systematizing, explaining, turning sequences into detailed maps; great value yet essentially later-era explanations — different explainers may map differently.
  • Modern practice layer — books and courses of today's lineages; using both layers above but selectively stressing (article M.E1.3 already saw).

Layer confusion causes two common errors: either attributing to the discourses what they do not say (for example: the 40-subject system, three concentration levels — the discourses never list them as systems; those are commentarial works); or regarding commentaries as useless because "not the Buddha's words" — discarding centuries of practice-tested systematization. The right way: the canon is the root standard, commentaries the map — both needed.

2. Visuddhimagga — the complete compendium on Southern meditation

The Visuddhimagga (Pali: visuddhimagga — "the path to purification") was compiled by the Bhikkhu Buddhaghosa in Sri Lanka around the 5th century, at the island chronicle's recorded request. The chronicle legend recounts: Buddhaghosa came to Sri Lanka to translate and compile commentaries from Sinhala, requested to prove capacity through a treatise — and that treatise is the Path of Purification. [Source: Mahāvaṃsa, ch. XXXVII - Theravāda Buddhism.]

The treatise's place: it synthesizes the Theravāda commentarial teaching into one road — from ethics, through collectedness (all subjects, all levels), to wisdom (detailed knowledges). Nearly all technical maps previous articles used come from here: the forty-subject system, three concentration levels, three sign stages, sixteen knowledges, recollection sequences. Hence the treatise is called the "Southern tradition's meditation encyclopedia" — not a discourse for direct practice, but a synthesizing handbook.

3. The seven-purification structure — the treatise's backbone

The treatise organizes through the seven purifications (Pali: satta visuddhi) — grounded in the discourses (SN 12.23) but made the whole road's organizing frame:

  • I–II. Purifications of ethics and mind: ethical conduct (chapter I) and collectedness — the "collectedness" part occupying nearly half the treatise, presenting all 40-system subjects and collectedness levels (chapters III–XIII);
  • III–VII. Purifications from view through knowledge-and-vision: the wisdom part — knowledges in sequence (chapters XIV–XXII) — the frame already summarized in article C2.5.

Hence, when reading modern meditations saying "following the Visuddhimagga", know: that is a road organized in a commentarial frame — some lineages walk it fully (Pa-Auk — article M.E1.3), some take parts (insight lineages taking the wisdom part); taking which part is the lineage's choice, not the treatise mandating a sole way.

4. Vimuttimagga — The Path of Freedom

Before the Visuddhimagga already existed a treatise of the same kind: the Vimuttimagga (Pali: vimuttimagga — "the path to liberation") — attributed to Bhikkhu Upatissa, around the 1st–2nd centuries, transmitted mainly through Chinese translation. The two share purpose, differing in some presentations — for example subject classification and collectedness description; the Southern tradition chose the Visuddhimagga as standard, and the Vimuttimagga keeps scholarly-comparative value: seeing the same contents organized in many ways, and seeing Buddhaghosa's choices in his era's context. [Source: Vimuttimagga - Theravāda Buddhism; the two-treatise comparison — editorial note of the app.]

The reader's takeaway: commentaries also have "versions" — one era's map is not the sole possible map; hence when meeting two commentarial books differing, neither is necessarily wrong — two presentation roads possible.

5. How to read discourses and commentaries — and summary

  • The first question when reading all materials: "which layer is this?" — discourses (speaking near original contents), commentaries – treatises (system maps), modern practice (one lineage's use);
  • Reading discourses: noting practice sequences, images, repetitions — discourses were transmitted for practising and memorizing, not for analysing like treatises;
  • Reading commentaries: noting maps – classifications – collectedness levels — using as reference maps, not for self-grading progress (article M.C2.5 already warned);
  • Reading modern lineage books: recognizing which lineage speaks (article M.E1.3) — avoiding mixing many lineages' techniques at once.

Summary:

  • Textual layering — discourses / commentaries / modern practice — decides each statement's weight; the canon is the root standard, commentaries the map.
  • Visuddhimagga (Buddhaghosa, 5th century, Sri Lanka) is the Southern meditation encyclopedia: all previous articles' technical maps come from here; organized through seven purifications — ethics → mind → wisdom levels.
  • Vimuttimagga (Upatissa, earlier) — the earlier same-kind treatise, transmitted through Chinese translation; showing commentaries also have versions — the map is not sole.
  • The three-layer reading skill + lineage recognition is self-learners' protection tool: knowing what "speaks nearest the discourses", what is a later-era map, and which is one particular lineage's choice.

Glossary:

  • Visuddhimagga (Pali: visuddhimagga): the treatise synthesizing the whole path per Theravāda commentaries — Buddhaghosa, 5th century.
  • Buddhaghosa (Pali: buddhaghosa): the bhikkhu compiling the treatise.
  • Vimuttimagga (Pali: vimuttimagga): the earlier same-kind treatise by Upatissa — transmitted through Chinese translation.
  • Seven purifications (Pali: satta visuddhi): the Visuddhimagga's path-organizing frame — grounded in SN 12.23 (article M.C2.5).
  • Treatises (Pali: pakaraṇa): systematizing textual forms — distinguished from commentaries (explaining each discourse) and discourses.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
