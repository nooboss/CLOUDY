M.D1.4. Contemplation of Dhammas (Dhammānupassanā)

This article enters the fourth domain — closing the four establishments of mindfulness (Pali: satipaṭṭhāna): contemplation of dhammas. This is the most misread domain, precisely because of its name: "dhamma" here does not mean "doctrine" but classified phenomenon groups — ready-made cabinets for sorting everything happening in experience. Contents: what "dhamma" means, the overall picture of observation groups, then each frame in turn: five aggregates, six internal–external bases, seven awakening factors, and four noble truths.

1. What does "dhamma" mean in dhamma contemplation?

Contemplation of dhammas (Pali: dhammānupassanā) is placing mindfulness (Pali: sati) on experience arranged in the teaching's classified frames. The word dhamma (Pali: dhamma, Sanskrit: dharma) is very broad; but in this section of the Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta, it means not "the Buddha's doctrine" but "dhamma groups" — each phenomenon group: five aggregates, six internal–external bases, five hindrances, seven awakening factors, four noble truths. [Source: Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 10 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

An easy image: body – feeling – mind are three coarse layers of experience; dhamma contemplation is the layer seen through an analytical lens. For example in the same sitting: seeing aching legs = body contemplation; clearly knowing unpleasant sensation = feeling contemplation; knowing the tense mind = mind contemplation; but when clearly seeing aching body → unpleasant feeling → tense mind → the cause is clinging — the causal chain appearing, that is dhamma contemplation. In other words: dhamma contemplation is the four establishments seen from the doctrinal floor.

Hence this frame naturally stands last — not because less important, but because only when the three coarse layers are distinct does the analytical layer grip. Beginners jumping straight into "dhamma contemplation" without distinctly knowing body – feeling – mind usually turn to speculation instead of observation.

2. The observation groups the discourses list

The Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta (MN 10, DN 22) lists five groups into dhamma contemplation. This article introduces each group in the canonical order; sections below deepen each frame.

  • Five hindrances — five obstacles: the discourses place them here because hindrances are what beginners meet first — present, clearly know present; absent, clearly know absent.
  • Five aggregates — five groups constituting experience (D1.4.1): viewing whole experience through five groups instead of the "self" notion.
  • Six internal–external bases — six sense–object pairs (D1.4.2): seeing the sense process and how bondage arises at contact.
  • Seven factors of awakening — seven factors of awakening (D1.4.3): recognizing which factor is lacking and nourishing it.
  • Four noble truths — four truths (D1.4.4): bringing the core teaching down to direct observation.

Notably: the discourses require with each group clearly knowing both "present" and "absent" — hindrances present, clearly know present; absent, clearly know absent; awakening factors present, clearly know; not yet, clearly know. That is, the dhamma-contemplation frame not only hunts faults but also records wholesomeness — the same "both sides" spirit as mind contemplation.

3. The five aggregates — seeing experience instead of "self"

The five aggregates (Pali: pañcakkhandha):

  • Form (Pali: rūpa): the material side — body and the physical world.
  • Feeling (Pali: vedanā): the pleasant – unpleasant – neutral tone.
  • Perception (Pali: saññā): recognizing and marking — "this is a bell sound", "this is a name".
  • Mental formations (Pali: saṅkhāra): fabricating mental activities — volition, reactions, emotions, habit machinery.
  • Consciousness (Pali: viññāṇa): classifying knowing — knowing colours, sounds, thoughts.

How to practise: when an experience appears, try dividing it by five groups instead of packaging in "I see..." — clearly seeing each group arriving then leaving by its own conditions, the notion of a central "self" gradually exposes its provisionality. This is the practical side of the not-self lesson — not a lecture to believe, but a way of arranging experience to see for oneself.

4. The six internal–external bases — seeing bondage at contact

The six internal–external bases (Pali: saḷāyatana, Sanskrit: ṣaḍāyatana): eye–forms, ear–sounds, nose–smells, tongue–tastes, body–touches, mind–mental objects. Along with contact (Pali: phassa) — the moment base meets field, and fetters (Pali: saṃyojana) — bonds; this chain was presented in the dependent-origination frame (article M.C2.4).

How to practise: when a contact occurs (hearing harsh words, seeing stimulating sights), attending right at the contact point — before becoming story and reaction — what is forming? This is where bondage is born and also the only place to release: waiting for reactions to become behaviour before caring is already one beat late.

5. The seven factors of awakening

The seven factors of awakening (Pali: satta bojjhaṅga): mindfulness (Pali: sati), investigation (Pali: dhammavicaya), effort (Pali: viriya), joy (Pali: pīti), tranquillity (Pali: passaddhi), concentration (Pali: samādhi), equanimity (Pali: upekkhā). This is the wholesome side of the mind's map: five hindrances are five barriers, seven awakening factors seven opening doors.

The nourishing principle per the Theravāda commentaries: a sluggish mind, nourish investigation – effort – joy; an agitated mind, nourish tranquillity – concentration – equanimity; while mindfulness stands outside both groups, always useful — the same spirit as the five faculties (article M.B5). [Source: Bojjhaṅga Saṃyutta, Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 46 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

6. The four noble truths — bringing the teaching to the cushion

The four noble truths (Pali: cattāri ariyasaccāni) — suffering (dukkha), origin (samudaya), cessation (nirodha), path (magga) — are often understood as lectures about life. But in the dhamma-contemplation spirit, this is a direct observation frame: in this very sitting —

  • There is discomfort (suffering): aching legs, tense mind, lacking feeling — distinctly present.
  • There is a clinging cause (origin): wanting to expel pain, wanting to prolong softness — the clinging cause is an observable event.
  • There are moments it settles (cessation): when reactions are unfed, tension dissolves by itself — that settling is also an event, not blind hope.
  • There is a way making it settle (path): exactly the eightfold way being practised — and each skill of groups B–C is the path's details.

This is the understanding making the four noble truths less abstract for beginners: not a declaration to believe, but four questions for self-verification in each sitting.

7. Summary and next steps

  • "Dhamma" in dhamma contemplation means classified phenomenon groups, not doctrine in the ordinary sense.
  • The discourses list five observation groups: five hindrances, five aggregates, six internal–external bases, seven awakening factors, four noble truths — each a "lens" for arranging experience.
  • This frame not only hunts faults — clearly knowing both present and absent, both obstacles and wholesome factors.
  • The shared practice: when experience is dense or hazy, opening the analytical lens — seeing the causal chain instead of one "I am..." block.

Suggested next reading: group M.D1 also has "The Four Establishments from Basic to Advanced" — the suggested path traversing the four domains, clarifying: the discourses mandate no single sequence, and many modern systems unfold differently. That is the reasonable transition before group M.D2 — The Divine Abodes (Brahmavihāra): loving-kindness, compassion, appreciative joy, equanimity.

Glossary:

  • Contemplation of dhammas (Pali: dhammānupassanā): the fourth domain of the four establishments — observing experience through the teaching's classified frames.
  • Dhamma (Pali: dhamma, Sanskrit: dharma): very broad meaning — teaching, phenomena, law; here used as "classified phenomenon groups".
  • Five aggregates (Pali: pañcakkhandha): five groups constituting all experience — form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness.
  • Perception (Pali: saññā): the function of recognizing and marking objects — "what is this".
  • Formations (Pali: saṅkhāra): fabricating mental activities — volition, reactions, habits.
  • Consciousness (Pali: viññāṇa): classifying knowing — knowing colours, sounds, thoughts.
  • Six internal–external bases (Pali: saḷāyatana): six sense–object pairs.
  • Fetters (Pali: saṃyojana): bonds tying the mind to the round of rebirth.
  • Seven factors of awakening (Pali: satta bojjhaṅga): seven factors of awakening — mindfulness, investigation, effort, joy, tranquillity, concentration, equanimity.
  • Investigation (Pali: dhammavicaya): the capacity to examine phenomena — "looking closely at what is happening".
  • Tranquillity (Pali: passaddhi): the softness and lightness of body and mind.
  • Equanimity (Pali: upekkhā): balance neither welcoming nor chasing — unlike avoidance or indifference.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
