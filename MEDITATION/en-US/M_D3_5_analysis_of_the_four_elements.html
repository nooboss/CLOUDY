M.D3.5. Analysis of the Four Elements

After two body-viewing ways in the aversion direction — foulness and 32 parts — the 40 system's body-contemplation subjects have an approach needing no frightening images yet still dismantling the "body is self" notion: four-elements analysis. This is a subject many modern teachers encourage because both canonically grounded and gentle — suitable for those not yet ready for strong contemplations. Contents: what the four elements are, canonical origins, how to practise each element, this practice's wisdom purpose, and summary.

1. What are the four elements?

Four-elements analysis (Pali: catudhātuvavatthāna) is observing the body through four "elements" — four basic qualities from which, per the canon's presentation, the material body is composed:

  • Earth element (Pali: pathavī): the hard, soft quality — the body's "solid" part: bones, flesh, skin; sensed when touching, grasping, standing.
  • Water element (Pali: āpo): the cohesive, flowing quality — the part holding other parts together: blood, sweat, saliva; the discourses define it through "liquid" and "cohesive" natures.
  • Fire element (Pali: tejo): the hot, cold quality — the body's heat part: warmth in the chest, heat in fever, coolness at rest.
  • Air element (Pali: vāyo): the moving, pushing quality — the body's transport part: breath, blood circulating, burps, tearing clusters.

The point to understand at once: the four elements here are not four chemical substances — they are four qualities (four ways matter manifests), each findable through direct bodily sensation: hardness of teeth, warmth of palms, stickiness of a swollen mouth, movement of the breathing belly. The Theravāda commentaries stress this definition: each element defined by sensible characteristics, not chemical composition — hence this practice is available to anyone with a body. [Source: Visuddhimagga, ch. XI - Theravāda Buddhism.]

2. Canonical origins

Four-elements analysis has distinct canonical roots. In the four establishments, the body-contemplation section has a passage: "further, one contemplates this body as this body stands: head-hairs, body-hairs, nails, teeth, skin..., and by classification — in this body there are earth, water, fire, air elements". The discourses use the butcher image: butchers dividing cattle into parts placing each place — four-elements contemplators seeing the body divided into qualities, the "cattle" (here: "my body") notion no longer gripping. [Source: Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 10 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

The canon also has the Great Elephant-Footprint Discourse and many other discourses fully presenting the four elements — and in Theravāda, this four-quality division further expands into transformations in the material classification of the Abhidhamma system — the body divided into minimal material parts, each complete with four elements. For practitioners, only know: this is a practice with direct canonical roots, not a later invention.

3. How to practise — each element in turn

The presentation below follows the canonical + commentary spirit, at a level suitable for lay practitioners (commentarial deep-collectedness levels need a teacher — article M.A6):

  • Step 1 — foundation: a few minutes of breath for the mind moderately settled.
  • Step 2 — earth element: attending where the body contacts chairs, floors: hard, soft, pressing sensations — silently labelling "hard" / "soft"; traversing regions: feet, hips, back, hands.
  • Step 3 — water element: attending to cohesion and flowing within the body: wetness in the mouth, skin's cohesion covering the body — silently labelling "cohesive" / "flowing". (The subtlest water element of four — not easily sensed; not straining to find, using however much known.)
  • Step 4 — fire element: attending to heat: warmth in the chest, heat in palms, coolness on wind-exposed skin — silently labelling "hot" / "cold".
  • Step 5 — air element: attending to movement: breath pushing the belly, pulse at the wrist, pushing sensations at the nose — silently labelling "moving" / "pushing".
  • Step 6 — merging: widening attention to the whole body — seeing the body as each region one occurring quality: hard places, warm places, cohesive places, moving places — no longer the "my body" image as one block. Holding several minutes, then back to the breath to close.

A one-element contemplation variant usable in the day: waiting for vehicles — "this place is earth element (hardness of feet on the ground)"; drinking water — "water element passing through"; standing in sun — "fire element"; walking — "air element". This way brings the practice into daily mindfulness markers.

4. Wisdom purpose: dismantling "body is self"

This practice's genuine value lies in observational consequences:

  • The body dismantled into qualities. When the body is seen as occurring qualities, the "body" notion — one single block, fixed, belonging to "self" — loses footing; exactly dismantling the "body is self" notion without any unpleasant images. Directly joining not-self contemplation and the mind – matter frame: four elements are how the matter part divides into qualities.
  • Not-self sensing from directness, not from argument: "this hardness is earth element; this warmth is fire element — so where is 'my body'?" That question needs no thinking answer — seeing only qualities remaining is itself the answer.
  • Balance with the body in illness and aging. Long-term practitioners often say: when ill, seeing "fire element imbalanced" is easier than seeing "I am ill" — the same reality, differently arranged notions, different mental burdens. This is not self-consolation — viewing by the body's genuine structure.

5. Place in the 40 system and for beginners

  • Within the 40 system: four-elements defining is placed by the commentaries in the group leading only to access concentration — the collectedness it creates is usable for insight meditation though not reaching jhāna; this is also how this article suits lay practitioners: its practice goal is wisdom, not deep collectedness.
  • For beginners: this practice is among the gentlest body-contemplation beginner lessons — no frightening images (unlike M.D3.3, M.D3.4), no equipment, direct canonical basis; beginning as section 3 presents right after a firm breath foundation is possible.
  • Natural combining: four elements are many practices' "shared language" — body scanning, 32 parts, earth – water – fire – air kasiṇas can all be viewed through the four-elements lens; learning once, using many places.

6. Summary and next steps

  • Four-elements analysis (catudhātuvavatthāna) observes the body through four qualities: hard / soft (earth), cohesive / flowing (water), hot / cold (fire), moving / pushing (air) — four directly sensible qualities, not four chemical substances.
  • Direct canonical roots in body contemplation (MN 10) with the butcher image; the modern part developed by the Visuddhimagga (ch. XI).
  • Wisdom purpose: dismantling the "body is self" notion — the body dismantled into qualities, not-self appearing from directness; and a balanced view of the body in illness and aging.
  • For beginners: the 40 system's gentlest body-contemplation beginner lesson — no unpleasant images, no equipment, direct canonical basis.

Suggested next reading: the 40 system's body-contemplation group has one independent subject on eating habits: Perception of the Repulsiveness of Food (Āhāre paṭikūlasaññā) — contemplating food to reduce taste-greed, with distinct warnings on eating disorders (article M.D3.6). See you there.

Glossary:

  • Four-elements analysis (Pali: catudhātuvavatthāna): defining the body's four basic qualities of matter.
  • Earth element (Pali: pathavī): the hard, soft quality — the body's "solid" part.
  • Water element (Pali: āpo): the cohesive, flowing quality — the part holding bodily parts together.
  • Fire element (Pali: tejo): the hot, cold quality — the body's heat part.
  • Air element (Pali: vāyo): the moving, pushing quality — the body's transport part.
  • Elements (Pali: dhātu): here meaning basic qualities — distinguished from the modern "chemical element" meaning.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
