M.A5. During and After a Sitting

With the seat and posture prepared (previous article), two tasks remain for a session: what to do when distraction, drowsiness, and restlessness appear mid-sitting, and how to end the sitting properly. Both are practical skills — no deep theory needed, just knowing beforehand "if this happens, what do I do" so that when it really happens, you are not bewildered.

1. While sitting: handling distraction, drowsiness, and restlessness

1.1. The underlying attitude: no self-judgment

What needs saying first is not technique, but attitude. The three familiar visitors for beginners — scattered thought, drowsiness, restlessness — will arrive in every sitting, even for practitioners of many years. The difference between one who trains and one who does not is not that "the visitors stop coming", but how they are received.

The two most common wrong attitudes:

  • Self-blame: "Why am I so bad — ten minutes of sitting and all thinking?" — self-judgment itself is a bigger distraction than the original distraction.
  • Resistance: forcing the mind "not to think", straining to suppress drowsiness — the more you press, the stronger that state grows, and the sitting becomes an exhausting battle.

The right attitude, in brief: recognize — release gently — return — without judging. And remember one point from the previous article: each return to the object is one round of training. A sitting with thirty "noticings and returns" is a sitting with thirty rounds of training — not at all wasted.

1.2. Distraction by thought

A wandering mind is situation number one. Proceed in four steps:

  • 1. Recognize: "Ah, I am thinking of something else." This very moment is the moment mindfulness (Pali: sati) wakes up — see it clearly, do not rush past it.
  • 2. Note briefly: place a short label in the mind — "thinking", "remembering", "planning" — and stop at the label, without continuing into the story.
  • 3. Release gently: no need to chase the thought away; just stop feeding it. A thought no longer attended to fades by itself, like a fire with no more wood added.
  • 4. Return: bring attention back to the object — the breath, or the object you have chosen (the meditation subject — the chosen object for training the mind, for example the breath - Pali: kammaṭṭhāna) — softly, like setting a light object down on a table.

The canon treats handling thought seriously enough to devote an entire discourse to five approaches when the mind is pulled away by thoughts — including: replacing an unwholesome thought with a wholesome opposite, investigating the cause of that thought, and simply restraining and forgetting it with clenched teeth and tongue pressed to the palate — a very concrete way of "cutting the source". The practitioner chooses the approach according to circumstances, like a traveller choosing shoes for the terrain. Source: Vitakkasaṇṭhāna Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 20 - Theravāda Buddhism.

For beginners, the four steps above are enough; the five approaches in the discourse are for when thoughts become seriously persistent. The full theory of these obstacles is in article M.C1.3, "The five hindrances".

1.3. Drowsiness

Drowsiness (heaviness, drooping, nodding eyes) is the hardest visitor to handle, because it does not "pass through" like thought but weighs down the whole body. Responses from gentle to strong:

  • Shift the point of attention: leave the comfortable haze that breeds sleep and move to something tighter and brighter — for example counting the breath again from one, or attending to the light touch of air at the nostrils.
  • Brighten the body: straighten the back fully (no leaning), stretch the shoulders, take a deep breath and sit tall; open the eyes.
  • Use the body: rub the palms warm and press them over the eyes, rub the ears gently, massage the neck and shoulders.
  • Walking meditation: stand up and walk slowly with clear knowing of each step — movement is the best remedy for dullness (see the walking-meditation section in article M.A4, "Preparing for a Sitting").
  • If still drowsy: rest lying down mindfully for a few minutes (lying on the right side), or end the sitting early and choose a more wakeful hour tomorrow — nodding sleep is a sign the body lacks sleep, not a fault of technique.

These responses have a clear canonical origin: when the Venerable Moggallāna was still in training, boredom and drowsiness visited him while he sat, and the Buddha taught in turn eight approaches against dullness — from not attending to the perception that breeds sleep, to stretching the body, washing the eyes with water, looking around, walking meditation, and finally lying down mindfully if none work. The canon thus acknowledges: sometimes proper rest is the solution, not further forcing. Source: Moggallāna Sutta, Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 7.58 - Theravāda Buddhism.

A tip for prevention: if morning sittings are always drowsy, try changing the hour, softening an over-cosy seat, brightening the room, and reviewing the night's sleep — these three physical causes account for most cases of "chronic drowsiness when sitting".

1.4. Restlessness

Restlessness (unable to sit still, wanting to stand up, limbs wanting to fidget) is handled in the opposite direction from drowsiness — using deliberate movement rather than forcing stillness:

  • Clearly know the feeling: first, know "restlessness is present" — where in the body, what it feels like. Often once clearly seen, it has already settled somewhat.
  • Shorten the sitting: if ten minutes is too much, five minutes is a reasonable victory — do not brace through to "make the hour" and then build aversion to the cushion.
  • Alternate with walking: three minutes sitting, three minutes walking, in alternation — the total time may be longer yet without breeding restlessness.
  • Give the hands a light job: counting beads, or simply counting the breath — restlessness is often surplus energy with nowhere to go.

If restlessness comes with regret, with thoughts circling what was done or left undone — that is restlessness and remorse (Pali: uddhacca-kukkucca), one of the five hindrances (Pali: nīvaraṇa); detailed understanding and antidotes are in article M.C1.3, "The five hindrances".

1.5. The "recognize — release gently — return" loop

Gathering all three visitors into a single loop usable in every situation:

  • Step 1 — Recognize: clearly know what is present ("thinking", "drowsy", "restless"). This is mindfulness waking up; it is already success.
  • Step 2 — Release gently: do not fight, do not feed further. With drowsiness, "releasing gently" may mean changing posture or opening the eyes; with restlessness it may mean standing up to walk — that is still intelligent adjustment, not giving up.
  • Step 3 — Return: bring attention back to the object, softly, like setting a light object down.
  • Step 4 — Without judging: drifted into the old story again before noticing? Still normal. Begin again from step 1. Continue like this until the timer ends.

The Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta describes the underlying attitude for this whole process in its famous repeated formula: practising "ardent, clearly comprehending, mindful" and then "putting away longing and discontent with the world" — that is, enthusiastic yet not clinging to whatever is happening, nor hating it. That is precisely the attitude of "non-judgment" in canonical language. Source: Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 10 - Theravāda Buddhism.

2. How to end a sitting

2.1. Why not jump up at once

The bell rings — many people habitually open their eyes, stand up, and rush straight to the next thing. That wastes the best part of the sitting: the final minutes are when the mind is calmest, and how you end determines the state you carry away from the cushion. Jumping up abruptly after deep sitting also easily causes dizziness, and over time it builds a reflex of "hurry" even toward practice.

2.2. Four steps for ending

  • Step 1 — Deliberately release attention: no need to hold the object any longer; let the mind widen to take in the whole sitting body, the surrounding room, sounds near and far.
  • Step 2 — Know the body: one final quick scan — back, shoulders, legs — clearly feeling the body's state now, any numbness or ache. Clearly knowing "there is numbness in the leg" helps you rise slowly and avoid postural dizziness.
  • Step 3 — Know the mind: what is the mind's state as you end — settled, gentle, or still caught in something? Just note it, no need to fix it.
  • Step 4 — Move unhurriedly: gently stretch, roll neck and shoulders a few rounds, open the eyes, look around the room for a few seconds, then stand up. The whole process takes about one to two minutes.

These four steps are in fact applying mindfulness (sati) to the very moment of transition — the most easily overlooked point of the session.

2.3. Dedication of merit — for those whose tradition includes it

Many Vietnamese following a tradition end with dedication of merit: silently wishing that whatever was just practised may be shared with parents, teachers, relatives, and all beings for their well-being. Both the Southern and Northern traditions have this practice, each with its own dedication verses. Its practical bonus: it turns the sitting from "my business" into "something meaningful for others" — a natural glue that makes the habit last, and a beautifully motivated way to end. If you follow no particular tradition, you may substitute a self-reminder: "may I carry this calm outward, to everyone I meet today."

2.4. After standing up

  • The golden thirty-second window: right after the sitting, the mind still rests in calm — try holding that state a little longer before reaching for the phone or noisy business; this small window often has the clearest effect of the whole day.
  • Write one short line (optional): was today more drowsy or more restless? How many returns? Twenty seconds of notes will be very valuable after a few weeks (article M.B7 treats this more fully).
  • Carry practice into life: take one familiar daily activity — washing the face, drinking tea, climbing stairs — and do it with the same clear knowing as when sitting. That is how the sitting "docks" into life instead of ending at the cushion (the "Mindfulness in daily activity" section in article M.B6).

3. Summary and next steps

  • Distraction, drowsiness, and restlessness are familiar visitors for everyone — a non-judging attitude matters more than technique; each "recognize — release gently — return" is one successful round of training.
  • Handle drowsiness from gentle to strong: shift attention → brighten the body → walking meditation → mindful rest; the canon teaches all eight approaches and acknowledges that proper rest is also a solution.
  • End the sitting in four steps — release attention, know the body, know the mind, move unhurriedly — to carry the settled state away from the cushion; those whose tradition includes it may add a dedication.

Glossary:

  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
  • Mindfulness (Pali: sati): remembering and staying with the present object — the "recognizing" step in handling distraction is mindfulness waking up.
  • Meditation subject (Pali: kammaṭṭhāna): the chosen object on which the mind is placed in practice — the breath, bodily sensations, sounds.
  • Mental label: a short name silently placed on what is happening ("thinking", "drowsy") — a compact noting tool, without continuing into the story.
  • Five hindrances (Pali: pañca nīvaraṇa): the five familiar obstacles obscuring the mind — sensual desire, ill will, dullness and drowsiness, restlessness and remorse, doubt.
  • Restlessness and remorse (Pali: uddhacca-kukkucca): restless inability to sit still together with regret — one of the five hindrances.
  • Walking meditation (Pali: caṅkama): slow walking with mindfulness, clearly knowing each step — the best remedy for both dullness and restlessness.
  • "Ardent, clearly comprehending, mindful" (Pali: ātāpī sampajāno satimā): the repeated formula in the Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta describing the attitude of practice — enthusiastic, clearly understanding, mindful; "putting away longing and discontent with the world" is the part of non-clinging, non-aversion.
  • Meditation cushion (Sino-Vietnamese): the practitioner's seat (a stool or cushion for sitting meditation); in the app it refers generally to the place of practice.
  • Dedication of merit: redirecting the significance / wholesome benefit of a good deed just done toward others; a common way of ending a sitting in both the Southern and Northern traditions.
  • Sentient beings (Sanskrit: sattva): all beings with mind — humans, animals; in dedication words, the recipients of good wishes.
