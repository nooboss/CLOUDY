M.D2.1. Loving-Kindness (Mettā / Maitrī)

The group overview (article M.D2) introduced the divine abodes — four qualities of mind nourished as meditation subjects: loving-kindness, compassion, appreciative joy, equanimity — pointing to loving-kindness as the recommended doorway. This article deepens the first quality: loving-kindness. 1. What is loving-kindness — and what is it not?

Loving-kindness (Pali: mettā, Sanskrit: maitrī) is goodwill — wishing well for oneself and others. Among the four divine abodes, it is the mind's warm "temperature": seeing others, the mind spontaneously thirsts for their good — like a mother wishing her child healthy, needing no reason. Cultivating loving-kindness through meditation is called loving-kindness meditation (Pali: mettā bhāvanā); bhāvanā as article M.A1 said means "mental training".

Three important distinctions to avoid confusion:

  • Unlike affection. Affection (Pali: pema) is private feeling joined to one particular person and hoping to be returned; loving-kindness is broad and unconditional goodwill — wishing others well whether or not they love us. The Theravāda commentaries call affection loving-kindness's near enemy: so resembling as to easily mix, yet one liberating, one binding (in detail in article M.D2.5).
  • Unlike forcing oneself to feel pleasant. Radiating loving-kindness is not lying to oneself that all is well, nor suppressing sorrow and anger. Sorrow remains sorrow, anger remains anger — loving-kindness is a goodwill sown alongside that, working longer-term than today's feelings.
  • Unlike outer sweetness. Loving-kindness does not demand sweet speech or friendly display; the Karaṇīyamettā Sutta defines it through conduct: body – speech – mind doing no harm, contentment in livelihood, an easy way of life — genuine goodwill appears as a harmless way of life. [Source: Karaṇīyamettā Sutta, Sn 1.8, Sutta-nipāta - Theravāda Buddhism.]

2. Why and when to practise loving-kindness

Why: the canon lists eleven benefits of rightly cultivated loving-kindness — sleeping well, waking happily, no nightmares, loved by humans and non-humans, guarded by deities, untouched by fire and poison, mind easily concentrating, bright countenance, dying unconfused, and those sufficiently developed reaching liberation. [Source: Mettānisaṃsa Sutta, Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 11.15 - Theravāda Buddhism.] At practice level, loving-kindness is also a meditation subject (Pali: kammaṭṭhāna) leading to deep collectedness — four jhānas (Pali: jhāna) — as article M.D2 said.

When suitable: loving-kindness practice is especially useful when (1) the mind is tense, irritated with someone; (2) self-aversion and self-criticism appear (but see section 4 on the "oneself" step); (3) before sleep — loving-kindness softens the mind and deepens sleep (among the canon's listed benefits); (4) when calm abiding with the breath feels dry, the mind too braced — loving-kindness softens the ground first (article M.B5).

3. The object sequence: from oneself to all beings

The classical method is radiating goodwill through an object sequence — from easy to difficult, like weight training gradually. The Visuddhimagga presents the sequence that became standard: [Source: Visuddhimagga, ch. IX - Theravāda Buddhism.]

  • 1. Oneself: "May I be well..." — the start is always oneself, because goodwill for others flows from a mind not hating itself.
  • 2. Loved, respected persons: teachers, parents, close friends — goodwill toward them arises naturally.
  • 3. Neutral persons: passing acquaintances, shopkeepers — neither loved nor hated.
  • 4. Difficult persons: those who caused difficulties — the hardest stretch, needing the previous steps' foundation.
  • 5. All beings: boundlessly extended — all directions, all kinds — exactly the "boundless" spirit of this frame.

The sequence's reason: goodwill resembles water poured into a jar — pouring where already wet first spreads; beginning at once with difficult persons, goodwill lacks strength while aversion already rises, the session becoming battle. The Karaṇīyamettā Sutta closes in the same direction: "above, below, all around — toward all beings, radiate a boundless mind, without hatred, without harm".

4. Two frequent stuck places and handling

Stuck at the "oneself" step: beginners sometimes radiating phrases for themselves yet feeling empty, even irritated with themselves (self-criticism, self-blame — most people have some of this). Handling:

  • Do not force it out. Recalling a moment of unconditional love (a relative, a pet, a time genuinely helped) — letting that warm quality rise, then directing it toward oneself.
  • Remembering wishing oneself well is not selfish: a jar must hold water before pouring for others — the canon encourages cultivating loving-kindness for oneself as the basis for outward goodwill.
  • Still stuck, beginning the sitting with step 2 (a loved person) for a few minutes, then returning to step 1.

Stuck at the "difficult person" step: just thinking of them, phrases turn false — mouth reading "may you be well" while inside is "hardly". Handling:

  • Choosing the right "difficulty level": slightly difficult persons (noisy disturbers, those whose words once stung) — not beginning with those who caused grave harm. Raising difficulty like climbing a slope: small steps.
  • Lowering demands: no need to "love" them — only a minimal genuine goodwill: "may this person suffer no more", "may this person cause less suffering for themselves". A small true sentence beats a large false one.
  • Seeing hatred's effect on oneself: holding hatred toward anyone is holding hot coals in one's own heart; this step trains partly for oneself.
  • Hatred rising too strongly → retreating to easier steps, or switching to the breath for a few minutes to settle — do not force.

5. Sample phrases in English

Phrases are means for sowing goodwill, not spells with magic power; choosing short, concrete phrases one genuinely wishes. Common templates:

  • For oneself: "May I be well. May I be peaceful. May I be free from suffering. May I live skilfully."
  • For others (replacing "I" with a name): "May you be well. May you be free from suffering. May good things come to you."
  • For difficult persons — the minimal phrase: "May you suffer no more. May you cause less suffering for yourself and others."
  • For all: "May all beings be well. May all beings be free from suffering. May all beings live in peace."

A few notes on using phrases: keeping one phrase per object for several minutes instead of reading the whole series; radiating with picturing the person (face, name); if any phrase reads untrue, switching to a shorter one; and when the mind has warmed, the words may be released, keeping only goodwill — like releasing breath counting when the mind has steadied.

6. A sample loving-kindness sitting

  • Minutes 1–3 — settling: sitting steadily, following the breath a few minutes for the mind to soften.
  • Minutes 4–6 — oneself: picturing oneself, radiating phrases; if warm, staying longer; if stuck, handling per section 4.
  • Minutes 7–9 — spreading: loved → neutral persons (one minute each, concretely pictured, short phrases); difficult persons if feeling steady — adding; if not steady, skipping, not required each sitting.
  • Minute 10 — widening and closing: "all beings" a few rounds; then back to the breath a few breaths, clearly knowing the mind's state at closing, transitioning to daily life as in ending sittings.
  • After the sitting: noting only two journal lines — "today kindest toward whom; where stuck". Loving-kindness progress is measured in daily life: less grumbling, easier seeing others' good — not measured by "warm" feelings in sittings.

7. Summary and next steps

  • Loving-kindness is goodwill wishing well for oneself and others — unlike affection (private, possessive), unlike forcing pleasant feelings, unlike outer sweetness.
  • The classical sequence: oneself → loved → neutral → difficult → all beings — from easy to difficult, like water poured into a jar.
  • Two familiar stuck places each have handling: recalled warm quality for the "oneself" step; "slightly difficult level" + minimal true phrases for the "difficult person" step.
  • Phrases are means for sowing goodwill — short, concrete, true; progress measured in daily life, not in-sitting feelings.

Suggested next reading: loving-kindness wishes others happiness; when seeing others suffering, goodwill turns into another form — Compassion (Karuṇā): wishing others' suffering reduced, and distinguished from pity as well as empathic exhaustion. See you there.

Glossary:

  • Loving-kindness (Pali: mettā, Sanskrit: maitrī): goodwill wishing well for oneself and others.
  • Loving-kindness meditation (Pali: mettā bhāvanā): the meditation method developing loving-kindness through an object sequence.
  • Affection (Pali: pema): love joined to one particular person and hoping to be returned — loving-kindness's near enemy.
  • Intention phrases: short phrases radiating goodwill per object — loving-kindness practice's means.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
