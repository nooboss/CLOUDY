M.C1.1. Foundations of Calm Abiding and Concentration

The "Shared Practical Skills" group (articles M.B1 → M.B7) is complete. You now have the tools: anchoring the mind, recognizing distraction, a balanced attitude, and ways of tracking yourself. The next step in the app's path is choosing a method — and the most natural doorway is calm-abiding meditation: the direction of practice that calms the mind and develops focus, the foundation nourishing every later method. This article has two parts: what calm abiding is (including how traditions understand it), and concentration — the heart of calm abiding — what it really means: not forcing attention hard, but sustaining attention steadily, softly, and with few breaks.

1. What is calm-abiding meditation (Samatha)?

1.1. Name and meaning

In Sino-Vietnamese, chỉ means "stopping, settling down" — the first face of the tranquillity–insight pair; the other side is insight meditation (Pali: vipassanā), the direction of clearly seeing observation, which will have its own group (C2). Calm-abiding meditation (Pali: samatha) can be summarized in one sentence: calming the mind and developing sustained focus on a chosen object. M.A1 already used the familiar image: calm abiding settles the mind, insight clarifies it; this article enters the "settling" side.

1.2. What calm abiding does

  • Trains on one meditation subject (Pali: kammaṭṭhāna): the breath, bodily sensations, sounds, images... — the common subjects will be presented in the next part (M.C1.2). From M.B1 to M.B2, you have already trained exactly this spirit with the breath.
  • Near result: the mind wavers less, grows usable, focus lengthens; body and mind feel pleasant and soft — this softness is real, but recall article M.B7: it is an accompanying sign, not the measure.
  • Deep result: at a high level, calm abiding leads to deep states of collectedness called jhāna (Pali: jhāna) — the advanced part of group C1 (section M.C1.4) will treat them separately.
  • Role: a settled mind is a sharpened tool — not the final destination of the path, but the basis for seeing clearly (wisdom). The canon places tranquillity and insight as two things to be cultivated, interwoven. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 2.31 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

1.3. What calm abiding is not

  • Not "freezing" the mind. Settled means waves grow smaller — not that nothing happens any more; experience still flows, only the mind is not swept away.
  • Not suppressing emotions. Avoiding and pushing aside only presses waves to the bottom; proper calm abiding lets agitation settle by itself like still water clarifying.
  • Not hunting special states. Genuine settling looks cheerfully boring: nothing spectacular at all.
  • No requirement to attain jhāna to "have concentration". Concentration begins with short chains of attention joined together; jhāna is the deep part of the same spectrum, not the entrance ticket.

2. Calm abiding across traditions

As the taxonomy's principle stated: tranquillity and insight are two shared methods, each tradition concretizing them in its own way:

  • Theravāda Buddhism: calm abiding is organized into systems of subjects — notably the forty subjects compiled in the Visuddhimagga (Pali: visuddhimagga); the subjects lead to different levels of collectedness, and this whole group has its own article in the app (group D3). [Source: Visuddhimagga - the Theravāda commentarial system.]
  • Mahāyāna Buddhism (East Asia): the tranquillity–insight pair is systematized into a path of practice — above all the Tiantai school with its doctrine of calming and contemplation (Chinese: zhǐguān); the two sides are seen as two wings of the same training. [Source: Samādhirāja Sūtra - Mahāyāna Buddhism.]
  • Vajrayāna Buddhism: calm abiding (Sanskrit: śamatha) is a foundation that may precede or accompany later practices, depending on lineage; the app has a separate article presenting it (group M.E3.3).

What is worth noting: all three traditions keep exactly the two core words — settling and focusing. They differ in subjects, stages, and ways of combining with insight meditation, not in essence.

3. Concentration — not squeezing attention

3.1. Two images of "focus"

Concentration (Pali: samādhi) is most misunderstood at the very word "focus". There are two different images:

  • The squeezing image: focusing means gripping attention tightly around the object — like clutching an object, veins standing on the hand. This creates a distinct sense of "straining", but a long-squeezed hand tires, trembles, and deforms what it holds. In meditation: head tense, chest constricted, restlessness rising — exactly the "too tight" signal of the lute string.
  • The placing image: focusing means placing attention on the object and letting it stand by itself — like laying a leaf lightly on water. No veins needed; only placing again each time it falls. This is true collectedness of mind.

The original meaning of samādhi says the same: "gathering together rightly in place" — the factors of mind converging on one point in balance, not squeezed. Concentration is a quality of balance, not of coercion.

3.2. Three marks of proper concentration

  • Stable: breaks fewer, chains of attention lengthening — perhaps not yet smooth, but trending so.
  • Soft: no bracing — no headache, no chest constriction, no restlessness. Body and face must be releasable while attention still operates.
  • Clear: the object truly clearly seen, not hazy. Unclear means sinking (the "too loose" side); clear yet tense means the "too tight" side — right concentration sits in the middle.

Missing any of the three, adjust as already learned: lacking softness → release gently, lower the goal; lacking clarity → straighten the back, open the eyes slightly, sharpen the "knowing"; lacking stability → that is time's work, train per section 4.

3.3. Why "forcing hard" backfires

Concentration grows through continuity, not through tightness. Each "straining" creates one agitation: straining → tension → trembling → losing collectedness → further straining — the beginner's familiar spiral. An everyday image: even handwriting comes from a hand holding the pen released just right, not strangling the nib; smooth driving comes from a light hand and far-seeing eyes, not clutching the wheel to death. On the cushion likewise: make it half as "strained" as you imagine necessary — most beginners find the quality of knowing rises, not falls.

4. The continuity of attention

4.1. Continuity decides concentration

Two people sitting "focused" alike: one broken densely — dropping out every few seconds, rejoining every few seconds; one joining a long chain — gently seamless. The second, though far "softer", truly has more concentration than the first. The discourses use a suggestive image: a concentrated mind is compared to oil poured out in an unbroken stream — not flicked in separate drops. [Source: Samādhibhāvanā Sutta, Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 5.28 - Theravāda Buddhism.] The quality of concentration is measured by continuity, not by the "hardness" of attention.

4.2. Continuity is built by rejoining

No one holds an unbroken chain from the start. Continuity is the accumulated result of rejoining:

  • Each time attention drops, the recognize — release gently — return loop is exactly the chain-joining operation — and it runs more naturally the more it has been trained.
  • The familiar subject (the breath) is the standard "connector": same place, same way of sensing, each return landing on the right joint.
  • A useful small goal: "three consecutive clearly known breaths" — repeating many chains. Once three in a row is doable, five arrives by itself; racing long chains from the start buys only strain.

4.3. What to measure continuity by

  • Fewer breaks slowly decreasing over weeks — the journal's "returned about... times" is exactly this measure.
  • Each chain lengthening: same number of breaks but each chain longer — quiet yet genuine progress.
  • Returning growing lighter: previously "returning" was a heavy act; now natural as a nod. When you notice this, concentration has grown unawares — people usually see concentration through results ("today noisy sounds hardly stirred the mind") before seeing it through feelings while sitting.

And because continuity matters more than strength, do not change subjects whenever "it won't settle" — changing subjects cuts the chain mid-weave. Article M.B2 already gave the rule: try long enough before changing.

5. Summary and next steps

  • Calm-abiding meditation (Samatha): calming the mind and developing sustained focus on one subject; the near result is a less wavering mind, the deep result the jhāna levels; its role is the basis for insight meditation — all three traditions keep the two core words: settling and focusing.
  • Concentration: stable — soft — clear, not squeezing; it grows through continuity, and continuity is built by repeated rejoining; measured by fewer breaks, longer chains, and lighter returning — all recordable in the journal.
  • By heart: half the straining, a little more continuity — that is the way of calm abiding.

Glossary:

  • Calm-abiding meditation (Pali: samatha, Sanskrit: śamatha): the direction of practice calming the mind and developing sustained focus; the Sanskrit form (śamatha) is commonly met in Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna contexts.
  • Calming and contemplation (Chinese: zhǐguān): the pair of "calming" (stopping, settling) and "contemplation" (observing, clearly seeing) as two sides of the same training; systematized by the Tiantai school (East Asian Mahāyāna).
  • Jhāna (Pali: jhāna, Sanskrit: dhyāna): the deep states of collectedness to which calm abiding leads at a high level; presented separately in the app's advanced section.
  • Meditation subject (Pali: kammaṭṭhāna): the object on which the meditator relies to gather and develop the mind, for example the breath, bodily sensations, sounds.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Visuddhimagga (Pali: visuddhimagga): the compendium of meditation practice in Theravāda Buddhism, composed by the Elder Buddhaghosa around the 5th century.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
