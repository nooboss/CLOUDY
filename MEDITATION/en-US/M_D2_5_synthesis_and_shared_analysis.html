M.D2.5. Synthesis and Shared Analysis

The four previous articles traversed the four qualities in turn: loving-kindness, compassion, appreciative joy, equanimity. This article merges them from a system viewpoint — exactly the two sub-articles of group M.D2: the four divine abodes as one system and the near enemies – far enemies of each quality. This is group M.D2's "overall map" article, and a mind-quality test for oneself: recognizing the quality of goodwill present, and recognizing when goodwill is occupied by disguised impostors.

1. The four qualities — one compensating system

The four qualities are not four discrete options but compensate one another — each keeping the others from skewing:

  • Loving-kindness lacking compassion: goodwill stopping at generic wishing — suffering persons right before the eyes only receiving hollow wishes. Compassion supplies loving-kindness the right-place part: seeing concrete suffering, wishing concrete good.
  • Compassion lacking equanimity: vibrating with others then sinking with them — compassion falling into sorrow, then compassion fatigue. Equanimity holds the viewing distance for compassion to endure.
  • Joy lacking equanimity: rejoicing with others becoming gushing, hollow when others' joy passes.
  • Equanimity lacking loving-kindness – compassion – joy: empty balance — drifting amid gain and loss with nothing warm to balance. The Theravāda commentaries warn newly trained equanimity easily turns cold under a beautiful name; genuine equanimity is the balance of an already warm mind, not of an empty mind.

The Theravāda commentaries' image: the four qualities like four adjoining houses — living alone in one house is not yet living fully; hence meditation lineages present them as a system to train fully, not choosing one and abandoning three. [Source: Visuddhimagga, ch. IX - Theravāda Buddhism.]

2. Each quality's objects and situations

The second system view: the four qualities divide by situations of meeting others — unmixed, unlayered:

  • Loving-kindness — the background, present in all situations: wishing well for others, undistinguishing suffering or happy.
  • Compassion — the situation of persons suffering: wishing suffering reduced.
  • Joy — the situation of persons happy: rejoicing along.
  • Equanimity — unclear situations or beyond one's part: balancing when nothing can yet be done, when results are beyond one's power, or when long viewing is needed before acting.

So viewed, practitioners have one test question before all situations: "which quality does this moment need?" — suffering persons, compassion; happy persons, joy; oneself highly upset, equanimity first then acting. The four qualities are like four tools in one box: choosing the right tool for each task; and loving-kindness is the box's shared handle.

3. Near enemies and far enemies — concept and use

The Theravāda commentaries divide each quality into two kinds of "enemies": [Source: Visuddhimagga, ch. IX - Theravāda Buddhism.]

  • Near enemy (Pali: āsanna-paccatthika): states disguised exactly like that quality — near-viewed very similar, essentially different in nature. Their danger is hard to recognize: bearers imagine they possess the quality; they pierce from inside.
  • Far enemy (Pali: dūra-paccatthika): polar opposite states — standing at the axis's other end; disguising nothing at all, plainly "not". Their danger is plain — meeting, seeing, no examining needed.

How to use the list below: using near enemies as mirrors — when seeing oneself possessing that quality, asking whether it is the near enemy; using far enemies as alarm bells — hearing the bell, knowing fully skewed, needing to retreat to the quality's basic principles (loving-kindness: unconditional goodwill; compassion: standing near without sinking; joy: rejoicing without comparing; equanimity: clearly knowing without leaning).

4. The near-enemy / far-enemy table of the four qualities

Contents per the Theravāda commentaries, presented as a list per the app's convention (no horizontal tables):

  • Loving-kindness: near enemy — affection (love joined to wanting and keeping for oneself — article M.D2.1); far enemy — ill will (Pali: byāpāda): plainly polar — anger shows loving-kindness absent.
  • Compassion: near enemy — sorrow (sinking with others' pain, losing capacity); far enemy — cruelty (Pali: hiṃsā): plainly polar — wanting to see others suffer more.
  • Joy: near enemy — self-interested joy (glad at others' good because it touches one's own interests); far enemy — envy (Pali: issā): plainly polar — seeing others' good as one's own loss.
  • Equanimity: near enemy — unwise indifference (a cold face from not seeing); far enemy — agitation (Pali: rāga-dosa — the mind rocked by gain – loss like a rudderless boat).

Reading the table both directions — self-mirroring: "today I see myself very kind — or is that affection? Very compassionate — or sorrow? Very equanimous — or indifferent?"; reading others — when someone claims a quality yet behaviour mismatches, a near enemy may be occupying the name behind. This second use is only for alertness, not a tool for judging others — evaluating others is outside the app's practice scope.

5. The four divine abodes across all three traditions

5.1. Theravāda

The four qualities are four of the forty meditation subjects (Pali: kammaṭṭhāna) — with distinct methods, object sequences, and collectedness destinations (loving-kindness – compassion – joy leading to the first three jhāna levels; equanimity — by its objects — to the fourth; details in article M.C1.4). [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 4.125–126 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

5.2. Mahāyāna

The four divine abodes are the basis of bodhicitta (Sanskrit: bodhicitta — the mind resolved on awakening for beings): loving-kindness and compassion placed foremost; compassion unfolded to great compassion (Pali: mahākaruṇā) — boundless compassion, unlimited in objects, the central motive of the whole Mahāyāna path. Mahāyāna practices (group E2) like giving-and-taking (article M.E2.6) all erect on this basis.

5.3. Vajrayāna

The four divine abodes keep a place in the path's preliminary part — four foundational reflections before deeper practices (group E3); compassion keeps the central-motive role like Mahāyāna, with absolute and relative bodhicitta — two faces of the same motive.

5.4. Shared points and differences

  • Shared: all three traditions keep all four qualities, all regard them as ethical – spiritual foundation, all have cultivation methods. This is one of Buddhism's clearest meeting points.
  • Different: differing in place on the path (collectedness subjects — bodhi basis — preliminaries) and destination (deep collectedness — awakening for beings — distinctive practices). Thus differing in path frame, not in quality content.

6. Summary and next steps

  • The four qualities are one compensating system: lacking each piece skews — loving-kindness lacking compassion becomes hollow, compassion lacking equanimity sinks, joy lacking equanimity gushes, equanimity lacking the other three empties.
  • They divide by situations: suffering → compassion; happy → joy; unclear / beyond one's part → equanimity; and loving-kindness is the shared handle.
  • Near enemies (resembling disguises — hard to mirror) and far enemies (polar opposites — meeting, seeing) of the four qualities are tools for self-checking the mind's quality; near enemies are mirrors, far enemies bells.
  • All three traditions keep all four qualities, differing in place on the path — one of Buddhism's clearest meeting points.

Glossary:

  • Near enemy (Pali: āsanna-paccatthika): states disguised exactly like a divine-abode quality — different in nature, hard to recognize.
  • Far enemy (Pali: dūra-paccatthika): the plain polar opposite of a quality — meeting, seeing.
  • Ill will (Pali: byāpāda): wishing disadvantage for others — loving-kindness's far enemy; one of the five hindrances (article M.C1.3).
  • Cruelty (Pali: hiṃsā): wishing to see others harmed — compassion's far enemy.
  • Envy (Pali: issā): seeing others' good as one's own loss — joy's far enemy.
  • Agitation: the mind pulled tilting by gain – loss, praise – blame — equanimity's far enemy.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
