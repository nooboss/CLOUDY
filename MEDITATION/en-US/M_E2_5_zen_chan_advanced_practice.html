M.E2.5. Zen / Chan — Advanced Practice

Article M.E2.2 presented Zen at introductory level — shared forms and basic Zazen. This article enters the two transmission lineages' advanced practices, merging all 4 sub-articles per the taxonomy: just sitting (M066 — the Japanese Sōtō lineage), silent illumination (M139 — the Chinese source of the same practice), kōans (M067), and huàtóu (M140 — the Línjì lineage). 1. Just sitting (Shikantaza)

Just sitting (Chinese: zhǐguǎn dǎzuò; Japanese: shikantaza) is the Japanese Sōtō lineage's central practice — literally: "just" (solely) "attending" (tending matters) "sitting meditation" — sitting, and only sitting, in open alertness. Its scope: the sitting body, natural breath, half-open eyes (article M.E2.2) — and a mind clinging to no particular object: no counting, no questions, no visualizations — only whole sitting – knowing.

Two things to understand rightly:

  • This is NOT "sitting doing nothing". Just sitting demands open and continuous alertness — hearing all sounds without running along, seeing all sights without sticking, knowing all thoughts without chasing; "doing nothing" is true only in the operation part (not squeezing the mind onto objects), untrue in the quality part (knowing must be whole). Beginners sitting "just sitting" usually only achieve... sitting with eyes closed daydreaming — that is looseness, not true release.
  • Usually taught after learners have a basis. Because no object "to hold" like breath or names, this method needs a basis: distinctly knowing body – breath (group B), past the distraction-handling phase (article M.B3), with a teacher confirming the right road — hence not a beginner method.

2. Silent illumination (Mòzhào) — the Chinese source

Silent illumination (Chinese: mòzhào — "silently luminous") is the Chinese source of the same practice — systematized by Master Hongzhi Zhengjue (1091–1157) of the Chinese Cáodòng lineage: silence means settled — the mind not following thoughts; illumination means illuminating — natural distinct knowing, without intentionally squeezing. These two words summarize the practice's structure: settled without haziness, knowing without bracing — its diagram image is exactly tranquillity – insight (article M.E2.1) compressed into one sitting method.

Relations with shikantaza: the Japanese Sōtō lineage (through Master Dōgen — 13th century) brought this practice to Japan and developed it into the "just sitting" form of section 1 — hence silent illumination is the father, shikantaza the child; essentially one group: sitting in whole settled – luminous knowing, no particular object. Modern Zen books often mention both names; readers understanding the relation avoid confusion enough.

3. Kōans

Kōans (Chinese: gōng'àn; Japanese: kōan) are ancient masters' situations – questions – dialogues used as meditation objects — short forms "unsolvable" by ordinary thinking. Familiar examples: the question "Does a dog have buddha-nature?" answered by Master Zhaozhou with one word "No" (Chinese: wú; Japanese: mu); or the question "Before your parents gave birth to you, what was your original face?" — famous from the Sixth Patriarch's era and widely used in kōan collections. — Such sentences have no "answers" in dictionaries; their function is stopping the mind where thinking cannot process — and from that stopping place, direct seeing has a chance to appear.

Points to understand rightly (L5 level, only introductory presentation):

  • Kōans are tools, not entertaining riddles: beginners meeting kōan books often misunderstand as "finding clever answers" — wrong in both function and use; answers do not lie in words.
  • Kōan practice is joined to teacher – student relations: teachers assign kōans fitting learners, and test levels through private interviews (meeting teachers one-to-one, presenting experience); kōans are not self-taken from books for sitting "pondering" — this self-way is both ineffective and easily breeding progress illusions (article M.C2.5).
  • Why L5 level: this practice demands experienced guides, standard kōan sequences, and the capacity to recognize intermediate states — unlearnable from articles; the app only introduces so readers understand when meeting books.

4. Huàtóu

Huàtóu (Chinese: huàtóu) is the practice of holding one living question — the distinctive presentation of the Chinese Línjì lineage and very familiar in Vietnam (Vietnamized Zen temples using huàtóu as a principal method). Typical questions: "What is reciting the Buddha?" — the Zen–Pure Land bridge question met in article M.E2.4; or "Who is dragging this corpse about?" — the classical question familiar in Zen centres.

The practice's structure (introductory-level presentation):

  • Choosing one sentence — holding unchanged: practitioners do not change sentences by mood; teachers assign and hold exactly that sentence.
  • Doubt-sensation (Chinese: yíqíng): the central concept — a living wondering kindled in the mind: neither intellectual analysis (answers do not lie there), nor mechanical mouth-repeating; a "genuinely wanting to know" state — like those losing keys searching each place, not releasing until the needed place arrives.
  • Holding questions like holding breath: into all activities — sitting, walking, eating, working — questions following like shadows; distraction cutting, clearly knowing then returning (the familiar loop — article M.B3).
  • Why teachers are needed: huàtóu's state-transition thresholds (from "questions" to "doubt-sensation" to deep states) are visible only to the experienced; self-going easily stops at mouth-repeating (ineffective) or falls into dreamy states (risky — article M.A3). Hence this method ranks L5 and the app only introduces it.

5. Two roads: "just sitting" and "questions" — and summary

Placing the four methods above into two roads shows Zen advanced practice's shared structure:

  • The "just sitting" road (Cáodòng): silent illumination → shikantaza — from "silently luminous" to "just sitting": objects are whole sitting – knowing; training materials: releasing, opening, continuity; common risks: looseness becoming daydreaming — needing teacher correction.
  • The "questions" road (Línjì): kōans → huàtóu — from "unsolvable situations" to "living questions": objects are thinking's stopping points; training materials: doubt-sensation, the question's genuineness; common risks: mechanical mouth-repeating or self-"solving" — needing teacher correction.

The two roads resemble in the basics: both are ways for the mind to cross thresholds ordinary thinking cannot self-cross — one side through whole settledness, one side through questions driving the mind to stopping points; and both demand guides at advanced level — the shared point of all L4–L5 methods in group E.

Summary:

  • Just sitting (shikantaza) — the Cáodòng lineage's "only sitting": no particular object, open continuous alertness; not "sitting doing nothing" and taught after a basis exists.
  • Silent illumination (mòzhào) by Hongzhi Zhengjue is the Chinese source — "silence" (settled) + "illumination" (luminous): shikantaza is the Japanese form of the same practice lineage.
  • Kōans — thinking-unsolvable situations used as Línjì meditation objects; tools not riddles; tightly joined to teacher – student relations through private interviews; L5 level — the app only introduces.
  • Huàtóu — living questions ("What is reciting the Buddha?", "Who is dragging this corpse?") + doubt-sensation (yíqíng) — the centre of Chinese and Vietnamese Línjì; needing guides.
  • Two roads — "just sitting" and "questions" — share purpose: moving minds across thinking thresholds; both demanding a basis (group B) and guides (article M.A6) at advanced level.

Glossary:

  • Just sitting (Chinese: zhǐguǎn dǎzuò; Japanese: shikantaza): "only sitting" in open alertness — the Japanese Sōtō lineage's central practice.
  • Silent illumination (Chinese: mòzhào): "silently luminous" — systematized by Hongzhi Zhengjue (1091–1157), the Chinese Cáodòng lineage.
  • Dōgen (Japanese: Dōgen, 1200–1253): the master bringing Cáodòng practice to Japan — founding the Sōtō lineage.
  • Kōans (Chinese: gōng'àn; Japanese: kōan): thinking-unsolvable Zen situations – questions used as Línjì meditation objects.
  • Huàtóu (Chinese: huàtóu): living questions holding the mind in the Chinese and Vietnamese Línjì lineage.
  • Doubt-sensation (Chinese: yíqíng): living wondering kindled in the mind — huàtóu's training material.
  • Private interviews (Japanese: dokusan): meeting teachers privately one-to-one in kōan practice — presenting experience and receiving confirmation.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
