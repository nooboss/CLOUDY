M.D1.0. The Four Foundations of Mindfulness — Overview

Previous groups trained skills piece by piece: holding attention (article group M.B), calming the mind (article group M.C1), observing clearly (article group M.C2). This article presents the most basic framework arranging all those pieces into one complete system — the frame the canon calls the four establishments of mindfulness: four domains for placing mindfulness: body, feelings, mind, dhammas. This frame has long been implicitly present on your path — "clearly knowing the breath" is body contemplation; "knowing the liking / aversion leaning" touches feeling contemplation; "clearly knowing the scattered mind" is mind contemplation. Now is the time to view the whole map.

1. Name and sources

The four establishments of mindfulness (Pali: satipaṭṭhāna, Sanskrit: smṛtyupasthāna) — "sati" is mindfulness (Pali: sati), "paṭṭhāna" understood in two complementary ways: "place of establishing" (where mindfulness stands firm) and "foundation" (mindfulness's footing). The four establishments are thus the four grounds on which mindfulness is established: body, feelings, mind, dhammas.

The sources are two foundational discourses: the Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta (Majjhima Nikāya, MN 10) and the Mahāsatipaṭṭhāna Sutta (Dīgha Nikāya, DN 22) - Theravāda Buddhism; the four-establishments frame also appears in Mahāyāna Buddhist (Sanskrit: mahāyāna) scriptures with corresponding presentations. The discourses open with the famous declaration: "This is the direct path for purifying beings, overcoming sorrow and lamentation, ending pain and grief, accomplishing truth, realizing nirvana — that is, the four establishments of mindfulness." [Source: Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 10 - Theravāda Buddhism.] Within Theravāda Buddhism's system of meditation subjects, the four establishments are also placed among the forty subjects (Pali: kammaṭṭhāna) — group M.B1 already met this word.

2. The four domains of mindfulness

2.1. Contemplation of the body — Kāyānupassanā

Contemplation of the body (Pali: kāyānupassanā): placing mindfulness on the body's material phenomena — breath, walking-standing-sitting-lying postures, everyday activities, and the tradition's more detailed body contemplations. The body is the distinctest, coarsest domain — the natural starting place, hence every path begins here.

2.2. Contemplation of feeling — Vedanānupassanā

Contemplation of feeling (Pali: vedanānupassanā): placing mindfulness on feeling (Pali: vedanā) — the body–mind's automatic reaction layer at contact with scenes. This domain is one notch "thinner" than body: feelings arise – pass fast, and exactly where liking / aversion leanings seed.

2.3. Contemplation of mind — Cittānupassanā

Contemplation of mind (Pali: cittānupassanā): clearly knowing the state of the mind present — with or without greed, with or without hatred, scattered or contracted, noble or base. Unlike feeling contemplation (where feelings), mind contemplation views the material and condition of knowing — as clearly seeing "today this mind is which mind".

2.4. Contemplation of dhammas — Dhammānupassanā

Contemplation of dhammas (Pali: dhammānupassanā): placing mindfulness on the canon's classified groups — five hindrances, five aggregates, six internal – external bases, seven awakening factors, four noble truths. "Dhamma" here is not "doctrine" in the ordinary sense — to say clearly to avoid misreading: dhamma in the four establishments means the canon's classified phenomenon groups for observing, like "filing cabinets" for sorting what is observed.

3. The repeated structure in the discourses

The Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta's special point: all four domains use the same sentence template — understanding this template is grasping how to practise:

  • Contemplating within – without: observing phenomena on one's own body, then on others / extended — the canon explains: mainly one's own self-observation, but sometimes comparing, understanding others through one's own body.
  • Contemplating arising – passing: "seeing phenomena arising, seeing phenomena passing" — exactly the core trained in article M.C2.3.
  • "Bare knowing, just enough": mindfulness established only at the knowing level — without further clinging.
  • Not relying, not clinging: dwelling unattached to anything in the world — the whole frame's final goal.

In other words: the four establishments are not four separate exercises but one manner applied to four domains — the familiar manner: clearly knowing, seeing arising – passing, not clinging.

4. Why the four establishments are called "the direct path"

Because these four domains cover tightly everything experience can have: every experience has a body side (matter), a feeling side (sensation), a mind side (knowing and state), and classifiable under dhamma groups. No "blind land": whatever is happening belongs to one of the four — and hence practising the four establishments is practising whole experience, not selecting beautiful scenes and discarding ugly ones. This is also why the frame is the basis of both methods — the four establishments are exactly insight meditation's observation frame (article M.C2.1), and other mindfulness forms (like loving-kindness — group M.D2) all stand on this basis.

5. Beginning practice within the four-establishments frame

  • From body outward: a standard sitting within the frame: a few minutes releasing and breathing (body contemplation) → recognizing pleasant / unpleasant / neutral feelings as they appear (feeling contemplation) → recognizing the present mental state (mind contemplation) → when hindrances or awakening factors appear, correctly naming the group (dhamma contemplation).
  • One domain per phase: beginners need not cover all four — choosing one domain as principal for several weeks (usually body), other domains only noted when naturally arriving; then expanding step by step.
  • Traversing all four long-term: traditions encourage not staying forever in one domain — body is the doorway, feelings – mind – dhammas the depth; the app's path will successively traverse these four domains in detailed articles.
  • The discourses mandate no single sequence: the four establishments are four grounds, not four rigid stairs — many modern systems unfold differently; the shared principle remains: beginning from what is distinctest for oneself.

6. Summary and next steps

  • The four establishments of mindfulness (satipaṭṭhāna): four grounds for establishing mindfulness — body, feelings, mind, dhammas; sources: Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta MN 10 and Mahāsatipaṭṭhāna Sutta DN 22; the discourses call it "the direct path".
  • One manner, four domains: clearly knowing — seeing arising – passing — not clinging; tightly covering all experience, selecting no scenes.
  • Practice: from the distinctest domain (body) outward, expanding step by step; no need to cover all four from the start.

Glossary:

  • Four establishments of mindfulness (Pali: satipaṭṭhāna, Sanskrit: smṛtyupasthāna): four grounds for establishing mindfulness — body, feelings, mind, dhammas; the canon regards it as the direct path to purification and nirvana.
  • Contemplation of body / feeling / mind / dhammas (Pali: kāyānupassanā / vedanānupassanā / cittānupassanā / dhammānupassanā): the four domains of mindfulness; "upassanā" (anupassanā) means continuous contemplation.
  • Feeling (Pali: vedanā): the pleasant – unpleasant – neutral sensation automatically appearing at contact with scenes; unlike purely physical sensation.
  • Dhamma in the four establishments: the canon's classified phenomenon groups (hindrances, aggregates, bases, awakening factors, noble truths) — not "doctrine" in the ordinary sense.
  • Forty subjects (Pali: kammaṭṭhāna): the collectedness-subject classification system per the Theravāda commentarial system; the four establishments are one group within it.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
