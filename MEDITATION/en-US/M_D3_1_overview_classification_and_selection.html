M.D3.1. Overview, Classification, and Selection

Previous groups traversed each practice frame: the four establishments of mindfulness, the four divine abodes. This article opens group D3 with the Theravāda commentarial system's largest picture: the forty meditation subjects — the map classifying all subjects, with the most practical question: which subjects lead to which collectedness levels, and where should someone like me begin. 1. What is a meditation subject (Kammaṭṭhāna)?

Meditation subjects (literally "where the mind's work is placed" - Pali: kammaṭṭhāna) are objects meditators choose to hold the mind on: the breath, a shrine image, a coloured circle, a quality of the Buddha, or the feeling of walking steps — each a subject. This concept already appeared from article M.A1 (meditation is directing the mind to a chosen object) and throughout group C1; the forty-subject system is how the Theravāda commentarial system — most notably in the Visuddhimagga — arranges all those objects into one complete map. [Source: Visuddhimagga, ch. III - Theravāda Buddhism.]

Two things to understand at once about this map. First, it is a commentarial synthesis — the canonical basket lists subjects scattered across many discourses; the Visuddhimagga (5th century) gathered, classified, and annotated them into the 40 system. Second, no one needs to traverse all 40 — the map is for choosing, not for roll-calling; practitioners may need only a few subjects in a lifetime to go very far.

2. The forty-subject map

The 40 system divides into six large groups, with two independent subjects — presented as a list per the app's convention (no horizontal tables):

  • Ten kasiṇas (Pali: kasiṇa): earth, water, fire, air, blue, yellow, red, white, light, space — coloured circles for developing deep collectedness. In detail in article D3.2.
  • Ten foulness meditations (Pali: asubha): ten corpse contemplations, reducing infatuation with the body — presented at historical–monastic level, with its own safety warnings (article M.D3.3).
  • Ten recollections (Pali: anussati): Buddha, Dhamma, Saṅgha, ethics, generosity, deities, death, body, breath, peace — the "remembering" group nourishing confidence and calm; overview and sub-articles in article D3.7.
  • Four divine abodes (Pali: brahmavihāra): loving-kindness, compassion, joy, equanimity — fully traversed in group M.D2; within the 40 system, these are four collectedness subjects.
  • Four formless attainments (Pali: āruppa): four imageless collectedness levels — presented in article M.C1.4; within the 40 system, they develop from kasiṇas.
  • One perception of repulsiveness in food (Pali: āhāre paṭikūlasaññā): contemplating food to reduce taste-greed (article M.D3.6).
  • One defining of the four elements (Pali: catudhātuvavatthāna): observing the body through four qualities hard / soft, cohesion, hot / cold, movement (article M.D3.5).

Additionally, mindfulness of breathing (Pali: ānāpānassati) — the subject groups B–C already used as basis — is one of the ten recollections but specially noted by the commentaries for leading to deep collectedness like kasiṇas.

3. Which subjects lead to which collectedness levels

The 40 system's subtlest point — what the taxonomy requires stating plainly: not all subjects lead to the same collectedness level. The Theravāda commentaries divide three levels: momentary concentration, access concentration, absorption (jhāna — Pali: appanā):

  • Leading to all four form-sphere jhānas: ten kasiṇas and mindfulness of breathing — the system's "deep collectedness" group.
  • Leading to the first jhāna: ten foulness meditations, food contemplation — aversion-tinged subjects cannot sustain deeper bliss.
  • Leading to the first three jhānas: loving-kindness, compassion, joy — three subjects joined to gladness – bliss, stopping before the fourth level.
  • Leading to the fourth jhāna: equanimity — per the commentaries' presentation (article M.D2.4 already gave the reason).
  • Leading only to access concentration: the remaining eight recollections (Buddha, Dhamma, Saṅgha, ethics, generosity, deities, death, peace) and the four-elements defining — these subjects nourish confidence, calm, and alertness; the collectedness they create is usable for insight meditation though not reaching jhāna.

The practical reading: "only to access concentration" does not mean "inferior" — for lay practitioners, mind-nourishing subjects are the layer supplying daily calm and inspiration; whether to reach jhāna is a separate path matter (article M.C1.4 already concluded: reference map, not scoreboard).

4. Six temperaments (Carita) and suggested subjects

The Theravāda commentaries add another layer to the map: different persons, fitting subjects differ — by temperament (Pali: carita). Six kinds:

  • Greedy-natured (Pali: rāgacarita): mind easily running after sense pleasures, liking beauty; suggested subjects — foulness contemplations, mindfulness immersed in the body.
  • Hating-natured (Pali: dosacarita): mind easily tense, irritated; suggested subjects — four divine abodes (especially loving-kindness), and coloured kasiṇas.
  • Deluded-natured (Pali: mohacarita): mind easily hazy, unclear about what is happening; suggested subjects — mindfulness of breathing, subjects demanding beat-by-beat distinctness.
  • Faith-natured (Pali: saddhācarita): mind easily trusting, wholesomely moved; suggested subjects — recollections (Buddha, Dhamma, Saṅgha, ethics, generosity, deities).
  • Intellect-natured (Pali: buddhicarita): mind liking analysis, quickly seeing everything; suggested subjects — four-elements defining, food contemplation, contemplative observations.
  • Speculative-natured (Pali: vitakkacarita): mind easily drifting in thought, scattered; suggested subjects — mindfulness of breathing (breath counting — article M.B1).

To understand rightly (as the taxonomy requires): this list is a commentarial reference tool, not a personality test. There is no "permanent temperament type"; the same person, greedy-natured now, may find the breath subject fittest. The list's genuine value suggests trial starting points — seeing oneself often tense and angry, trying a few minutes of loving-kindness; seeing oneself hazy, trying breath counting; fitting, continuing; unfitting, changing — exactly section 5's spirit.

5. Choosing a fitting subject for yourself

Synthesized from canon, commentaries, and modern lineages' experience, four choosing principles — in the order to follow:

  • 1. By goal first: needing a settled mind for observing (basis for insight meditation — group C2) → breath or still subjects; needing calm and inspiration → recollections; needing to work with loving-kindness – compassion – joy – equanimity → group D2. Goals decide the subject region first.
  • 2. By temperament and circumstances: using section 4 as suggestion; plus living circumstances — those surrounded by noise, sound becomes a ready subject; those with chronic pain, bodily sensation needs careful use; the busy, short subjects needing no equipment.
  • 3. Trying long enough before changing: one subject needs several steady weeks before evaluating — the first week's "unfitting" feeling is not yet data (the mind only beginning to learn something). The practical rule: trying one subject at least two steady weeks, journaling, then deciding to keep or change; avoiding subject-hopping by per-sitting feelings.
  • 4. With a teacher, asking the teacher: traditionally, choosing subjects is part of being guided — teachers see mind habits one does not see oneself (article M.A6); self-learning, using principles 1–3 and asking the experienced when possible.

Two safety warnings accompany choosing principles: foulness subjects (article M.D3.3) and death recollection (article M.D3.7) have their own warning volumes — not starting subjects for beginners, and unsuitable for those with body-image difficulties, death anxiety, or depression (article M.A3). The 40 map is not an equally good menu for everyone at all times — hence it accompanies the temperament classification and teachers' guidance.

6. Summary and next steps

  • Meditation subjects (kammaṭṭhāna) are chosen objects for holding the mind; the 40-subject system is the Visuddhimagga's synthesis — a map for choosing, not a list to complete.
  • Six groups + two independent subjects: kasiṇas, foulness, recollections, divine abodes, formless, food, four elements — each group one character; mindfulness of breathing is the distinctive subject leading to deep collectedness.
  • Different collectedness levels by group: kasiṇas and breath → four jhāna levels; foulness – food → first level; loving-kindness – compassion – joy → three levels; equanimity → fourth level; eight recollections and four elements → access concentration — enough for insight meditation.
  • Six temperaments suggest trial starts; choosing subjects in order: goal → temperament – circumstances → trying long enough (two steady weeks) → asking teachers when possible.

Suggested next reading: group D3 continues through each subject group — beginning with the most "deep-collectedness" and most purely technical group: Kasiṇa Devices — the ten kasiṇas (article M.D3.2). Beginners need not follow this article at once — understanding the map is enough, but it is the missing part of the 40-system picture. See you there.

Glossary:

  • Meditation subject (Pali: kammaṭṭhāna): chosen objects for holding the mind in meditation; kamma = work, ṭhāna = place.
  • Kasiṇa (Pali: kasiṇa): coloured circles or objects used as deep-collectedness subjects — earth, water, fire, air, four colours, light, space.
  • Foulness (Pali: asubha): corpse contemplations reducing infatuation with the body — presented at historical–monastic level.
  • Recollection (Pali: anussati, Sanskrit: anusmṛti): "remembering"-form subjects — Buddha, Dhamma, Saṅgha, ethics, generosity, deities, death, body, breath, peace.
  • Divine abodes (Pali: brahmavihāra): the name of the four qualities loving-kindness – compassion – joy – equanimity (group M.D2) when used as collectedness subjects.
  • Temperament (Pali: carita): the mind's existing inclinations per the commentaries — six kinds; a reference tool, not a test.
  • Access and absorption concentration (Pali: upacāra and appanā): two high collectedness levels — in detail in article C1.4.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
