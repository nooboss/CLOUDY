M.D2.2. Compassion (Karuṇā)

The previous article addressed loving-kindness — wishing well for oneself and others. But when seeing others suffering, that goodwill naturally turns into another form: wishing others' suffering reduced. That is compassion — the second quality of the divine abodes (article M.D2). Contents: what compassion is and how it differs from pity, where compassion begins, how to practise, three things often mixed (compassion – sorrow – empathic exhaustion), and compassion in daily life.

1. What is compassion — and how does it differ from pity?

Compassion (Pali: karuṇā, Sanskrit: karuṇā) is recognizing suffering and developing the wish to reduce that suffering. Compassion has three components together: seeing (recognizing suffering present), moved (the mind not indifferent before it), directed (wishing it reduced, and readiness to contribute to that).

The most important distinction: compassion differs from pity:

  • Pity looks from above: "sorry for you" — hiding a distance and an implicit safety: that suffering is theirs, I stand outside.
  • Compassion looks level: standing near, knowing that suffering could be mine — the same body, the same feeling, the same things that could befall. Practitioners often describe the difference in one sentence: pity keeps oneself clean of others' pain; compassion dares to approach.
  • Pity only feels; compassion wishes to act: pity stops at feeling sorry; compassion has "direction" — wishing suffering reduced, and asking what one could do, however small.

Compassion's near enemy on the other side is sorrow — sinking into others' pain until oneself collapses — detailed in article D2.5. And because the four qualities are one system, equanimity is what keeps compassion near without sinking (article M.D2.4).

Also worth knowing: in both Theravāda and Mahāyāna, this quality is raised very high — the Buddha is honoured with this very noun (great compassion — Pali: mahākaruṇā); compassion is described as the motive behind nearly all the Buddha's deeds across forty-five years.

2. Where compassion begins: clearly seeing suffering first

Compassion begins not from general good wishes, but from accurately seeing suffering — exactly the trained skills:

  • Seeing unpleasant feeling present — not skipping, not hastily covering;
  • Seeing the unsatisfactoriness of what oneself and others chase — understanding why suffering appears right amid what is taken as joy;
  • Seeing one's own suffering first — neither magnifying nor trivializing.

The Theravāda canon introduces the four divine abodes as liberations of mind countering each obstacle: loving-kindness to remove hatred, compassion to remove harmfulness (Pali: vihiṃsā). [Source: Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 46.54 - Theravāda Buddhism.] The discourses' understanding is very practical: "harm" arises when we do not see the suffering of those we resent — they appear in our eyes only as obstacles, not as persons also fearing pain and losing sleep from worry. Compassion is the right remedy there: training to see others' suffering — including difficult persons' — as suffering.

3. Compassion meditation — how to practise compassion

The method called compassion meditation (Pali: karuṇā bhāvanā) — the same structure as loving-kindness meditation: choosing objects, radiating phrases, holding the mind on objects — differing in that objects are suffering persons. The canon presents developing compassion through radiating it across directions, the same way as loving-kindness. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 10.208 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

The object sequence — easy to difficult:

  • 1. Oneself suffering: a genuine pain (body, mind, circumstances) — "May I be free from suffering. May this pain soften."
  • 2. Loved ones suffering: those whose circumstances you know well — goodwill easily arises.
  • 3. Strangers suffering: those in hospitals, those on sidewalks — pictured, then radiating.
  • 4. Difficult persons: the same "slightly difficult" level article M.D2.1 advised — seeing they too have their own suffering.
  • 5. All suffering beings: boundlessly extended.

The image often used to grasp compassion's spirit: seeing someone with a thorn in the foot — wishing to pull it out at once, not stopping to judge "why did you let the thorn pierce". Compassion meditation trains that "wanting to pull the thorn" impulse into a stable capacity — not waiting for natural kindness to rise at the right moment.

4. Compassion, sorrow, and empathic exhaustion — three things not to mix

Three states stand near and are often merged; distinguishing carefully keeps practice safe:

  • Compassion ≠ sorrow: sorrow is sinking together with others into pain — losing capacity and perspective. Compassion stands near without sinking; the "not sinking" part is held by equanimity (article M.D2.4) and the collectedness foundation (group C1). When seeing yourself sinking — retreating to the breath a few minutes, then continuing.
  • Compassion ≠ compassion fatigue: nurses, family caregivers, and social workers can all meet it. The cause is usually not "too much compassion" but receiving pain in a swept-away way — without method, without equanimity. The app's health programmes (group G2) present this form and care in detail; at neutral description level: methodical compassion — with distinct phrases and balanced perspective — is regarded as the way to sustain caregiving capacity, not the cause of depletion.
  • A quick self-check after thinking of someone's suffering: (a) do I want to do something feasible — however small? That is compassion operating. (b) Or only tired, heavy, wanting to avoid? If (b) lasts many days — that is a signal to rest, to equanimity, and when heavier, right-level support (article M.A3).

5. Compassion in daily life

  • Compassion toward oneself. This is the foundation, not indulgence: when in pain — bodily illness, failure, criticism — the usual reaction is blaming oneself ("how did it come to this"). Training to replace with a compassion sentence: "May I be free from suffering. May I be gentle with myself." This sentence is especially useful in practice's difficult phases and overload (articles M.C2.6, M.A3) — between genuine suffering and self-blame suffering, the latter is the part releasable at once.
  • Compassion in communication. Before someone telling a suffering story: hearing to the end — not hastily fixing, not hastily telling one's own story — then asking: "What can I do to help?" Practical compassion begins with listening; many feel the difference between "being heard to the end" and "being interrupted for the point" — exactly compassion and its lack.
  • Compassion does not demand carrying everything. "Wishing suffering reduced" does not mean "having to resolve everything". Often compassion's right action is very small: one question, one waiting, one shoulder pat. Recognizing "just enough" is a skill — both compassion and pity can turn into over-intervention; right-measure compassion trusts the other's capacity, not believing for them.
  • Warning about "selective" compassion. Compassion naturally floods toward those like oneself; toward others — difficult persons, those not on one's side — often empty. That is not wrong, only the starting point; training to widen compassion toward them small steps at a time like section 3's sequence, and not self-tormenting if that step is not yet done — noting in the journal is enough.

6. Summary and next steps

  • Compassion = seeing suffering + a moved, non-indifferent mind + wishing suffering reduced — unlike pity (looking from above, standing outside) and unlike sorrow / compassion fatigue (sinking with pain).
  • Compassion is the direct remedy of "harmfulness" (SN 46.54); it begins from accurately seeing suffering — the skills of feeling contemplation and suffering contemplation (article M.C2.3).
  • Compassion meditation shares loving-kindness meditation's structure: suffering objects + phrases, easy-to-difficult sequence; compassion toward oneself is the foundation.
  • Equanimity keeps compassion from burning — awaiting article M.D2.4; but before that comes the third quality: Appreciative Joy (Muditā) — genuinely rejoicing in others' happiness.

Suggested next reading: compassion faces those suffering; facing those happy is a skill harder than many think — Appreciative Joy (Muditā): genuinely rejoicing in others' happiness, the direct remedy of comparison and envy. See you there.

Glossary:

  • Compassion (Pali: karuṇā, Sanskrit: karuṇā): recognizing suffering and developing the wish to reduce suffering.
  • Pity: looking down on suffering persons from above, with distance — unlike compassion.
  • Sorrow: sinking into others' pain until losing capacity — compassion's near enemy (article M.D2.5 details).
  • Compassion fatigue: the depleted form of caregivers after long exposure to suffering — belonging to health contents (group G2).
  • Harmfulness (Pali: vihiṃsā): the wish to harm others — what compassion counteracts.
  • Great compassion (Pali: mahākaruṇā): the honorific noun for the Buddha — boundless, unlimited compassion.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
