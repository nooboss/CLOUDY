M.C2.4. Deeper Analytical Frameworks

The two previous articles trained the eye to see arising – passing and the three characteristics. This article brings in two analytical frameworks — "lenses" the canon uses to cut experience so its structure shows clearly: distinguishing mind and matter (separating body and mind), and dependent origination (seeing links giving rise to one another). Frameworks are not for memorizing — they are organizing tools during observation, like a floor plan helping one inside a building know which floor one is on. These two frameworks, and the progress map of the next article, are the deep part of the insight group: read slowly, verify through your own experience.

1. Distinguishing mind and matter

1.1. The concept

The canon divides experience into two sides: mind and matter (Pali: nāma-rūpa):

  • Matter (Pali: rūpa): bodily phenomena — breath, sensations, movements, the hard – soft – hot – cold – moving elements.
  • Mind (Pali: nāma): mental phenomena — knowing objects (as eye knows forms, ear knows sounds), with accompanying factors: feeling, perception, volition, thought.

The purpose of dividing is not to "live with only matter and mind" as a doctrine — but a breaking of the "I am..." block. Before distinguishing, experience arrives as a block: "I am in pain", "I am angry". After dividing, the same moment appears composed of parts: there is a material phenomenon (a hot sensation in the chest), and there is a mental phenomenon (knowing, a state of aversion). The "self" — bearer of the whole block — begins to fade in analysis's light; clearly seeing each part's separate arising – passing is a short step toward not-self.

1.2. How to train

  • Within one familiar phenomenon: the breath — recognizing two sides: belly movement (matter), the knowing of that movement (mind). Pain — sensation (matter), the knowing and aversive leaning (mind).
  • Reminding with two-sided labels: "knowing a sensation" instead of "I see"; some insight systems (especially Burmese lineages) make mind–matter distinguishing the first survey step and call the corresponding wisdom "knowledge distinguishing mind and matter" (Pali: nāmarūpapariccheda-ñāṇa).
  • Do not favour one side: the goal is not "dumping all into mind" (treating body as illusory) nor "dumping all into matter" (treating mind as by-product). The two sides run parallel, co-present, co-arising – passing.

2. Dependent origination in direct observation

2.1. The principle and the twelve-link version

Dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda, Sanskrit: pratītyasamutpāda) is Buddhism's central principle, summarized by the canon in one sentence: "When this exists, that exists; when this arises, that arises; when this ceases, that ceases; when this dissolves, that dissolves." [Source: Paṭicca-samuppāda Saṃyutta, Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 12 - Theravāda Buddhism.] The full version has twelve links — from ignorance leading to suffering. On the cushion, we need only observe the middle stretch — the most "alive" stretch in each moment:

contact → feeling → craving → clinging → (formations)

  • Contact (Pali: phassa): sense meeting scene — ear meeting sound, body meeting sensation, mind meeting thought.
  • Feeling (Pali: vedanā): pleasant – unpleasant – neutral feeling arising right after contact, automatically.
  • Craving (Pali: taṇhā): the mind's leaning — wanting more, wanting to push, wanting to keep.
  • Clinging (Pali: upādāna): craving one notch tighter — the "mine" story forms.
  • Formations: body – speech – mind acting along; and a new chain of results begins.

2.2. The point that can be "wedged into"

The dependent-origination chain is not a one-way road with no exit — the canon itself points to the wedge-in point: between feeling and craving. Feeling is contact's fruit — no one can forbid feeling arising; but craving need not arise along feeling — if feeling is clearly seen in time, mindfulness placed in, the leaning takes no root. The discourses use the image: two bundles of sticks — one bundle lacking enough fuel to flare. [Source: Saḷāyatana Saṃyutta, Saṃyutta Nikāya, SN 35.191 - Theravāda Buddhism.] That "feeling without lusting for feeling" is exactly this section's practice — also the "gap" between seeing and following previous articles mentioned (articles M.B3, M.C2.2), now placed in the canon's tightest doctrinal frame.

2.3. How to train

  • Reviewing one reaction through the dependent-origination chain: choose a recent event (an irritation, a craving), tracing back link by link: which contact? What feeling? How did craving rise? Where did clinging grip? At which link did the "mine" story form?
  • In the sitting hour: when a phenomenon arrives — holding only two sentences: "there is contact", "there is feeling"; and watching exactly the place: "will there be craving?". Even when craving still rises — seeing it rise is already one successful observation round.
  • Do not hate craving: craving arising is a phenomenon like all phenomena — hating craving is also a craving (wanting non-craving). The right view: seeing, and naturally not nourishing.

3. Summary and next steps

  • Mind – matter: dividing experience into material and mental sides — breaking the "I am..." block; the two sides parallel, co-arising – passing; the first survey step in many insight systems.
  • Dependent origination: the "when this exists, that exists" principle; on the cushion observing the stretch contact → feeling → craving → clinging; the wedge-in point lies between feeling and craving — clearly seeing feeling as it arises, the leaning takes no root.
  • These two frameworks are two lenses — not for memorizing to speak, but for using while looking; verified through experience itself, not through theoretical eloquence.

Glossary:

  • Mind and matter (Pali: nāma-rūpa): the pair of mental (mind) and material (matter) phenomena composing each moment of experience.
  • Knowledge distinguishing mind and matter (Pali: nāmarūpapariccheda-ñāṇa): the wisdom clearly separating mind and matter — regarded as the first survey step in the insight progress.
  • Dependent origination (Pali: paṭiccasamuppāda, Sanskrit: pratītyasamutpāda): the principle of phenomena arising conditionally; the full version has twelve links, from ignorance to aging – death.
  • Contact – feeling – craving – clinging (Pali: phassa – vedanā – taṇhā – upādāna): the four middle links of the dependent-origination chain, observable on the cushion: contact – feeling – thirst – grasping.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
