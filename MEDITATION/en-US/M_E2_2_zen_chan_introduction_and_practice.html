M.E2.2. Zen / Chan — Introduction and Practice

The most famous lineage of East Asian Mahāyāna is Zen — the school placing meditation itself at the path's centre. 1. What is Zen — names and origins

Zen (Chinese: Chán; Japanese: Zen) is the Mahāyāna school placing direct experience of mind at the centre — "Chán / Zen" is itself the transcription of jhāna / dhyāna (article M.C1.4) through Chinese. Origins: formed in China around the 6th–7th centuries, taking Bodhidharma — the Indian meditation master arriving in China around the 5th–6th centuries — as the first patriarch, and flourishing through successive Chinese patriarchs.

Zen's essence is summarized in this lineage's famous declaration: "no establishment of words, directly pointing at the human mind, seeing nature becoming Buddha" — Zen does not place scriptural study at the centre, but aims at directly viewing the mind's nature. This does not mean abandoning scriptures — Zen uses sūtras as "a finger pointing at the moon" (the finger is not the moon) — but the emphasis lies on practice under a teacher's guidance more than accumulating knowledge. The Vietnam connection: the Vinitaruci (6th century) and Vô Ngôn Thông (8th century) Zen schools are Vietnam's two earliest Zen lineages — the historical predecessors of the Trúc Lâm lineage met in group F1.

2. A map of practice forms

Zen is not only "sitting" — one day's life in a meditation hall is a continuous practice chain, each form one character:

  • Seated meditation (Zazen — section 4): the central practice — sitting in alertness;
  • Walking meditation (Japanese: kinhin): walking meditation between sittings — slow, rhythmic, keeping continuous alertness between two sittings (compare Theravāda walking meditation — article M.B6);
  • Work practice (Japanese: samu): cleaning, cooking, gardening — Zen regards daily work as meditation in the true sense; Baizhang's famous saying (Chinese master): "a day without work, a day without eating";
  • Ritual and chanting: morning-evening chanting, offering rites — practised in alertness, unseparated from meditation;
  • Teacher – student interviews: meeting teachers privately to present experience and receive confirmation – adjustment — a form with a distinctive role (in detail with kōans in article M.E2.5).

How to read this map: Zen presents meditation as life, not a separate activity — sitting, walking, working, and chanting are all "places" of the same alertness; this organization matches article M.B6's spirit (walking meditation + daily-life mindfulness) but at a higher ritualized and communal level.

3. Two transmission lineages: Línjì / Rinzai and Cáodòng / Sōtō

Zen's two great transmission lineages surviving to today — differing in principal methods, resembling in basis (Zazen, hall life, nature-seeing purpose):

  • The Línjì lineage (Chinese: Línjì; Japanese: Rinzai): famous for kōans (Chinese: gōng'àn; Japanese: kōan) — living questions, "unsolvable" language situations used as meditation objects — and huàtóu (the kōan's focal question, as called by the Chinese and Vietnamese Línjì lineage). The method demands close teacher guidance (L5 level — only presented in article M.E2.5, unguided); the overall character: quick, sharp, sudden.
  • The Cáodòng lineage (Chinese: Cáodòng; Japanese: Sōtō): stressing sitting meditation as the complete road on the spot — the central practice is just sitting (shikantaza — article M.E2.5) with Chinese roots in silent illumination (mòzhào — "silently luminous" of Master Hongzhi). The overall character: quiet, slow, permeating.

The right comparison (true to the taxonomy's neutral spirit): the two lineages are not "competing", but two mind-training temperaments on the same basis — like two ways of sharpening two different knives; many modern meditation halls, including in Vietnam, mix forms by circumstances. For beginners, what to know is: meeting Zen books speaking of "kōans" or "just sitting" — that signals which lineage.

4. Zazen — sitting meditation in Zen

Zazen (Chinese: seated meditation) is the central form — and a practice group with many implementations, not a single technique. Basic components:

  • Posture: full / half lotus on a cushion (zafu — round cushions); unable to cross legs, kneeling (seiza) or sitting on chairs — the principle: the back naturally straight, hips higher than knees, the body steady as "a mountain face"; compare the shared posture principles — article M.A4;
  • Hands: placed in an oval — left hand inside right, two thumb tips lightly touching, resting close to the lower belly (the Sōtō lineage's common form);
  • Breathing: abdominal breathing, slow, quiet — not strongly controlled; many lineages teach breath counting to stabilize the start (counting out-breaths 1–10);
  • Eye placement: Zen's characteristic — eyes half-open, gazing down about 45 degrees ahead, neither fully closed nor fixed on objects — the spirit: alert without scattering, settled without hazing;
  • Mind: varying by lineage — Cáodòng: "just sitting" in open alertness (M.E2.5); Línjì: holding huàtóu / kōans (M.E2.5); beginning practitioners: clearly knowing breath and sitting body (group B's shared basis).

Hence the right understanding of Zazen: not a "special secret sitting style", but a posture – breathing – eyes – mind set with implementations by lineage and person; group B's skill basis (breath, releasing, handling distraction) directly applies.

5. The meditation-hall atmosphere — and summary

One part separately noted in the taxonomy: the meditation hall's atmosphere. First-time hall visitors are often surprised by the environment's efficacy:

  • Shared rhythmic tightness: everyone sitting the same hours, moving by bells — synchronization creating a very strong stillness basis one person sitting alone hardly self-creates;
  • No talking, no looking at one another: within the still frame, the mind sheds the "scanning social reactions" part — exactly the noble silence of retreats (article M.A6) presented in Zen's way;
  • Bells and wooden instruments: sound signals marking sitting – walking – eating transitions — sounds themselves becoming alertness markers (compare sound meditation — article M.C1.2);
  • Daily sequence: waking early, alternating sitting – walking meditation, eating in mindfulness, work, chanting — even short-retreat participants receive the value of a complete Zen-rhythm-life experience.

Summary:

  • Zen (Chán / Zen) is the Mahāyāna school placing direct experience of mind at the centre — "no establishment of words, directly pointing at the human mind"; Vietnam's two earliest Zen lineages (Vinitaruci, Vô Ngôn Thông) are Trúc Lâm's predecessors (group F1).
  • Practice map: seated meditation – walking meditation – work – ritual – interviews: meditation as life, not a separate activity.
  • Two transmission lineages: Línjì / Rinzai — kōans, huàtóu, quick-sharp character (in detail M.E2.5); Cáodòng / Sōtō — just sitting, silent illumination, quiet-permeating character (M.E2.5); two temperaments on one basis, unranked.
  • Zazen is a multi-implementation practice group: posture (straight back, hips higher than knees), slow abdominal breathing, half-open eyes at 45 degrees, mind by lineage — group B's skill basis directly applies.
  • Meditation-hall atmosphere (shared rhythmic tightness, silence, bells, daily sequence) is part of the method — the value of short retreats.

Glossary:

  • Zen (Chinese: Chán; Japanese: Zen): the Mahāyāna school placing meditation at the centre; "Chán / Zen" transcribes dhyāna.
  • Bodhidharma: the Indian meditation master arriving in China around the 5th–6th centuries — honoured as Chinese Zen's first patriarch.
  • Zazen (seated meditation): sitting meditation in Zen forms; zafu (round cushions) and seiza (kneeling) are posture forms.
  • Walking meditation (Japanese: kinhin): walking meditation between sittings in meditation halls.
  • Kōans (Chinese: gōng'àn; Japanese: kōan) and huàtóu: Línjì lineage methods — in detail in article E2.5.
  • Just sitting (shikantaza) and silent illumination (mòzhào): Cáodòng lineage methods — in detail in article E2.5.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
