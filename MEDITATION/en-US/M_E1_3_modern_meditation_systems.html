M.E1.3. Modern Meditation Systems in Theravāda

The previous article (article M.E1.2) presented the three core frames — the Southern tradition's shared language. This article uses that language to view modern meditation systems: why many very different systems share the name "vipassanā"; four fair comparison criteria; and a map of prominent lineages — the Burmese cluster (Ledi, Mahāsi, U Ba Khin–Goenka, Pa-Auk, U Tejaniya) and the Thai Forest Tradition. The running principle per the taxonomy: neutral presentation, unranked — each lineage described as it presents itself.

1. One name, many systems — comparing by four criteria

"Vipassanā" (Pali: vipassanā) today names one method implemented by many differing systems — article M.C2.1 already sealed this. Why this phenomenon: in the late 19th – early 20th centuries, as meditation practice strongly revived in Theravāda countries, great monastics in differing circumstances (some urban, some opening mass retreats, some in forests) each re-systematized canon – commentaries in their own way — each system a different reading on the same basis, transmitted through large retreats to become proper names in the modern meditation world.

For fair — non-judging — comparison, using the four criteria introduced in article M.E1.1: (1) principal subject — the object the system begins with; (2) use of collectedness — which collectedness level is needed before observing; (3) noting technique — whether silent labels are used, dense or loose; (4) retreat structure — schedule shape, long or short. With these four criteria, differences between lineages appear distinctly without win – lose comparison: like three cooks cooking the same dish from the same original recipe — differing preparation, differing seasoning, not differing dishes.

2. The Burmese cluster — five representative lineages

Myanmar (Burma) is where modern Theravāda meditations were born earliest and most influential — partly because the Pali canon and commentaries were continuously preserved, partly because the 20th-century "meditation-ization" movement joined schools and mass retreats.

2.1. The Ledi Sayādaw lineage

Ledi Sayādaw (1846–1923) is the movement's "theoretical starting point": he rewrote Abhidhamma summaries into short texts ordinary people could read, and presented mindfulness of breathing combined with Dhamma analysis — practice going together with distinct understanding of what one is doing. Influence: nearly all later lineages drew strength from his textual popularization; his lineage directly stresses both practice and a firm theoretical basis.

2.2. The Mahāsi Sayādaw lineage

Mahāsi Sayādaw (1904–1982) is the noting lineage — most systematized and widespread: principal subject is belly movements (rising – falling) when sitting, steps when walking; use of collectedness: momentary concentration suffices for observing (the "wisdom as vehicle" model — article M.C1.5); noting technique: dense silent noting — continuously placing short labels on all phenomena (the technique presented through this lineage in article M.B4); retreats: dense, silent schedules, two months for dedicated practitioners — 8–10 days for the public. This lineage directly influenced Western "mindfulness" courses through students.

2.3. The U Ba Khin – S. N. Goenka lineage

U Ba Khin (1899–1971) — an official and the first meditation teacher opening a meditation centre in Myanmar's government environment; student S. N. Goenka (1924–2013) brought this lineage to the world through standardized ten-day retreats. Characteristics by four criteria: principal subject: breath in the early phase (anāpāna) then shifting to whole-body sensation scanning (near the body scanning trained in group B); use of collectedness: a momentary-concentration basis on the breath; noting: no labels — directly sensing sensations, with stressing mental evenness (equanimity — article M.D2.4) toward all sensations; retreats: 10 days of absolute silence, fixed schedules, no fees — the most familiar model for Vietnamese today through vipassanā centres.

2.4. The Pa-Auk Sayādaw lineage

Pa-Auk Sayādaw (born 1934) fully keeps the commentarial deep-collectedness road: principal subject: the breath until the meditative sign (nimitta) appears, then jhāna — expandable to other subjects (kasiṇas, four elements); use of collectedness: "concentration as vehicle" — full collectedness first, observing after; noting: few labels, weighting distinctly knowing collectedness states; retreats: long — practitioners staying in monasteries many months, gradually increasing subject by subject. This lineage represents the tradition's "commentarial-faithful" reading.

2.5. The U Tejaniya direction

U Tejaniya (born 1962) — not a "new system" but a mind-observing direction with right practice attitude: principal subject: the very mind and attitude present while practising ("with what attitude is the mind knowing?"); use of collectedness: loose — collectedness as consequence of continuous observation, not a braced goal; noting: light — not by label rhythm, weighting test questions; retreats: unstandardized — encouraging regular practice in daily life. This lineage is especially near the app's group B articles (especially article M.B3 — practice attitude).

3. The Thai Forest Tradition

The Forest Tradition (Pali: araññavāsa) is the school of monastics living and training in deep forests, caves, cemeteries — the lineage continuing the Buddha's time "entering natural silence" thought, revived by Ajahn Mun (1870–1949) and spread to the world by Ajahn Chah (1918–1992) through Western monasteries. Characteristics by criteria:

  • Basis — ethics and lifestyle: unlike "retreat" lineages, the Forest Tradition regards ethical conduct, simplicity, deportment as inseparable parts of meditation — collectedness and wisdom sprouting on right life (exactly the three-trainings spirit — article M.A1);
  • Subjects: flexible per person — the lineage's classics are "Buddho" (the "Buddha" recitation word — used as a mind-holding subject), the breath, or direct body – mind observation; Ajahn Chah famously taught through life circumstances (house and temple work) instead of closed techniques;
  • Collectedness – wisdom: naturally in harness — not separating two links (the yuganaddha model — article M.E1.2);
  • Teaching way: through direct teachers more than curricula — hence this lineage's "system" lies in monasteries' shared life more than books.

Buddhadāsa Bhikkhu (1906–1993) — not of the Forest lineage but contemporaneous — presented the sixteen steps of mindfulness of breathing (MN 118) in his own way: four groups of four concisely explained, followable by ordinary people; this presentation influences many current Vietnamese meditation materials.

4. Which lineage to choose — and summary

  • Fair comparison: four criteria (principal subject, use of collectedness, noting technique, retreat structure) show lineages differing in departure points and techniques, not destination — all lineages aim at liberation per the Southern teaching.
  • Teachers' role: lineages all live through direct transmission — choosing a lineage is choosing persons and community more than curricula; the "one teacher – one lineage – one time" principle (article M.A6) is how to protect oneself from mixing and wrong self-conclusions (article M.C2.5).
  • Neutrality: this article describes, unranked; each lineage has those fitting and those unfitting — fitting – unfitting is practitioners' conditional matter, not lineages' quality.

Summary: the five Burmese lineages + the Thai Forest Tradition are all modern readings on the same canonical – commentarial basis; sharing the vipassanā name because sharing wisdom-aiming — differing in four criteria; and this diversity is the tradition's asset: differing practitioners finding differing roads.

Glossary:

  • Sayādaw: the "great teacher" title in Myanmar — one heading a meditation or monastic centre.
  • Ajahn: the "teacher" title in Thai.
  • Buddho: the "Buddha" recitation word used as a mind-holding subject in the Forest Tradition — sounded with the breath or silently repeated.
  • Forest Tradition (Pali: araññavāsa): the school of monastics training in forests — revived by Ajahn Mun, spread through Ajahn Chah.
  • Anāpāna: the short name of ānāpānassati (mindfulness of breathing) — used in the U Ba Khin–Goenka lineage for the early phase.
  • Mental evenness: equanimity (upekkhā) in practice — neither welcoming nor chasing away all sensations (article M.D2.4).
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
