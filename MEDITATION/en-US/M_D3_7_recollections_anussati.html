M.D3.7. Recollections (Anussati) — The Ten "Remembering" Subjects

The 40 system's final subject group is the recollections — ten "remembering"-form subjects: Buddha, Dhamma, Saṅgha, ethics, generosity, deities, death, body, breath, peace. Unlike deep-collectedness groups (kasiṇas) or countering groups (foulness), recollections work through timely remembering: recalling known things to nourish confidence, calm, and practice inspiration — simultaneously the basis for collectedness. This is also the group closest to Vietnamese Buddhists' lives: buddha-recitation, repentance, and gratitude all root here. This article merges the whole group: the ten-subject map, each recollection in turn, use, and safety warnings.

1. What are recollections — the ten-subject map

Recollection (Pali: anussati, Sanskrit: anusmṛti) is the practice of actively bringing a wholesome theme into mind — recalling, holding the mind on it a while, letting that theme's material permeate mood. The canon speaks of the first six recollections: those familiar in training them have calm minds, fears settled, confusions ended. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 6.10 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

Ten subjects per the commentarial listing (Visuddhimagga ch. VII):

  • 1–3. Buddha, Dhamma, Saṅgha recollections — recalling the Three Jewels (section 2);
  • 4–6. Ethics, generosity, deities recollections — recalling one's own virtues and wholesome destinations (section 3);
  • 7. Death recollection — recalling life's finitude (section 4);
  • 8. Body recollection — mindfulness immersed in the body (article M.D1.1);
  • 9. Breath recollection — mindfulness of breathing;
  • 10. Peace recollection — recalling stilling, nirvana (section 6).

This group's role per the commentarial system: nourishing confidence, calm, and inspiration — three things long-term practitioners all need; in collectedness, eight recollections (except body and breath) lead to access concentration — a usable level for insight meditation (article overview section 3). In the temperament classification, recollections are subjects suggested for faith-natured persons — minds easily trusting and wholesomely moved.

2. Buddha, Dhamma, Saṅgha recollections — the Three-Jewel recollections

2.1. Recollection of the Buddha (Buddhānussati / Buddhānusmṛti)

Recollection of the Buddha (Pali: buddhānussati, Sanskrit: buddhānusmṛti) is contemplating the Buddha's qualities, image, or name. In Theravāda, the principal form is recollecting virtues: memorizing and reflecting on the nine-quality formula — "That Blessed One is an arahant, perfectly awakened, accomplished in knowledge and conduct, well-gone, knower of worlds, unsurpassed trainer of persons to be tamed, teacher of deities and humans, awakened, blessed" — each quality in turn, clearly seeing meaning, holding the mind a while. The difference from East Asian Mahāyāna needs stating plainly (exactly the taxonomy's requirement): name recitation in the Pure Land tradition (article M.E2.3) — reciting "Namo Amitābha Buddha" as the principal practice for rebirth in the Pure Land — is a separate system with its own doctrine; resembling in material (holding the mind on the Buddha), differing in purpose and path structure. Practitioners reading both presentations should not mix: virtue recollection is confidence–calm-nourishing recollection; Pure Land name recitation is one tradition's complete path.

2.2. Recollection of the Dhamma (Dhammānussati / Dharmānusmṛti)

Recollection of the Dhamma (Pali: dhammānussati, Sanskrit: dharmānusmṛti) is contemplating the teaching's qualities — the six words the discourses use: well-taught (svākkhāta), visible here and now (sandiṭṭhika), timeless (akālika), inviting to come and see (ehipassika), worth following (opāneyika), the wise personally realizing (paccattaṃ veditabbo). Practice is not only memorizing six words — but checking back through one's own experience: "have I seen this teaching here in life? — yes, where?" Recollection of the Dhamma thus strengthens practice orientation: the teaching is not to be believed, but being verified (joining the four-noble-truths spirit to the cushion — article M.D1.4).

2.3. Recollection of the Saṅgha (Saṅghānussati / Saṃghānusmṛti)

Recollection of the Saṅgha (Pali: saṅghānussati, Sanskrit: saṃghānusmṛti) is contemplating the practice community and predecessors' qualities — the discourses summarize in words "practising rightly, practising straight, practising by the teaching, practising skilfully". Practice: recalling monastics, teachers, and fellow practitioners met — seeing the path has predecessors and co-travellers. This is the recollection directly countering the walking-alone feeling — nourishing what good friends (article M.A6) give in daily life.

3. Ethics, generosity, deities recollections — three life-near recollections

These three recollections are rarely mentioned in Western books yet very near Vietnamese Buddhists' lives:

  • Recollection of virtue (Pali: sīlānussati): recalling one's own ethical conduct — precepts kept, good things not done. This is not self-praise: remembering a truth about oneself to regain self-respect and a remorseless mind — exactly the support article M.A1 presented (ethics as collectedness's basis: a remorseless mind settles — the AN 11.2 chain). Evenings before sitting, reviewing: "today what did I keep?" — the mind lightens, sittings differ entirely.
  • Recollection of generosity (Pali: cāgānussati): recalling one's own giving – sharing acts — large and small: money, time, strength, tolerance, an easy-to-hear word. Generosity recollection gives the mind a "one who can give" feeling — directly countering hoarding and the poor feeling amid sufficiency; this is also naturally nourishing material for loving-kindness – compassion.
  • Recollection of deities (Pali: devatānussati): contemplating qualities leading to wholesome destinations — the confidence, ethics, learning, generosity, and wisdom deities possess. The practical understanding (true to the canonical spirit): deities are those with virtues higher than humans at a heavenly level — remembering them remembers that confidence, ethics, and giving lead to higher happiness, not a luck-seeking spell. Those not using the cosmological frame, the previous two recollections (ethics, generosity) already supply the practice part.

4. Death recollection — and safety warnings

Mindfulness of death (Pali: maraṇassati, Sanskrit: maraṇasmṛti) is contemplating life's finitude — aiming to raise alertness and reorder what matters, not to nourish fear. The canon advises: those knowing how to train death mindfulness live alertly, undelaying, like those with burning heads on fire not playing. [Source: Maraṇassati Sutta, Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 6.19 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

The gentle practice (entirely unlike foulness contemplation): no detailed images needed — only one straight recognition: "this life is finite; its business has order — what genuinely matters?" Most practical is letting it work outside sittings: before busy days — "if today were the last, which business deserves time most?"; before conflicts — "is this anger, against the time I have, worth it?" Rightly used death mindfulness is a priority filter — making people live denser, not more panicked.

Mandatory safety warnings: if contemplating death causes prolonged anxiety — insomnia, un-self-switching images, clinging persistent fear — stopping this subject, back to gentle subjects (breath, loving-kindness), and those with death anxiety or depression should not use this recollection (joining article M.A3's warnings); using the discourses' tool to cure fear reverses the discourses' purpose — the discourses give it for alert living, not fearful living.

5. Body and breath recollections — cross-links

The list's final two recollections are not re-presented here because each has full separate articles: body recollection — mindfulness immersed in the body, including the 32-part contemplation — and breath recollection — the central subject of groups B–C, with the sixteen-step discourse (the app's advanced L4 article). Restating here only to keep the ten-subject map complete: within the 40 system, these two recollections are two distinctive subjects leading to deep collectedness like kasiṇas — hence both belonging to the recollection group and standing as the jhāna road's principal subjects.

6. Peace recollection

Recollection of peace (Pali: upasamānussati) is contemplating stilling — with nirvana as the path's goal: recalling that "there is complete stilling — the utter ending of thirst, hatred, and delusion" — as the canon affirms: "there is the unborn, unbecome, unmade... if there were not that, there would be no escape here". [Source: Udāna, Ud 8.3 - Theravāda Buddhism.] This subject leans toward thinking – contemplating rather than attention technique: practice at educational-map level — short reflections on the destination, especially when the mind tires and desponds mid-way: "this road has a destination; the destination is stilling" — re-nourishing motive. Detailed doctrinal points on nirvana belong to advanced articles (article M.C2.5 already touched at the wisdom level); here only keeping recollection's exact function: recalling the destination.

7. Using the recollection group: when, at which level

  • When: (1) dry minds, lost motive — Three-Jewel recollections (section 2) are the fastest "refuelling"; (2) hurt self-love, remorseful minds — ethics and generosity recollections (section 3); (3) walking-alone feelings — Saṅgha recollection; (4) drifting lives, tangled priorities — death recollection at priority-filtering level; (5) mid-way fatigue — peace recollection.
  • Shared practice: choosing one theme, using exactly its material (names – sentences – concrete events), holding the mind a few minutes, letting feelings permeate — then back to the breath to close. Recollections demand not length — demanding timeliness and genuineness.
  • Collectedness level: eight recollections (except body, breath) lead to access concentration — collectedness usable for insight meditation; those wanting deeper collectedness switch to jhāna-leading subjects (breath, kasiṇas).
  • Long-term role: recollections are the "below-sitting-hour" practice layer — nourishing confidence and calm for entire lives; naturally combining with daily mindfulness markers and journaling (noting "today what recall helped the mind?").

8. Summary and next steps

  • Recollection (anussati) is ten "remembering" subjects: Buddha, Dhamma, Saṅgha, ethics, generosity, deities, death, body, breath, peace — nourishing confidence, calm, inspiration; eight recollections lead to access concentration.
  • Practitioners use by need: lost motive → Three Jewels; remorse → ethics – generosity; walking alone → Saṅgha; drifting → death (priority filtering); mid-way fatigue → peace.
  • Buddha recollection in Theravāda is virtue recollection — distinctly distinguished from Pure Land name-recitation (article M.E2.3); death recollection for alertness not fear — stopping at once when causing prolonged anxiety.
  • The recollection group closes the forty-subject map: traversed groups — kasiṇas, foulness, body-contemplation subjects (32 parts, four elements), food, divine abodes, recollections — together forming a system distinctly classified by collectedness level and character.

Glossary:

  • Recollection (Pali: anussati, Sanskrit: anusmṛti): the practice of actively bringing a wholesome theme into mind to nourish confidence, calm, and collectedness.
  • Recollection of the Buddha (Pali: buddhānussati): recollection of the Buddha — in Theravāda principally the nine virtues; distinguished from Pure Land name-recitation.
  • Recollection of the Dhamma (Pali: dhammānussati): recollection of the teaching's six qualities.
  • Recollection of the Saṅgha (Pali: saṅghānussati): recollection of the practice community and predecessors; Saṅgha introduced in article A6.
  • Recollection of ethics / generosity / deities (Pali: sīlānussati / cāgānussati / devatānussati): three recollections recalling one's own ethics, one's own giving acts, and qualities leading to wholesome destinations.
  • Mindfulness of death (Pali: maraṇassati, Sanskrit: maraṇasmṛti): recollection of life's finitude — for alertness and prioritizing, not for fear.
  • Recollection of peace (Pali: upasamānussati): recollection of stilling — with nirvana as goal; a thinking – contemplating-leaning subject.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
