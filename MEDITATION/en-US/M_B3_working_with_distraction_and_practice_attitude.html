M.B3. Working with Distraction and Practice Attitude

The previous article set the rhythm of the central loop of practice: recognize — release gently — return. This article examines the two ends of the loop closely. The first end is what the mind tends to stick to: thought and distraction — the skill of recognizing them without fighting their content. The second end is the attitude of the one repeating the loop: balanced effort, neither forcing nor drifting — what determines whether you still sit down on the cushion tomorrow. For beginners, this is the most everyday work: a wandering mind is not a malfunction to fix, but the natural character of an untrained mind.

1. Noticing thought and distraction

1.1. Thought as phenomenon, not enemy

Eyes just closed, the "meeting room in the head" convenes at once: unfinished business, a remark that should have been answered differently, an order not yet placed. Beginners often conclude "I cannot meditate". But thoughts arising is as natural as dust flying when wind blows — that is the mind's business. The first step of this skill is not extinguishing, but only seeing it: shifting from being submerged in thought to clearly knowing a thought is present.

The Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta — the root discourse of the four establishments of mindfulness (Pali: satipaṭṭhāna) — devotes a whole domain to this: contemplation of mind (Pali: cittānupassanā). The discourse lists each pair: "a mind with greed, one clearly knows a mind with greed; a mind without greed, one clearly knows a mind without greed; a mind with hatred, one clearly knows a mind with hatred; a contracted mind, one clearly knows a contracted mind; a scattered mind, one clearly knows a scattered mind...". [Source: Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 10 - Theravāda Buddhism.] Beginners need not memorize the whole list — the point is the move from "I am anxious" to "an anxiety is present". Only the phrasing differs, but the feeling is entirely different: the first is locked inside, the second stands beside and looks.

1.2. Recognizing familiar kinds of distraction

Distraction is not one thing but many "flavours". Giving each kind a short label helps recognition next time:

  • Problem-solving thought: "thinking" (plans, predictions, deliberations).
  • Memory – recollection: "remembering" (old stories, a conversation just replayed).
  • Hearing: "hearing" (sounds out there pulling attention away).
  • Imagining: "imagining" (images, lights, scenes appearing — recall the earlier article: this is not attainment, only a phenomenon).
  • Drowsiness – dullness: "drowsy" (hazy mind, nodding head).
  • Restlessness – wanting to get up: "restless" (discomfort, wanting to shift posture, wanting to do something else).

A label need only be short, silent, and not become a story. "Thinking" is enough; "thinking about what I should have said yesterday so now..." — that is another thought, needing one more label. The full labelling technique will be presented separately in group M.B4 ("Noting / silent labeling"); here only the natural level above is needed.

1.3. Why the command "don't think" fails

There is a paradox everyone meets: the harder you try not to think of something, the more vividly it appears. The prohibition itself is a thought — meaning one more log added to the fire, then surprise that the fire still burns. The right way with a thought: allow it to be present, see it clearly, and do not follow it. Deprived of attention, a thought runs out of fuel after a few seconds — because it lives on our attention and reaction.

The canon devotes an entire specialized discourse to distracting thoughts: the Vitakkasaṇṭhāna Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 20 - Theravāda Buddhism, teaching five ways to lead the mind away from distracting thoughts — from the subtlest (passing over, paying no attention, like closing the eyes to ignore a crowd) to more active ways when thoughts are too strong. Practical handling of each situation is already in "During and After a Sitting" (M.A5), while the theoretical framework of the five obstructing states — the five hindrances (Pali: pañca nīvaraṇa) — has its own article in the app. This article keeps to its part: recognizing — clearly seeing what is happening before speaking of handling.

1.4. The real value: the pause between seeing and following

Recognizing thought matters far beyond keeping a sitting "tidy". Between a thought appearing and my following it there is always a gap — tiny as a lightning flash, but real. Recognition is the tool that widens that gap. While sitting: seeing "thinking about this afternoon's meeting" without following → attention returns by itself to the breath. In daily life: seeing "irritation" as an argument heats up → a gap in which to choose gentler words. The same skill, used everywhere — which is why this article group belongs to "Shared Practical Skills" and not to any single method.

2. The attitude of practice: balanced effort, neither forcing nor drifting

2.1. The lute-string story

The canon records a very human story. The bhikkhu Soṇa — of noble family, soft white hands, never having known hardship — strove in walking meditation until the skin of his feet split, blood staining his seat, yet the mind still came to nothing. Discouraged, he thought of leaving the robe for lay life. The Buddha, knowing this, came near and asked: back in lay life, you were a skilled lute player; with strings too tight, how does the sound come? With strings too loose, how? "Too tight or too loose, no sound comes; just right, the finest sound." The Buddha concluded: too forceful effort leads to restlessness; too slack effort leads to laziness; keep it just right. [Source: Soṇa Sutta, Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 6.55 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

The word being balanced here is effort (Pali: viriya) — one of the mental qualities to nurture, but at the right dose. The lute-string image is memorable because it negates both errors at once: rigid forcing and limp letting-go.

2.2. Recognizing "too tight"

Too tight rarely names itself; it shows through signals:

  • Body: chest tight, brow furrowed, jaw clenched, shoulders carried up — exactly the areas "Releasing bodily tension" in article M.B1 mentioned.
  • Mind: miscounting then blaming yourself; finishing a sitting by grading yourself "bad"; every sitting having to "produce results" — hunting for a calm feeling to prove to yourself.
  • Trajectory: restlessness rising the longer you sit — because strain breeds heat, heat breeds boredom.

The remedy: lower expectations for this sitting alone ("today's goal is only to be fully present"); a few extended breaths; and treating each recognition-and-return as one complete round, not a fault needing compensation.

2.3. Recognizing "too loose"

Too loose is subtler, because it borrows the language of "letting go":

  • Sitting "blank": body sitting there, mind daydreaming as if watching a film through frosted glass.
  • The count drifting — not remembering what number you are on, and not minding either.
  • "Letting things be natural" gradually becoming permission for everything to carry you away, until at the sitting's end you do not know where you were for twenty minutes.

The remedy: straighten the back a little more; open the eyes slightly if drowsy; raise the clarity of "knowing" — not straining harder, but finer: "breathing in" truly clearly known as "breathing in". Or switch to a few minutes of walking meditation to regain alertness.

2.4. Friendliness toward yourself

Finally, the most sustainable attitude is not iron discipline, but friendliness toward yourself. The Karaṇīyamettā Sutta (Sn 1.8, Sutta Nipāta) - Theravāda Buddhism teaches radiating love like a mother sheltering her only child toward all beings — and the first being to receive that love is the one sitting. Three concrete gestures:

  • Speak to yourself as to a close fellow practitioner: "ah, noticed — let's return", instead of "wandered again, hopeless".
  • Acknowledge effort, do not grade results: sitting down on time is already doing your part; the rest is time's work.
  • Count what is worth counting: each recognition of distraction is one moment of genuinely vivid mindfulness — a "busy" sitting with dozens of successful recognitions succeeds far more than a seemingly smooth sitting in which nothing was known at all.

Friendliness is not laxity — a good friend neither scolds nor pretends not to see you late. Exactly like the lute string: neither tight nor loose.

3. Summary and next steps

  • Noticing thought and distraction: thought is phenomenon, not enemy; place short labels ("thinking", "remembering", "hearing"...); the command "don't think" only adds fuel; the greatest value is the pause between seeing and following — usable both in the sitting hour and in daily life.
  • Practice attitude: effort like a lute string — too tight breeds restlessness, too loose breeds laziness; learn to recognize the two extremes through bodily signals and the end-of-sitting "results"; and nurture friendliness toward yourself — each recognition is one win.
  • These two skills prepare for finer observation: only when no longer fighting experience can experience be clearly seen.

Glossary:

  • Contemplation of mind (Pali: cittānupassanā): the third domain of the four establishments of mindfulness — clearly knowing the mental state present (with or without greed, with or without hatred, contracted or scattered...) at the very moment it exists.
  • Effort (Pali: viriya): the capacity of exertion and diligence of the mind; it needs balancing — too strong breeds restlessness, too slack breeds laziness.
  • Five hindrances (Pali: pañca nīvaraṇa): the five states obstructing collectedness and wisdom — sensual desire, ill will, dullness and drowsiness, restlessness and remorse, doubt; presented separately in one article of the app.
  • Four establishments of mindfulness (Pali: satipaṭṭhāna): the four domains of mindfulness listed in the canon: body, feelings, mind, dhammas.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
