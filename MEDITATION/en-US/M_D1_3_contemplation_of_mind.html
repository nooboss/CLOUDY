M.D1.3. Contemplation of Mind (Cittānupassanā)

After body and feeling, this article enters the third domain of the four establishments of mindfulness (Pali: satipaṭṭhāna): contemplation of mind. This is a domain "one floor up" from feeling contemplation: no longer stopping at the pleasant – unpleasant tone of experience, but looking directly at the state of the knowing itself present. Contents: what mind contemplation is and how it differs from feeling contemplation, the pairs of mental states the discourses list, the right view when observing, why this is a deeper training level, and how to begin.

1. What is contemplation of mind — and how does it differ from feeling contemplation?

Contemplation of mind (Pali: cittānupassanā) is placing mindfulness (Pali: sati) on the mental state present within the four-establishments frame. The discourse image is very simple: "a mind with greed, one clearly knows 'a mind with greed'; a mind without greed, one clearly knows 'a mind without greed'..." — the discourses do not say "I have greed" but "a mind with greed": a phenomenon present, clearly known. [Source: Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 10 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

The difference from feeling contemplation lies in the object:

  • Feeling is the "tone" accompanying each experience — pleasant, unpleasant, neutral; it colours what is known.
  • Mind is the knowing itself with its overall state — with or without greed, contracted or scattered, concentrated or not.

An example showing the two layers: aching legs (body) → unpleasant sensation (feeling) → tense, irritated mind wanting to get up (mind). The three layers occur almost simultaneously, yet each is a separate event — and this article trains the third layer. On the cushion, this distinction is very practical: with the same aching legs, sometimes the mind stays soft and steady, sometimes the mind has already knotted — different in mind, not different in pain, and seeing that is already contemplation of mind.

2. The pairs of mental states the discourses list

The Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta does not teach "observing the mind generally" but gives a list of opposite pairs — clearly knowing this side, also clearly knowing the other. The Theravāda canon lists eight pairs (sixteen states); the six pairs most often mentioned are:

  • With greed (Pali: rāga) / without greed: the mind pulling toward a wanted object, or not pulling.
  • With hatred (Pali: dosa) / without hatred: the mind pushing away, hardening before an object, or not pushing.
  • With delusion (Pali: moha) / without delusion: the mind hazy, unclear about what is happening — the hardest pair to know, because the very haze also covers knowing.
  • Contracted (Pali: saṅkhitta) / scattered (Pali: vikkhitta): two extremes of the mind's "openness" — clumped, sluggish, drowsy on one side; drifting, jumping everywhere on the other (exactly sloth-and-torpor and restlessness among the five hindrances — articles M.C1.3, M.B3).
  • Exalted (Pali: mahaggata) / unexalted: a broad, lofty mind with breathing room — often when working with the subject at a collected level — against a mind locked in a small affair.
  • Concentrated (Pali: samāhita) / unconcentrated: the mind gathered, firm on the subject, or not yet.

And the final pair — deepest: liberated (Pali: vimutta) / unliberated. To understand rightly: the discourses speak of the temporary level too — the mind released from one particular defilement in the moment, when it no longer grips the mind — not only the radical level. Meeting this pair in sittings, the work remains only clearly knowing, never self-concluding "I am liberated" — article M.C2.5 already warned of the self-attributed-attainment danger.

Two points worth noting about this list. First, the discourses require clearly knowing the "without" side too — mind without greed, hatred, delusion — that is, wholesome states too. Beginners usually hunt only their own faults; the discourses' list corrects: observing the mind is seeing rightly both directions, not a fault-sweep for self-blame. Second, the canon elsewhere adds: the mind is by nature luminous, defilements only visiting guests polluting then leaving — so understood, each state in the above list is only a guest's visit, not the mind's permanent nature. [Source: Aṅguttara Nikāya, AN 1.49–52 - Theravāda Buddhism.]

3. How to view when observing: clearly knowing, not evaluating

The shared template of all four establishments — "bare knowing, just enough", not relying, not clinging (article M.D1) — applied to mind contemplation becomes three concrete principles:

  • Names are not seals. The label "a mind with greed" describes the state present, not a verdict on character. Saying "the mind is greedy" differs entirely from saying "I am a greedy person" — the distance between those two sentences is exactly the doorway to not-self (Pali: anattā) which insight meditation trains through observing, seen here right on the mind.
  • States come then go. True to arising – passing nature: today's irritation was absent yesterday, nor will it stay the same next week. Repeated observation gradually teaches the mind: no need to believe every state, nor to chase it away.
  • Evaluating helps nothing. "Why is my mind so scattered!" is only another state (unpleasantness) piled on the old. The discourses require only clearly knowing; commentary is added by us — and the added part is itself observable.

The directly supporting skill is article M.B4's noting / silent labeling: short labels — "greed", "anger", "haziness", "scattered", "broad", "settled" — placed in rhythm, then released. Labels only touch the state; words do not replace knowing.

4. Why contemplation of mind is a deeper training level

The taxonomy places feeling contemplation at L2 and mind contemplation at L3, because its object is thinner and faster:

  • Feeling has "location" on the body (aching legs in the legs, softness in the chest) — easy to hold. Mind has no such location; it must be known by mind itself — in other words, this is mind mirroring mind, easily slipping into imagining without a firm mindfulness foundation.
  • Mental states shift without warning — one sentence in the head and the mind has already switched from settled to scattered. Catching the switching moment is an advanced skill; fortunately, the discourses do not demand catching it at once.
  • But the reward is worthy: mind contemplation looks straight at the source of reactions — before becoming words and deeds. On the dependent-origination chain, this is the decisive-impact layer; that is also why modern insight systems keep mind among principal objects (article M.C2.2).

Hence the recommended order remains as the discourses present: firm body first, distinct feeling next, then mind contemplation has foundation — not skipping.

5. How to begin a mind-contemplation sitting

  • Step 1 — foundation: a few minutes of breath as subject for the mind to stand on.
  • Step 2 — the key question: releasing attention from the object, silently asking: "what state is the mind in now?" — not rushing to answer in words; waiting a few seconds for the state to show itself.
  • Step 3 — labelling: choosing the closest short word from the discourses' list — "greed", "anger", "scattered", "contracted", "broad", "settled" — then releasing the label, continuing to know.
  • Step 4 — noticing the counterpart: when a difficult state is present (for example scattering), gently remembering the other side also exists in oneself — the mind is sometimes unscattered. So remembering counters negatively self-labelling.
  • Step 5 — when difficult: states too strong (panic, stuckness), use safety tools: back to breathing, grounding, opening the eyes, lowering intensity (article M.A3) — mind contemplation is not a battle.
  • Outside sittings: attaching to daily mindfulness markers one question: "what mode is the mind in?" — noticing states earlier and sticking in them shorter is itself a reliable sign of progress (article M.B7).

6. Summary and next steps

  • Contemplation of mind is clearly knowing the state of the knowing itself — "a mind with greed, one clearly knows a mind with greed" — differing from feeling contemplation in that the object is no longer tone but the mind's overall state.
  • The discourses list opposite pairs (with / without greed, hatred, delusion, contracted / scattered, exalted / unexalted, concentrated / unconcentrated, liberated / unliberated...) — clearly knowing both sides, not only hunting one's own faults.
  • Clearly knowing, not evaluating: labels are descriptions, not verdicts; states come then go; this is a direct doorway to not-self.
  • Mind contemplation is deeper than feeling contemplation because its object is thinner — needing a firm body – feeling foundation first, and rightly using safety tools when overloaded.

Glossary:

  • Contemplation of mind (Pali: cittānupassanā): the third domain of the four establishments — clearly knowing the mental state present.
  • Greed (Pali: rāga): the pull drawing the mind toward a wanted object; distinguished from craving (taṇhā) in that rāga points to the presently stained mental state.
  • Hatred (Pali: dosa): the mental state hardening, pushing away, wanting to demolish the object.
  • Delusion (Pali: moha): the hazy state, unclear about what is happening; rooted in ignorance.
  • Exalted (Pali: mahaggata): a broad, lofty mind with breathing room — often appearing when working with the subject at a collected level.
  • Luminous mind (AN 1.49–52): the discourse image — the mind by nature bright and clear, defilements like visiting guests; used to avoid fixing negative labels on one's own mind.
  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
