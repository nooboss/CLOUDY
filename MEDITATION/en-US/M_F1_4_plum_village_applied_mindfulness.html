M.F1.4. The Plum Village Tradition — Applied Mindfulness

After two articles on Trúc Lâm Zen, this article introduces another meditation presentation also Vietnamese-rooted and widely known in the world: Plum Village — characterized by applied mindfulness: bringing mindfulness into all everyday activities, from eating and walking to speaking and listening. The article also answers a practical question: where does this presentation stand — between traditional Buddhism and secular mindfulness?

1. What is Plum Village?

Plum Village is the name of the practice community co-founded by Master Thích Nhất Hạnh (1926–2022) in 1982 in southern France — named for the Dordogne plum village where the community placed its first headquarters. Before that, Master Thích Nhất Hạnh was already famous for Engaged Buddhism — the concept he initiated in Vietnam in the 1960s: meditation practice and social action are not two separate tasks; practitioners do not stand outside their society's suffering.

Traditionally, this presentation belongs to Zen (the Línjì lineage) with a Mahāyāna doctrinal basis; its distinction is a very simply rewritten guidance layer: few terms, many concrete practices, and special attention to lay practitioners — including children. The community's familiar closing sentence summarizes the spirit: "there is no such thing as separate meditation — meditation is how one lives". Plum Village-style practice groups now exist in many places worldwide, including in Vietnam joined to Từ Hiếu Temple (Huế) — the temple where the Master received full ordination.

2. Distinctive forms

Plum Village's presentation keeps Zen's basis practices (sitting meditation, walking meditation, chanting) adding a set of "small tools" for everyday life. The four most-mentioned forms:

  • Slow walking meditation: walking step by step, each step one breath, as if "where I stand is the most pleasant place on earth". Unlike many walking-meditation styles demanding fixed step rhythms, this way does not count steps — slow and distinct suffices, hence children can do it too.
  • Eating meditation: eating slowly, in silence or very little conversation; fully seeing food — each cake bite resulting from soil, sun and rain, and many people's labour. This is the intuitive way of teaching interbeing — the term created by Master Thích Nhất Hạnh: all things "inter-are" in one another, nothing standing separately.
  • Mindfulness bells: when bells ring, whoever doing whatever stops, breathes three breaths, then continues. At home, bells may be replaced by phone chimes or boiling-water sounds — any sounds, when heard, becoming reminders to stop and return. This is exactly "mindfulness markers" installed into long days (the spirit already trained in group B).
  • Verses (Sanskrit: gāthā): short few-line sentences for quiet recitation joined to each activity — waking up, brushing teeth, serving rice, stepping through doors. For example the waking verse means: "waking up, I smile — there is a new twenty-four-hour day to live". Verses place right intentions into each daily moment — the same spirit as article B6.

Across all forms, the "stopping — breathing — dwelling" process is most shared: stopping what one does, following a few breaths, then continuing — readers will recognize this is the same basis skill trained from group B articles, "packaged" by Plum Village into easily remembered forms.

3. Practice in daily activities and in relationships

Plum Village's most prominent point against many other presentations: practice does not stop at individuals sitting still, but spreads into relations with others — the hardest place:

  • Loving speech and deep listening: training to speak so as not to hurt others; training to listen to help others lighten suffering rather than rebut. In retreats, there are "healing" sessions: listeners keeping exactly the listening role, not interrupting, not reacting — because deeply enough listening is itself a remedy.
  • Responding to strong emotions: instead of swallowing or exploding, using the two-breath formula: "breathing in — I know I am anxious; breathing out — I know anxiety is not all I have". The familiar image is waves and water: angry waves and sad waves are still water — the vibrating mind is still mind itself; recognizing that, no need to chase waves away (the same "thoughts and feelings are not self" spirit in article M.C2.2).
  • Shared daily rhythm: Plum Village-style mindfulness days or retreats have simple schedules — waking early, sitting and walking meditation, slow eating, mindful work, evening listening-sharing sessions. Children fully participate with their own forms: bells, verses, walking meditation — hence this model especially suits families with small children.
  • Five Mindfulness Trainings: the modern, positive presentation of the traditional five precepts (article M.A1) — each describing meaningful ways of living (avoiding destroying life, taking what is not given, hurtful behaviour, lying, using mind-clouding substances) rather than stopping at "not doing that". This is how this lineage keeps the three-trainings' ethics part.

4. Between Buddhism and secular mindfulness

The most practical question for Vietnamese: is Plum Village "temple meditation" or "secular meditation"? The answer: it stands between — the famous bridge between both sides:

  • On basis: this is complete Buddhism — temples, monastics, chanting sūtras, precepts, and schools. Attending one mindfulness day, traditional elements are distinctly present.
  • On presentation: guidance language is nearly universal — "breathing in, breathing out", "stopping", "dwelling in the present" — few Pali / Sanskrit terms, not demanding listeners be Buddhists. Exactly because of this accessibility, Western secular mindfulness programmes are directly influenced by Plum Village's presentation — the Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) initiator once learned and received distinct influence from Master Thích Nhất Hạnh (article M.A1).
  • On goals: unlike purely secular mindfulness, Plum Village's goal does not stop at stress reduction but is transformation — seeing suffering's roots in one's own mind and way of life, then changing both. This distinction is exactly the Buddhist-vs-secular-meditation distinction presented in article M.A1, appearing here concretely.

For Vietnamese, one interesting detail: Plum Village's "practising amid life" spirit meets Trúc Lâm's "dwelling in the world, content with the Way" spirit (article M.F1.2) — two differently formed presentations (one side Vietnamese-founded in France, one side from the Trần era) but the same axis: meditation is not for leaving life. The greatest difference lies in "tool genres": Trúc Lâm uses the "knowing delusion, not following it" loop directed into the mind (article M.F1.3), Plum Village uses bells, verses, slow eating, and deep listening — "anchors" joined to everyday deeds.

5. Summary and next steps

  • Plum Village is the community co-founded by Master Thích Nhất Hạnh; characterized by applied mindfulness — meditation as a way of life, not a separate task in one hour.
  • Four distinctive forms: slow walking meditation, eating meditation, mindfulness bells, verses — all "packaging" basis skills (stopping — breathing — returning) into easily remembered forms, usable even for children.
  • Practice spreads into relationships: loving speech, deep listening, healing — the hardest and also most distinctive part of this presentation.
  • Position: a Buddhist basis, universal presentation, transformative goal — the bridge between Buddhism and secular mindfulness, and among the influences on modern mindfulness programmes.

Glossary:

  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
  • Plum Village: the practice community co-founded by Master Thích Nhất Hạnh in 1982 in France; named for the Dordogne plum village.
  • Zen masters: honoured meditation teachers; in Vietnam broadly used for reputable meditation-guiding monastics.
  • Engaged Buddhism: the viewpoint initiated by Master Thích Nhất Hạnh from the 1960s: meditation practice joined to deeds reducing society's suffering.
  • Línjì lineage (Chinese: Línjì, Japanese: Rinzai): the East Asian Zen lineage originating with master Línjì Yìxuán (China); the most widespread lineage in current Vietnamese Buddhism.
  • Interbeing: Master Thích Nhất Hạnh's term — all things co-existing through one another, nothing standing separately.
  • Verses (Sanskrit: gāthā): short verses, usually a few lines, for quiet recitation joined to specific activities.
  • Mindfulness bells: bells (or other sounds) used as signals to stop and breathe mindfully.
  • Walking meditation (Pali: caṅkama): walking meditation — practising mindfulness while walking slowly, on par with sitting meditation.
  • Five Mindfulness Trainings: the modern presentation of the five precepts, describing positive ways of life rather than prohibition lists.
  • Five precepts (Pali: pañca-sīla): the lay ethical basis: not killing, not taking what is not given, no sexual misconduct, not lying, not using intoxicants.
  • Secular mindfulness: mindfulness programmes presented independently of religion, aiming at health and daily life.
  • Transformation: in Plum Village language — recognizing and radically changing suffering in mind, not only outwardly soothing.
