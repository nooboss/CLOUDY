M.F1.2. Trúc Lâm Yên Tử and the Spirit of "Dwelling in the World, Content with the Way"

The previous article sketched the map: among the three early Zen lineages, the lineage still alive today is Trúc Lâm Zen — the first and only Zen lineage founded by Vietnamese themselves, formed in the late 13th century. This article enters two parts of the story: context and persons (the Trần era, King Trần Nhân Tông's role), and the practice spirit — prominent through four words "Dwelling in the world, content with the Way": living amid the dusty world yet at ease with the Way.

1. The Trần-era context — soil for a Zen lineage to sprout

In the second half of the 13th century, Đại Việt under the Trần dynasty was a firm political – cultural foundation: the court had just led victories against three Mongol – Yuan invasions (1258, 1285, 1288), while actively developing culture, including Buddhism. Zen lineages from the 6th century had merged into one another after many transmission generations; the monastic class had highly educated levels; kings and royalty were friendly with the teaching. Such a basis conditioned reorganizing old Zen lineages into a new school with its own style — what happened around 1299.

What is worth noting linguistically: from here people speak of the Trúc Lâm Zen school (Chinese: Zhúlín) — while previous articles used "Zen" to point to the shared East Asian tradition, "Trúc Lâm" is the proper name of one school on Vietnamese soil, with its own founding patriarch, successors, and texts. The name "Trúc Lâm" (bamboo grove) evokes quiet practice places — taken from the founder's religious title.

2. Trần Nhân Tông — from sovereign to patriarch

The founder is King Trần Nhân Tông (1258–1308), the Trần dynasty's third king: ascending in 1279, abdicating in 1293. Records note him as the supreme commander of two victories against Mongol – Yuan armies (1285, 1288) — together with generals like Trần Hưng Đạo. What is distinctive: after becoming Retired Emperor, he did not choose the ordinary retirement road, but ascended Yên Tử Mountain (today's northeast region) to practise meditation, then reorganized existing Zen lineages into one unified school — bearing the religious name Trúc Lâm Đầu Đà, the title Điều Ngự, the honour Giác Hoàng.

Trúc Lâm later had a distinct transmission chain: Trần Nhân Tông transmitted to Pháp Loa (1284–1330), then to Huyền Quang (1254–1334). These three are later honoured as the Three Trúc Lâm Patriarchs. At its strongest, the school had extensive temple and monastic systems, wide influence in both palace and populace.

Why does this story matter for today's meditation learners? Not for historical pride, but for the lesson on practice conditions: one who once bore mid-life heavy responsibilities — with a king's genuine pressures — came to meditation as a genuine need, not decorative need. That very "paradox" — kings practising, generals practising, scholars practising — is the material making the school's spirit: meditation is not for escaping life.

3. The "Dwelling in the world, content with the Way" spirit

These four words come from Trần Nhân Tông's most famous work — the Cư Trần Lạc Đạo Phú (prose-poem form; name meaning: "prose-poem living amid the dusty world, at ease with the Way"). The often-cited opening lines: "Dwelling in the world, content with the Way, just follow conditions / The profound mechanism, leave it whether others add or subtract" — roughly: living amid the dusty world yet joyful with the Way, simply following conditions; the Way's profound mechanism, leaving whether people discuss more or less. [Source: Cư Trần Lạc Đạo Phú, Trần Nhân Tông - Trúc Lâm Zen school, Vietnamese Buddhism.]

Within such short lines already lies a whole practice direction:

  • "Dwelling in the world" — amid the dusty world: the starting point is present life, with family, work, and social responsibilities — not some other empty place. This most distinctly differs from the "leaving the world to practise" image.
  • "Content with the Way" — at ease with the Way: practice is not a heavily austere life; amid ordinary routines genuine practitioner ease can still exist.
  • "Just follow conditions" — simply following conditions: a comfortable, unbraced spirit: one's available conditions are the standing place for practice, not obstacles awaiting removal.

Placed beside learned articles, this spirit matches a whole series of contents: meditation is mind training in daily life — daily life is part of practice; the unforced, uncoerced practice attitude; and the ethics – concentration – wisdom basis lay practitioners can entirely walk. Differing in that: here that spirit is spoken in Vietnamese, through one particular school, becoming an era's living slogan.

In other words: "Dwelling in the world, content with the Way" is the Vietnamese experiential answer to "can lay practitioners practise?". The meeting between these two languages (canonical frames and indigenous spirit) is exactly what keeps Trúc Lâm still vital for Vietnamese today.

4. Two texts to know — Khóa Hư Lục and Tuệ Trung Thượng Sĩ's records

Beyond the Cư Trần Lạc Đạo Phú, the taxonomy mentions two contemporary texts — today's learners need not read at once, but should know names to avoid losing when meeting materials:

  • Khóa Hư Lục (Chinese: Kè xū lù — Lessons on Emptiness) — by King Trần Thái Tông (1218–1277, the Trần dynasty's second king). Regarded as among Vietnam's earliest surviving Chinese-language Zen works: the king recording self-learning processes and realized points on impermanence, on the "empty" character — emptiness, non-clinging. The style torn between governing and practising — another witness to the "dwelling in the world" spirit. [Source: Khóa Hư Lục, Trần Thái Tông - Trúc Lâm Zen school, Vietnamese Buddhism.]
  • Tuệ Trung Thượng Sĩ's recorded sayings (recorded sayings: recorded words; Superior Man: the noble lay title) — recording dialogues of Tuệ Trung Thượng Sĩ (Trần Tung, 1230–1291), Trần Nhân Tông's elder brother: a lay practitioner, recognized by contemporary masters as profoundly levelled. These records were printed by Trần Nhân Tông himself after the Superior Man's passing. Dialogues therein show Zen's characteristic direct questioning style — twisting questions forcing learners to see directly, no lengthy explanations. [Source: Tuệ Trung Thượng Sĩ's recorded sayings, printed by Trần Nhân Tông in 1299 - Trúc Lâm Zen school, Vietnamese Buddhism.]

One right reading point: these three texts are Trúc Lâm's literary – recorded-sayings floor, not technical handbooks. Like reading the Thiền Uyển Tập Anh in the previous article, they show spirit and the path's direction more than listing each training step. The modern Trúc Lâm lineage's practice-presentation part — the familiar formula "knowing delusion, not following it" — is the next article's content.

5. Summary and next steps

  • The Trúc Lâm Zen school formed around 1299 on existing Zen lineages' basis: founded by Vietnamese, with its own style, transmission, and texts — what the three early lineages lack.
  • Trần Nhân Tông — the king defeating invading armies then ascending Yên Tử to practise meditation — is the founder; Pháp Loa and Huyền Quang succeeding, honoured as the Three Patriarchs.
  • The "Dwelling in the world, content with the Way" spirit: amid the dusty world, at ease with the Way, following conditions — meditation is not for escaping life; this is the indigenous answer to lay practice.
  • Three basis texts: the Cư Trần Lạc Đạo Phú, Khóa Hư Lục, Tuệ Trung Thượng Sĩ's recorded sayings — the literary – recorded-sayings floor, read for spirit, not technical handbooks.

Glossary:

  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
  • Trúc Lâm Zen school (Chinese: Zhúlín): the Zen lineage founded by Trần Nhân Tông around 1299, Vietnam's first Vietnamese-founded Zen lineage, named for the Trúc Lâm Đầu Đà religious title.
  • Yên Tử Mountain: the mountain in Vietnam's northeast region, where Trần Nhân Tông practised meditation and opened the Trúc Lâm Zen school; today the lineage's pilgrimage centre.
  • Patriarch / master: a school's founding patriarch, opening or holding dharma lineages.
  • Three patriarchs: three successive patriarchs holding one school's teaching; in Trúc Lâm comprising Trần Nhân Tông, Pháp Loa, and Huyền Quang.
  • Dharma lineage: teacher-student transmission lines, used to determine a Zen lineage's origins and legitimacy.
  • Prose-poem (rhapsody): the long rhymed literary form of ancient East Asian literature, rhyming by passages.
  • Profound mechanism: the Sino-Vietnamese words pointing to the Way's profound, subtle mechanism; in the Cư Trần Lạc Đạo Phú's opening lines.
  • Records (Chinese: lù): records; used in book names recording teachings — for example Khóa Hư Lục, recorded sayings.
  • Recorded sayings (Chinese: yǔlù): the book form recording one master's dialogues and teachings, widespread in East Asian Zen.
  • Superior Man: the honorific of Tuệ Trung Thượng Sĩ (Trần Tung), Trần Nhân Tông's elder brother — a lay practitioner recognized as levelled.
  • Lay Buddhists (Sanskrit: upāsaka / upāsikā): Buddhists living lay lives, unordained.
  • Wandering thoughts (Chinese: wàngxiǎng): drifting thoughts automatically arising in mind, the "knowing delusion, not following it" formula's object (article M.F1.3).
  • According with conditions (Chinese: suíyuán): the attitude adapting to available circumstantial conditions, neither opposing nor clinging.
