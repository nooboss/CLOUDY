M.G1.8. Guided Audio, Bells, and Apps — Benefits and Limits

Nearly all beginners start with one kind of sound: guided voices on apps, timer bells, relaxing guidance recordings. This tool is genuinely useful — and this article frankly presents both sides exactly its focus: when to use voiced guidance, when to sit in silence, how to reasonably use bells and timers, and dependence risks on guiding sounds.

1. Four kinds of sounds — four different tasks

First, distinctly distinguishing — because each kind serves one task, confusing will misuse:

  • Content guidance (step-by-step guidance recordings: "releasing shoulders...", "bringing attention to breath..."): substitute teachers — used when not yet knowing how.
  • Session opening / closing bells: boundary signals — used to begin and end sittings.
  • Periodic mid-session bells (every few minutes one sound): "mindfulness markers" — used to check whether still with subjects or already astray.
  • Background music / environmental sounds (rain, waves, monastery bell sounds): covering noise and creating atmospheres — not practice articles; hearing background music calling it meditation resembles opening gyms without training anything.

The shared choosing principle: asking "right now I need sounds for what task?" — not yet knowing how → guidance; already knowing how → only bells needed; noisy places → background music covering noise (exactly the sound-subject essence already presented in article M.C1.2).

2. When to use guidance — when to sit in silence

This is the most practical question. Divided by phases and purposes:

  • Guidance should be used when: newly beginning (learning postures, learning how to return to breath); learning new practices (body scans, releasing — first guided readings will be more accurate than self-guessing); when minds are too chaotic to self-organize (outer guiding voices are ready anchors); and when needing warm voices for easier releasing (many hard sleepers using short guidance — acceptable).
  • Silence should be sat in when: known articles wanting depth — because guidance occupies attention bandwidth: while hearing and understanding guidance, part of minds is always busy hearing; silently sitting, entire attention is for subjects themselves themselves. Hence silent retreats almost never use mid-session guidance; also why many feel "sitting with apps" and "sitting alone" distinctly differ though the same articles.
  • The natural transition formula: using guidance until articles are memorized → choosing "gradually sparse guidance" versions (only reminding at starts, middles, ends) → only start-end bells. These three steps usually take weeks; each goes at their own rhythm — no need to hurry.

One easily remembered sentence pair: guidance is crutches when learning to walk; crutches kept forever are no longer help. This sentence is also section 4's spirit.

3. Bells and timers — reasonable use

Bells are the cheapest and least risky tools — rightly used very durable:

  • Session start – end bells: setting timers with soft ending sounds (avoiding default phone bells — they report "work done" not "returning to the present"). The silence at bell sounds' ends (awaiting sounds completely ending) is itself one hearing article — immediately usable.
  • Periodic mid-session bells: placing bells after 5–10 minutes; each hearing, quickly checking: still with subjects? — then returning, no blaming oneself. This is the automatic version of "mindfulness markers" already trained in article M.B6; not too densely using (each minute once loses self-checking's very essence).
  • Outside-session bells: random daily bells (some apps have this feature) or self-choosing fixed markers (after each standing up, before each mail opening) — the same spirit as busy persons' "anchors" and Plum Village bells.
  • On choosing apps: practical criteria — soft bell sounds, flexible timers, quality guidance articles, and few mandatory installations (switching off social notifications, no auto-opening when machines boot). Apps are toolboxes, not playgrounds: fewer features better for practice purposes.

4. Dependence risks — and gradual transitioning

This article's most important part. Dependence on guiding sounds is another form of clinging onto outer help — detected through signs:

  • Four dependence signs: (1) without apps unable to sit — feeling "missing something" when silently sitting; (2) awaiting guidance as awaiting someone doing instead — minds passively following reading voices instead of self-doing articles; (3) unable to know passing time — entirely no awareness of sessions' rhythms without bell sounds; (4) regarding guiding voices as experiences — sitting to enjoy hearing, no longer for practising. Point (2) matters most: practice articles are tasks one does with one's own mind; guiding voices only describe those tasks — hearing descriptions is not doing.
  • Why dependence is a genuine risk: practice needs usability where no apps exist — in buses, amid arguments, midnight waking moments. Minds familiar with "needing someone guiding" will always await ideal conditions — while life is unideal. Additionally, guiding voices forever keep persons at beginning levels: memorized articles yet sitting with guidance resembles knowing counting yet clinging onto calculators.
  • Gradual transition plans (reference): each week reducing one sound part — first week: full guidance; second week: guidance only at starts and ends; third week: only start-end bells; fourth week: trying one completely silent session (possibly silently timing, vibrating modes). Into week four, sessions will be shorter than usual and feel "driftier" — normal business: the first time minds self-do everything; a few later sessions will lighten. Long-term persons may use two parallel modes: daily silent sessions, sparse guidance for new articles.
  • What needs no transitioning: session start – end bells and outside-hours markers are not dependence — they are boundary signals, like alarm clocks: no one "depends" on alarms to sleep. The part needing transition is only mid-session guidance.

5. Summary and next steps

  • Four kinds of sounds doing four different tasks: guidance (teaching), opening / closing bells (boundaries), periodic bells (checking), background music (covering noise — not exercises).
  • Using guidance when not yet knowing how or minds too chaotic; silently sitting when already knowing and wanting depth — because guidance occupies attention bandwidth.
  • Rightly using bells: soft ending sounds, sparse periodicity (5–10 minutes), outside-hours markers; choosing apps by "fewer features better than many features" criteria.
  • Dependence risks have four signs; gradually transitioning through three steps (full guidance → sparse guidance → only bells); what needs transitioning is mid-session guidance — boundary bells need no abandoning.

Glossary:

  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
  • Attention bandwidth: a metaphor — attention amounts for one task at once at once; hearing guidance costs part of these amounts.
  • Anchors: fixed objects used to hold attention returning — outer guiding voices are one ready form.
  • Silent retreats: retreats keeping "noble silence" — limiting speech and no phone use.
  • Sound as meditation subject: using sounds present as practice objects — hearing unclassifying, unseeking sources.
  • Mindfulness cues: pre-defined signals used to remind stopping and returning to the present in long days.
