M.G1.4. Short Practices, 1–5 Minutes

So far, group G1 has traversed three daily-life contexts (eating, working, emotions); this article gathers into a handheld toolkit: extremely short practices, one to five minutes long, ordered by goals — because in real life, people do not choose articles by names but by immediate needs: needing minds regathered, needing alertness, needing releasing, needing pauses regained before reacting. All use materials already trained in groups B and G1 — no new techniques to learn from scratch.

1. Short practices — how to use rightly?

Before entering articles, three use rules for all sub-five-minute practices:

  • They do not replace principal sittings. Short practices are on-the-spot relief toolkits — 10–20 minute daily sittings remain the principal "training" part; short practices are the "genuine use" part. Those only using short tools abandoning sittings will see skills stall — tools and training sessions need both.
  • Small enough to unfail. One known breath is already practice. This rule matters more than article lists: the busiest days still have room for one breath — and keeping even threads is more precious than everything.
  • Aiming at goals, not numbers. Choosing articles by current needs (scattered minds → steadying articles; tired → state-shifting articles...), not by "today must do X articles" feelings. The below list is a medicine cabinet, not a timetable.

2. Goal group: steadying attention

Used when minds scatter, before focus-needing work, or as "session openers" for time intervals. All three below take breath as anchors (the attention-holding-point technique of article M.B2):

  • Three complete breaths (≈ 30 seconds): the abridged version of the whole system: breathing in — knowing breathing in; breathing out — knowing breathing out; three times. Usable everywhere: before meetings, before entering homes, before messaging important texts. So short yet fully doing three tasks: stopping, knowing, returning.
  • Counting ten breaths (≈ 2 minutes): counting each breath one to ten then repeating; going astray — returning to one, no blaming oneself (exactly the "recognize — release gently — return" loop, as in article M.B3). This is the "pocket version" of breath-counting articles: enough to sense today's mind rhythms — today's scattered or relatively settled.
  • Five-minute short sitting (≈ 5 minutes): with calm seats available — the abridged version of genuine meditation sittings: postures, natural breath, when distracted returning. Busy persons may use this version replacing long sittings on special days — provided it does not become routine.

And one "hearing" article — usable even when eyes must view other work: hearing sounds one minute (≈ 1 minute) — stopping, opening ears: recognizing sounds present, unclassifying "noisy – un-noisy", unseeking sources; only hearing until one minute ends. Sounds are handy subjects in noisy places because they arrive by themselves — no need to actively direct minds away (the sound-subject characteristics already presented in article M.C1.2).

3. Goal group: recognizing breath, releasing, shifting states

Three small groups most used in real life:

  • Recognizing breath (state-checking): placing one hand on bellies, one breath — asking: today how is my breath? Fast shallow chest breaths usually go with tension; slow deep belly breaths go with settled states. This is a quick-measure article — three seconds showing where one's mind is (joining article M.B1 and bodily-signal-reading skills in article M.G1.2).
  • Releasing (unwinding bodily tension): the extended out-breath article: breathing in counting three, breathing out counting six — this lệch rhythm activates the body's natural relaxing responses; doing six times. With: quickly scanning one round — shoulders, jaws, hands, bellies — wherever braced "releasing one beat" when breathing out (the abridged version of group B's body-releasing article and group G2's scanning skills). Releasing is most useful before tension peaks — once rigidly braced, longer time is needed.
  • Shifting states (when tired, bored, sluggish): two options by places: (a) standing up moving with knowing — fetching water, walking one round: each step knowing steps, each movement knowing movements — shifting subjects from breath to bodies in movement; (b) changing environments one beat — opening windows, stepping to balconies, viewing farther than day-long viewed points: both eyes and minds "changing pictures" together. Shifting states is not fighting fatigue — giving minds new materials to self-rebalance.

4. Goal group: regaining pauses

The group for the hottest moments — before reacting (the central skill of article M.G1.3). Three articles ordered by speed:

  • One breath before answering (seconds): when harsh sentences just reach ears, when wanting to answer "let me think" also fine — the sole goal is not letting automatic reactions run at once. One breath is the boundary between "exploding along" and "choosing words".
  • Stopping – breathing – asking (≈ 30 seconds): stopping what one does; three breaths; one question: "what is genuinely important right now?" — this question gathers all noise to one point, exactly group B's subject-gathering way.
  • Naming then choosing (≈ 1 minute): silently labelling what is happening ("irritated", "hurried"), then actively choosing next steps — including choosing "doing nothing yet". The "naming" skill is the noting technique; when adding "choosing" steps, it becomes decision-making tools in emotional storms.

This toolkit touches its usefulness ceiling there: it regains pauses, not replacing root handling — things needing dialogue, fixing, and time still must be done their own right ways.

5. Summary and next steps

  • Short practices are goal-based toolkits, not replacing principal sittings; small enough to unfail; choosing by needed business, not quantities.
  • Five goal groups: steadying attention (three breaths, counting ten breaths, five-minute sittings, hearing sounds), state-checking (hands on bellies — three-second quick measures), releasing (extended out-breaths + quick scans), shifting states (knowing movement, changing environments), regaining pauses (one breath before answering, stopping – breathing – asking, naming then choosing).
  • All articles are abridged versions of learned articles — hence when conditions allow, returning to train full versions in group B.

Suggested next reading: still in the everyday toolkit, one article is reserved for modern persons' hardest circumstances: practice for the very busy — when no "separate time intervals" exist to spare for meditation, and all skills must live in the smallest gaps. The next article will present.

Glossary:

  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
  • Mind anchors: fixed points used to hold attention returning — in short articles usually breath or sounds.
  • Meditation subject (Pali: kammaṭṭhāna): chosen objects for placing minds onto when practising.
  • Switching subjects: changing practice objects from one to another (from breath to bodies in movement) — intentionally done, a skill; spontaneously done, distraction.
  • Labels (mental labels): silent names placed on happening phenomena — exactly the noting technique.
  • Mindfulness (Pali: sati): remembering and staying with what is happening right now — the basis capacity of all articles in the collection.
