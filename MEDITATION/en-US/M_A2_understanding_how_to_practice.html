M.A2. Understanding How to Practice

Once you have grasped "What Meditation Is", this article prepares three essentials before you begin practising: correctly understanding the two core elements of every meditation method — mindfulness and clear comprehension, knowing how to read meditation terminology across the three language layers of Pali – Sanskrit – Sino-Vietnamese, and avoiding the common misconceptions that make many beginners give up at the very start. 1. Mindfulness and clear comprehension — the two core elements

In the discourses, these two terms always appear together, yet they are not the same thing. Distinguishing them is the key to not reducing "mindfulness" to mere "attention".

1.1. Mindfulness (sati) — remembering and staying with

Mindfulness (Pali: sati, Sanskrit: smṛti) originally means remembering: the mind stays with the chosen object right in the present — the breath coming in and out, sensations in the body, feelings as they rise — without slipping away.

The point to grasp: sati is not simply "attention". Attention is a general capacity of every mind; a hunting predator also has sharp attention. Mindfulness in Buddhism has two further distinctive marks:

  • Directed to the present — the object of mindfulness is what is happening right now, not stories of the past or future.
  • Accompanied by non-greed and non-aversion — observing without seizing what is pleasant or pushing away what is unpleasant.

The discourses define mindfulness within the Noble Eightfold Path (Pali: ariya aṭṭhaṅgika magga) as staying with four domains: body, feelings, mind, and dhammas — that is, the four establishments of mindfulness (Pali: satipaṭṭhāna). [Source: Mahācattārīsaka Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 117 - Theravāda Buddhism.] This four-domain framework is presented in full in article group M.D1.

1.2. Clear comprehension (sampajañña) — understanding context and purpose

Clear comprehension (Pali: sampajañña, Sanskrit: saṃprajanya) is the clear understanding that accompanies mindfulness: understanding the context, understanding the purpose, and knowing whether the present action is appropriate.

The Theravāda commentaries divide clear comprehension into four aspects:

  • Understanding the purpose — why am I doing this?
  • Understanding suitability — is this appropriate right now?
  • Understanding the domain — is the mind staying within the field of practice or has it drifted outside?
  • Freedom from confusion — not acting like someone bewildered or deluded.

[Source: the four kinds of clear comprehension — Visuddhimagga, chapter XIV - the commentarial level, Theravāda Buddhism.]

1.3. An example distinguishing the two

Suppose you are sitting in meditation and your hand reaches for the phone:

  • Mindfulness alone: "the hand is holding the phone" — the action is clearly known, but the "why" has not yet been asked.
  • Mindfulness with clear comprehension: "the hand is holding the phone — out of inadvertent habit; this is practice time, and the phone is not appropriate now" — and the hand puts the phone down by itself.

In short: mindfulness clearly knows what is happening; clear comprehension clearly understands what it means and whether to continue. Mindfulness answers "what?", clear comprehension answers "why, and is it suitable?". The discourses describe the practitioner with exactly this pair: ātāpī sampajāno satimā — ardent, clearly comprehending, mindful — three qualities joined to every act of observing body, feelings, mind, and dhammas. [Source: Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya, MN 10 - Theravāda Buddhism.] In practice, the two interweave continuously: sati keeps the mind from drifting, sampajañña keeps it from acting blindly. Article group M.B will show you how to train both step by step.

2. Reading meditation terminology: Pali, Sanskrit, and Sino-Vietnamese

When reading meditation literature, you will meet the same concept in three different written forms. Take the familiar framework of the establishments of mindfulness:

  • Pali: satipaṭṭhāna — the language of the Southern canon.
  • Sanskrit: smṛtyupasthāna — the form used in most Mahāyāna Buddhist (Sanskrit: mahāyāna) and Vajrayāna Buddhist (Sanskrit: vajrayāna) scriptures.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: tứ niệm xứ — the form most familiar to Vietnamese readers, through scriptures translated into Chinese characters.

All three point to the same thing — like "cà phê", "coffee", and "咖啡" pointing to the same drink. Recognizing this lets you read books from any tradition without confusion.

2.1. Three language layers — three origins

  • Pali is the language recording the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism — so in articles on tranquillity, insight, and the establishments of mindfulness, you will meet Pali words most often.
  • Sanskrit is the language of the Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna scriptures — so articles in tradition groups M.E2 and M.E3 will use Sanskrit forms (for example śamatha/vipaśyanā instead of samatha/vipassanā).
  • Sino-Vietnamese is the Vietnamese reading of Chinese characters — most everyday familiar terms (sitting meditation, buddha-recitation, investigating the kōan, quiet sitting) come through this route.

2.2. How to read the diacritics

Pali and Sanskrit words in the app carry diacritics. You do not need native-like pronunciation — the following simple conventions are enough:

  • Macrons (ā, ī, ū): long vowels — sati reads "sa-ti", samādhi reads "sa-maa-dhi" (with a longer a).
  • ṃ (dot below): a light nasal ending — saṃsāra reads close to "sang-saa-ra".
  • ṅ, ñ: read like "ng" and "ny" — paṭṭhāna close to "pat-thaa-na", paññā close to "pan-nyaa".
  • ṭ, ḍ, ṇ, ḷ (dot below): retroflex consonants — for Vietnamese speakers, reading them like ordinary t, d, n, l is enough.
  • ś, ṣ: read close to a soft "s" — śamatha reads "sha-ma-tha".

2.3. How terms are displayed in the app

Throughout the app's articles, a Pali / Sanskrit / Sino-Vietnamese term is presented as: the English term first, with the source form in parentheses after it — for example: mindfulness (Pali: sati). An explanation appears only the first time a term is used in each article; later occurrences use the plain English term so the prose stays light.

3. Common misconceptions about meditation

The six misconceptions below are why many beginners grow discouraged or go astray in the first weeks. Check whether you are carrying any of them.

3.1. "Meditation means making the mind blank"

In fact: a thinking mind is its normal state, as eyes have sights and ears have sounds. The exercise is not to forbid thinking, but to recognize "I am thinking" and gently return to the object — each recognition and return is one complete "repetition" of the exercise. Expecting "a blank mind from the very first sitting" is like expecting someone who has never trained to lift heavy weights on day one at the gym.

3.2. "You must see lights or strange visions for it to count as right meditation"

In fact: lights, visions, and feelings of elation can appear while developing collectedness — the commentaries call them the meditation sign (Pali: nimitta) — but they are side effects, not a measure of right and wrong, and most long-term practitioners do not rely on them (article M.C1.3 explains in detail). Using "strange feelings" as the goal leads to two failures: either trying to fabricate them (losing focus), or becoming anxious because "I don't see anything" (losing confidence).

3.3. "Thoughts arising means failure"

In fact: in a 20-minute sitting, a beginner may return to the object dozens of times — that means dozens of repetitions of practice, not dozens of "failures". The traditions therefore do not demand that beginners "must not think" — they only teach how to return more quickly and more gently.

3.4. "Meditation is a way of escaping problems"

In fact: meditation does not erase problems; it changes how we stand before them. When the mind is calm and clear, you often see more clearly even what you previously avoided — and seeing clearly is the first step to genuinely resolving it. Of course, meditation does not replace practical work (paying debts, treating illness, mending relationships), and in some phases when emotions surge strongly the practice needs adjustment — article M.A3 discusses this in depth.

3.5. "The longer you sit, the better you are"

In fact: the reliable measure is quality and regularity, not minutes. Sitting 10–15 minutes every day carries you further than sitting 90 minutes once every few weeks, because meditation skill grows by gradual familiarization like learning music, not by brute force. Conversely, forcing overlong sittings before you are ready easily breeds aversion to practice — the most common cause of giving up. Article group M.B suggests a gradual progression; article M.A4 shows how to choose a starting duration.

3.6. "Meditation is guaranteed to remove stress"

In fact: some stress-reduction benefits have scientific evidence, and many people feel them early. But meditation is not a guaranteed medicine: there are phases (especially when old emotions surface) when practice feels uncomfortable, and for some people with particular mental-health conditions, forcing high-intensity practice can backfire. That is why the very next article after this one is on safety — designing the path in the right sequence is precisely how to avoid falling where you least expect it.

4. Summary and next steps

  • Mindfulness (sati) is remembering and staying with what is happening; clear comprehension (sampajañña) is understanding the purpose and suitability. These two elements are the core of every meditation method, however different the technique names may be.
  • The same concept may carry three names: Pali, Sanskrit, and Sino-Vietnamese — three names, one reality. Knowing the app's display convention and how to read diacritics is enough to read every article without confusion.
  • The six common misconceptions (blank mind, chasing visions, treating thought as failure, meditation as escape, longer sitting as better, meditation as guaranteed stress relief) all have clear answers — avoiding them means you have already passed the narrowest gate for beginners.

Suggested next reading: the next step in the app's suggested sequence is Safety in Meditation (article M.A3) — which reactions you may meet, and when to adjust or stop. Once safety is understood, move on to preparing for an actual sitting (article M.A4).

Glossary:

  • Nikāya (Pali: Nikāya): the names of the major collections of discourses in Theravāda Buddhism, compiled by theme or length.
  • Mindfulness (Pali: sati): originally "remembering, keeping in mind" — holding the object before the mind; not the same as "thinking" or "thinking about".
  • Clear comprehension (Pali: sampajañña): clearly understanding the context, purpose, and suitability of what one is doing, accompanying mindfulness.
  • Commentarial level: treatises and explanatory works compiled after the canonical basket in order to systematize doctrine and practice; an example in Theravāda Buddhism is the Visuddhimagga.
  • Pali: the language in which the scriptures of Theravāda Buddhism are preserved.
  • Sanskrit: the language of much of the Mahāyāna Buddhist and Vajrayāna Buddhist scriptural literature.
  • Sino-Vietnamese: the Vietnamese pronunciation of Chinese characters; most meditation terms entered Vietnamese through scriptures translated into Chinese (the Chinese Canon).
